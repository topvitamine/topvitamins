Introduction

Vitamins are organic micronutrients required in small amounts for many physiological processes. Deficits in specific vitamins produce characteristic clinical signs because each vitamin has distinct biochemical roles. This article summarizes common deficiency patterns, risk groups, and practical diagnostic considerations.

Key vitamin deficiencies and typical symptoms

- Vitamin D: Deficiency commonly presents with bone pain, proximal muscle weakness, fatigue, and increased susceptibility to respiratory infections. Long-term insufficiency impairs calcium homeostasis and can contribute to osteomalacia or osteoporosis, particularly in older adults and people with limited sun exposure.

- Vitamin B12 (cobalamin): Low B12 may cause fatigue, pallor from megaloblastic anemia, paresthesia (tingling) in hands and feet, gait instability, memory impairment, and mood changes. Risk factors include strict vegetarian diets, pernicious anemia, gastrointestinal malabsorption, and some medications.

- Folate (vitamin B9): Folate deficiency produces megaloblastic anemia, fatigue, irritability, and in pregnancy increases the risk of neural tube defects. Causes include inadequate dietary intake, certain anticonvulsant drugs, alcohol use, and malabsorption.

- Vitamin A: Insufficiency manifests as night blindness, xerosis (dry skin and conjunctiva), increased infection risk, and in severe cases xerophthalmia. Populations at risk include those with fat malabsorption and limited dietary carotenoid intake.

- Vitamin C: Low vitamin C leads to impaired collagen synthesis, resulting in bruising, bleeding gums, poor wound healing, joint pain, and in advanced cases scurvy. Suboptimal intake can also correlate with increased infection frequency.

- Vitamin E: Deficiency is uncommon but can cause peripheral neuropathy, muscle weakness, and ataxia in individuals with fat malabsorption or genetic transport defects.

- Vitamin K: Deficit presents with easy bruising, mucosal bleeding, and prolonged clotting times. Newborns, those with cholestatic liver disease, or prolonged antibiotic exposure are at higher risk.

Other micronutrients such as biotin (B7), pantothenic acid (B5), and pyridoxine (B6) can produce hair and skin changes, burning sensations in the feet, mood changes, and anemia when deficient. Clinical patterns often overlap and require targeted testing.

Diagnosis and assessment

Laboratory tests (serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D, serum B12, folate, complete blood count, prothrombin time for vitamin K, and specific assays where indicated) are essential for confirming deficiencies. Clinical context—dietary history, medication use, gastrointestinal disease, age, and pregnancy status—guides interpretation and follow-up testing.

Management principles

Addressing deficiency begins with correcting the underlying cause (e.g., improving dietary intake, optimizing absorption, or adjusting interacting medications). Replacement regimens should be individualized based on severity and patient factors. In many cases oral supplementation is effective; parenteral routes are used when absorption is impaired.
