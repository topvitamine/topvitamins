Vitamins are small organic compounds the body needs in minute amounts to maintain essential physiological functions. Humans require 13 recognized vitamins: four fat‑soluble (A, D, E, K) and nine water‑soluble (vitamin C and the eight B vitamins). Understanding their classification, primary roles, food sources, and deficiency signs helps guide dietary choices and appropriate supplementation.

Introduction

The 13 essential vitamins function as cofactors, antioxidants, structural components, and signaling molecules. Fat‑soluble vitamins are stored in adipose tissue and the liver and thus have different dosing considerations than water‑soluble vitamins, which are not stored extensively and require more frequent intake.

Classification and primary roles

- Fat‑soluble: Vitamin A (vision, epithelial health), Vitamin D (calcium homeostasis, bone health), Vitamin E (lipid antioxidant), Vitamin K (coagulation, bone metabolism).
- Water‑soluble: Vitamin C (collagen synthesis, immune support, iron absorption) and the B complex: B1 (thiamine), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin), B5 (pantothenic acid), B6 (pyridoxine), B7 (biotin), B9 (folate), B12 (cobalamin). B vitamins largely support energy metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, and red blood cell formation.

Dietary sources and bioavailability

A diverse diet typically supplies most vitamins. Examples:
- Vitamin A: liver, orange and dark green vegetables
- Vitamin D: fatty fish, fortified foods, and cutaneous synthesis via UVB exposure
- Vitamin C: citrus, peppers, berries
- B vitamins: whole grains, legumes, dairy, meat, and leafy greens

Bioavailability varies by food matrix and nutrient form (e.g., vitamin D3 vs D2, methylcobalamin vs cyanocobalamin). Food preparation can reduce some water‑soluble vitamins; gentle cooking and minimal processing help preserve them.

Recognizing deficiencies

Deficiencies may develop due to inadequate intake, malabsorption, restricted diets, or increased physiological demand. Common presentations include:
- Vitamin A: night blindness, dry eyes
- Vitamin D: bone pain, muscle weakness
- Vitamin C: fatigue, poor wound healing, gum bleeding
- B12: anemia, neuropathy, cognitive changes

Diagnosis relies on clinical assessment and targeted laboratory testing. Treatment focuses on dietary correction and, when necessary, supplementation under clinical guidance.

Conclusion

Maintaining adequate vitamin status is central to metabolic health, immune competence, and developmental processes. Prioritize a varied diet, be aware of individual risk factors for deficiency, and use laboratory testing and professional advice when considering supplementation.
