Vitamin A is a fat‑soluble micronutrient with diverse roles across vision, immune function, skin integrity, cellular growth and antioxidant defense. Deficiency can cause night blindness, increased susceptibility to infection and impaired tissue repair, while appropriate intake supports physiological processes from fetal development to healthy aging. Forms and sources

Dietary vitamin A exists as preformed retinoids (retinol and its esters) from animal foods and as provitamin A carotenoids (notably beta‑carotene) in plants. Preformed vitamin A is readily used by the body, whereas provitamin A compounds are converted to active vitamin A dependent on need, reducing toxicity risk. Public health guidance such as EFSA intake ranges and upper limits inform safe supplementation strategies. Vision health

Vitamin A is essential for the visual cycle: retinal combines with opsin to form rhodopsin, enabling low‑light vision, and it maintains corneal and conjunctival epithelial health. Night blindness and xerophthalmia are classic deficiency outcomes. Population supplementation programs in regions with documented deficiency reduce rates of blindness and infection‑related mortality; in clinical practice, maintaining adequate vitamin A status is a preventive strategy for ocular resilience.

Immune support

Vitamin A contributes to both innate and adaptive immunity by preserving mucosal barriers and supporting differentiation and function of immune cells (T and B cells, macrophages, natural killer cells). It also modulates antibody production—especially secretory IgA—and inflammatory signaling, helping balance effective pathogen defense with controlled inflammation. Observational and interventional data link deficiency with higher infection rates; targeted supplementation in deficient settings lowers morbidity and mortality.

Skin, cell growth and repair

At the cellular level, vitamin A regulates gene expression relevant to cell differentiation and proliferation. Retinoids are well documented in dermatology for acne management, photodamage and epidermal repair via effects on keratinocyte turnover, sebum production and collagen synthesis. During growth, pregnancy and wound healing, vitamin A supports organogenesis and tissue regeneration, but dosing must avoid excess due to teratogenic and hepatotoxic potential of high preformed vitamin A intakes.

Antioxidant properties and balance

Carotenoid forms like beta‑carotene provide antioxidant protection, limiting lipid peroxidation and oxidative DNA damage. Combined antioxidant approaches (for example pairing carotenoids with vitamins C and E or omega‑3 fatty acids) can support cellular resilience, although supplementation should be informed by clinical context and safety thresholds.

Conclusion

Vitamin A is multifunctional: it underpins vision, modulates immune competence, supports skin health and guides cellular development. Safe, evidence‑based use—guided by dietary assessment, clinical status and public health recommendations—allows populations to gain benefits while minimizing toxicity risk.
