Quick answer summary:

- Vitamin K2 is increasingly recognized for its role in cardiovascular health, particularly in preventing arterial calcification.
- Many cardiologists acknowledge its potential benefits, though it is not yet a universal prescription in cardiology practice.
- MK-7 is the preferred supplemental form for daily use because of its longer half-life and steady plasma levels.
- Clinical evidence suggests K2 supports calcium balance and may reduce arterial stiffness when combined with vitamin D and magnesium.

## Introduction

Interest in vitamin K2 has grown as researchers examine nutritional strategies to support arterial health. This article summarizes current evidence on K2’s cardiovascular role, dosing considerations, and safety so you can discuss it knowledgeably with a healthcare provider. ## What K2 does and why it matters

Vitamin K2 (menaquinones) helps activate matrix Gla-protein (MGP) and osteocalcin. Activated MGP inhibits calcium deposition in arterial walls, reducing vascular calcification, while osteocalcin helps incorporate calcium into bone. Epidemiological studies, including large cohort analyses, have associated higher dietary K2 intake with lower risk of coronary heart disease and reduced arterial calcification. Randomized trials examining MK-7 indicate improvements in markers of arterial stiffness over months of supplementation, particularly when paired with vitamin D and magnesium.

## Forms and dosing

Two common supplemental forms are MK-4 and MK-7. MK-7 (often derived from fermented soy, natto) has a longer half-life and is typically dosed once daily; studies commonly use 100–180 mcg/day. MK-4 may require higher and more frequent dosing. Regulatory bodies like EFSA have not set an upper limit for K2 due to low toxicity in available data, but typical clinical recommendations remain conservative and individualized.

## Safety and drug interactions

K2 is generally well tolerated. The principal clinical concern is interaction with vitamin K antagonists (e.g., warfarin), where increased K intake can reduce anticoagulant effect. People on anticoagulant therapy should consult their prescribing clinician before starting K2 and have INR monitored if doses change. Those who are pregnant, breastfeeding, or have significant kidney disease should seek personalized medical advice.

## Practical considerations

- Absorption: K2 is fat-soluble; take with a meal that contains dietary fat.  
- Synergy: Combining K2 with vitamin D and magnesium supports calcium handling and vascular health.  
- Monitoring: For individuals at cardiovascular risk, clinicians may monitor calcium, vitamin D status, and imaging markers (e.g., CAC scores) over time.

## Conclusion

Evidence supports a physiologic mechanism by which K2 may protect arteries by directing calcium away from vessel walls and toward bone. Many cardiologists are monitoring the literature and may recommend K2 in select patients, especially when used alongside vitamin D and magnesium; however, individualized clinical judgment and consideration of medication interactions remain essential.
