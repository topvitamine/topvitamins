## Introduction

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of biochemical reactions that support energy production, nervous system function, muscle contraction and protein synthesis. As a cofactor for many enzymes, magnesium helps convert food into usable energy and contributes to normal physiological functions such as reduction of tiredness and fatigue, normal muscle function and normal psychological function.

## Key benefits

Magnesium’s nutritional roles include supporting energy-yielding metabolism, helping maintain electrolyte balance, and enabling normal nerve signal transmission and muscle relaxation. It also participates in protein synthesis and influences vitamin D metabolism, making it a useful component of overall bone-supportive nutrition. These roles are supported by nutrient function claims and are distinct from clinical treatment claims.

## Supplementation and forms

Common supplemental forms include magnesium citrate, magnesium glycinate (bisglycinate), magnesium oxide, magnesium malate and magnesium chloride. Organic salts such as citrate and glycinate are generally better absorbed and better tolerated than inorganic magnesium oxide, which provides more elemental magnesium per weight but typically has lower bioavailability. Glycinate is often chosen when minimizing gastrointestinal effects is a priority; citrate provides a cost-effective, well-absorbed option but may be laxative at higher doses.

Recommended intake varies by age, sex and life stage; labels list elemental magnesium so compare that value when choosing a product. Taking magnesium with meals and splitting the daily dose can improve absorption and reduce GI side effects. People on prescription medications (for example certain antibiotics, bisphosphonates or diuretics) should consult a healthcare professional because timing and interactions matter.

## Recognizing low intake

Signs associated with suboptimal magnesium status include muscle cramps, twitches, increased tiredness, sleep disruption and changes in concentration or mood. Certain groups have higher risk: older adults, people with malabsorptive gastrointestinal conditions, those with uncontrolled diabetes, heavy sweat losses (athletes) and people on long-term medications that affect magnesium balance. Serum magnesium tests are commonly used but can miss tissue-level depletion; clinical assessment considers diet, symptoms and risk factors together.

## Bones, immunity and safety

Magnesium contributes to normal bone structure and supports osteoblast and osteoclast activity as part of a network that includes calcium and vitamin D. Mechanistic and observational research links magnesium to bone mineralization, but nutrient interactions are complex. Magnesium is also involved in cellular processes relevant to immune cell function, though claims about preventing or treating infections are not supported.

Toxicity from food is rare in people with normal kidney function. Excessive supplemental magnesium can cause diarrhea, cramping and nausea; in cases of renal impairment, supplementation should be supervised by a clinician because of the risk of hypermagnesemia. Start with conservative doses, monitor tolerance, and prioritize food sources like leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes and whole grains.
