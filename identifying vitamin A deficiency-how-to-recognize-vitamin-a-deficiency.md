Vitamin A is essential for vision, immune function, epithelial integrity, and cellular differentiation. Mild shortages often begin with subtle signs before advancing to more severe tissue damage. This short guide summarizes common clinical features, assessment approaches, and prevention strategies grounded in current clinical practice.

Introduction

Vitamin A deficiency typically presents first with night blindness and progressive ocular changes. Early recognition can prevent long-term harm. For a fuller overview and context, see [How to Recognize Vitamin A Deficiency](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/how-to-recognize-vitamin-a-deficiency).

Common signs and symptoms

- Visual changes: Night blindness (nyctalopia) and reduced contrast or color discrimination are early markers because retinal photoreceptors depend on retinoids for phototransduction.
- Ocular surface disease: Xerosis, Bitot’s spots, conjunctival drying, and in severe cases keratomalacia or corneal ulcers reflect mucosal epithelial breakdown.
- Skin and mucosa: Hyperkeratosis, dry and rough skin, and mucosal fragility arise when epithelial differentiation is impaired.
- Increased infections: Impaired mucosal immunity contributes to more frequent or severe respiratory, gastrointestinal, or urinary infections.
- Growth and systemic effects: In children, deficiency can cause growth faltering, fatigue, and higher infectious morbidity; in pregnancy it may affect fetal development if not managed appropriately.

Diagnosis and evaluation

Diagnosis combines clinical assessment with laboratory measures. Serum retinol concentration (<0.70 µmol/L or 20 µg/dL) is commonly used but can be influenced by infection, inflammation, and recent dietary intake. Clinical eye exams (including conjunctival inspection and slit-lamp assessment) remain valuable, especially where lab access is limited. A careful dietary and supplement history helps distinguish inadequate intake from malabsorption or inappropriate dosing.

Management and prevention considerations

Correcting deficiency is typically done through dietary improvement (liver, eggs, fortified dairy, dark leafy greens, and orange vegetables) and targeted supplementation where indicated. Preformed vitamin A (retinol) is the active form, while provitamin A carotenoids (e.g., beta-carotene) are converted as needed and have a lower toxicity risk. Because vitamin A is fat-soluble, co-ingestion with dietary fat improves absorption. Clinical protocols sometimes use supervised high-dose regimens for severe deficiency, followed by maintenance dosing; these should be managed by healthcare professionals to avoid hypervitaminosis A.

When to consult a clinician

Seek professional evaluation for persistent visual changes, progressive eye dryness, recurrent infections, or when considering therapeutic high-dose vitamin A—especially in pregnancy or when underlying liver disease or malabsorption is present. Clinical assessment ensures appropriate dosing, monitoring, and safety.
