Vitamins are essential micronutrients that support metabolism, immunity, growth, and tissue maintenance. When intake or absorption is insufficient, characteristic deficiency diseases can develop. This concise guide describes ten well-established conditions caused by inadequate intake of specific vitamins, their key symptoms, and general prevention strategies.

1. Vitamin A — Night blindness and xerophthalmia
Vitamin A deficiency impairs retinal function and epithelial integrity. Early signs include difficulty seeing in low light; severe, prolonged deficiency can cause xerophthalmia and irreversible corneal damage. Prevention emphasizes sources like liver, dairy, and carotenoid-rich vegetables.

2. Vitamin B12 — Pernicious anemia and neuropathy
B12 deficiency leads to megaloblastic anemia and neurologic problems (paresthesia, balance issues, cognitive changes). Causes include inadequate dietary intake (vegans), pernicious anemia, and malabsorption. Diagnosis uses serum B12 and methylmalonic acid; treatment may be oral or injectable cobalamin.

3. Vitamin C — Scurvy
Vitamin C is required for collagen synthesis and antioxidant protection. Scurvy presents with fatigue, gum bleeding, petechiae, poor wound healing, and anemia. Regular intake of citrus fruits and vegetables prevents deficiency.

4. Vitamin D — Rickets, osteomalacia, and osteoporosis risk
Vitamin D deficiency impairs calcium homeostasis. In children it causes rickets (bone deformities); in adults it leads to osteomalacia and contributes to osteoporosis and fracture risk. Sun exposure, dietary sources, and targeted supplementation help maintain levels.

5. Vitamin E — Neuromuscular dysfunction
Though rare, vitamin E deficiency (often due to fat-malabsorption) causes peripheral neuropathy, muscle weakness, and coordination problems. Dietary sources include nuts, seeds, and vegetable oils; supplementation is considered under medical supervision.

6. Vitamin K — Bleeding disorders
Insufficient vitamin K disrupts clotting factor synthesis, producing easy bruising, prolonged bleeding, and, in infants, hemorrhagic disease of the newborn. Dark leafy greens and appropriate neonatal prophylaxis or supplementation reduce risks.

7. Thiamine (Vitamin B1) — Beriberi and Wernicke encephalopathy
Thiamine deficiency impairs energy metabolism. “Wet” beriberi affects the cardiovascular system (edema, tachycardia) while “dry” beriberi produces neuropathy. Chronic deficiency in alcohol use disorder can precipitate Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome, a medical emergency.

8. Riboflavin (Vitamin B2) — Mucocutaneous and ocular signs
Riboflavin deficiency presents with cheilosis, angular stomatitis, glossitis, and photophobia. It often coexists with other B-vitamin insufficiencies and responds quickly to dietary correction and supplementation.

9. Folate (Vitamin B9) — Megaloblastic anemia and neural tube defects
Folate deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia and elevated homocysteine; in pregnancy, low folate increases risk of neural tube defects. Fortified foods and periconceptional folic acid supplementation are preventative measures.

10. Niacin (Vitamin B3) — Pellagra
Niacin deficiency produces the classic triad of dermatitis, diarrhea, and dementia (pellagra). It arises with poor diets, certain medications, or malabsorption, and resolves with niacin repletion.

Prevention and diagnosis rely on dietary assessment, targeted laboratory testing, and addressing absorption issues.
