## Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell production, DNA synthesis, myelin maintenance and energy metabolism. Deficiency can develop slowly and present with a mix of hematological, neurological, cognitive and oral signs. For a comprehensive article summarizing causes and practical guidance, see [What are the symptoms of vitamin B12 deficiency?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-are-symptoms-of-vitamin-b12-deficiency).

## Common clinical features

- Fatigue and weakness: Reduced B12 impairs cellular energy production and may cause persistent exhaustion that does not resolve with rest. Muscle weakness and decreased exercise tolerance are common complaints.

- Hematological signs (including pernicious anemia): Macrocytic anemia may present with pallor, shortness of breath, dizziness and palpitations. Pernicious anemia is an autoimmune cause characterized by intrinsic factor deficiency and long-term malabsorption.

- Neurological symptoms: B12 is required for myelin synthesis. Deficiency can cause peripheral neuropathy (numbness, tingling, burning in hands or feet), gait disturbance, impaired coordination and, if prolonged, irreversible nerve damage.

- Cognitive impairment: Patients may report 'brain fog,' memory lapses, difficulty concentrating and slowed processing. In older adults, B12 deficiency has been associated with accelerated cognitive decline.

- Glossitis and oral manifestations: A swollen, red, smooth tongue, recurrent mouth ulcers or angular cheilitis are early mucosal signs that can precede systemic symptoms.

## Mechanisms and risk factors

B12 absorption requires gastric intrinsic factor and an intact terminal ileum. Risk factors include vegetarian or vegan diets, older age (reduced gastric acid), gastric surgery, autoimmune gastritis, inflammatory bowel disease, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth and medications such as proton pump inhibitors or long-term metformin. When absorption is impaired, oral intake—even from fortified foods—may be insufficient.

## Diagnostic considerations

Diagnosis relies on clinical assessment and laboratory testing. Serum B12 concentration is a common initial test but has limitations; additional markers such as methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine can improve diagnostic accuracy. Complete blood count often shows macrocytosis. Neurological signs warrant prompt evaluation to reduce risk of permanent damage.

## Treatment principles

Treatment aims to restore tissue B12 stores and correct underlying causes. Routes include oral high-dose supplementation, sublingual formulations or intramuscular injections, depending on the etiology and absorption capacity. The form of B12 (eg, methylcobalamin vs cyanocobalamin) and dosing schedule should be individualized based on clinical context and laboratory response.

## Takeaway

Vitamin B12 deficiency can present with subtle or multisystem symptoms. Early recognition—particularly of fatigue, neuropathy, cognitive change and oral signs—combined with appropriate laboratory testing and tailored supplementation, reduces the risk of long-term complications. Clinical management should be individualized and guided by laboratory results and underlying causes.
