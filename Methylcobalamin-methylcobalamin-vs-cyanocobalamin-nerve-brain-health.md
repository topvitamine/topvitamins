Vitamin B12 is essential for neurological function, cellular methylation, and neurotransmitter synthesis. Two common supplemental forms—methylcobalamin and cyanocobalamin—differ in chemistry, metabolism, and potential neurological impact. This article summarizes the evidence comparing these forms and offers practical points on absorption and supplementation for nerve and brain health.

Methylcobalamin is a bioactive form of vitamin B12 that participates directly in methylation reactions, including the conversion of homocysteine to methionine. Because it does not require enzymatic conversion to an active form, methylcobalamin can be more readily available to neural tissues involved in myelin maintenance and neurotransmitter production. By contrast, cyanocobalamin is a synthetic analogue that must be metabolically converted to active coenzymes; this conversion can be less efficient in older adults or people with impaired methylation pathways.

Clinical research provides modest but consistent support for methylcobalamin in nerve repair. Studies of peripheral neuropathy (including diabetic neuropathy) report improvements in nerve conduction velocity and symptom reduction with methylcobalamin supplementation. Mechanistically, methylcobalamin supports remyelination and protein synthesis needed for nerve regeneration. Evidence for cognitive benefits is suggestive: maintaining adequate B12 status is associated with better memory and reduced cognitive decline risk, and methylcobalamin’s direct role in methylation may be advantageous in populations with impaired B12 metabolism.

Absorption and delivery considerations matter. Oral cyanocobalamin requires intact gastric function and intrinsic factor for optimal uptake, whereas methylcobalamin in sublingual, liquid, or injectable forms can bypass some absorption barriers. Conditions such as atrophic gastritis, Crohn’s disease, long-term proton pump inhibitor use, or metformin therapy may reduce B12 absorption from oral foods and some supplements. Practical strategies include choosing a formulation that matches the individual’s absorption capacity and monitoring serum B12 markers when treating deficiency.

When evaluating supplements, consider bioavailability, dosing, and co-nutrients. Methylation cycles rely on a balance of folate, B6, and B12; combined B-vitamin approaches are common in clinical practice. Doses used in trials vary widely; clinicians often individualize dosing based on deficiency severity and response. Safety profiles for methylcobalamin are good, with adverse effects uncommon at typical supplemental doses.
