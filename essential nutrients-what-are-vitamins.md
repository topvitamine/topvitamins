## What are vitamins?

Vitamins are organic micronutrients required in small amounts for normal metabolism, growth, and health. They act as coenzymes, cofactors, antioxidants, and signalling molecules that enable biochemical reactions and cellular processes. Because humans cannot synthesize many vitamins in sufficient amounts, they must be obtained from the diet or, when necessary, from supplements. Vitamins are commonly grouped by solubility. Water-soluble vitamins (the B-complex and vitamin C) dissolve in water, are generally not stored extensively, and excesses are often excreted in urine. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are absorbed with dietary fat and can be stored in liver and adipose tissue, so intakes that are too high can accumulate. Supplement formulation reflects these properties: fat-soluble vitamins are often provided in oil-based or emulsified matrices to support absorption, while water-soluble vitamins appear in tablets, capsules, powders, or gummies. Product labels may also list specific chemical forms (for example, methylcobalamin or 5-methyltetrahydrofolate) that can influence bioavailability for certain populations. Appropriate dosing and professional guidance minimize the risk of under- or overconsumption.

Dietary supplements come in many formats—multivitamin-mineral complexes, single-vitamin products, gummies, effervescents, and powders. Multivitamins can help reduce the risk of multiple shortfalls, while single-nutrient preparations allow precise correction of known deficiencies. Lifestyle and clinical factors that commonly create gaps include restrictive eating patterns, limited sun exposure (vitamin D), gastrointestinal disorders that impair absorption, certain medications, and increased physiological demands (pregnancy, growth, aging). Recognizing deficiency signs is important because clinical features vary by nutrient and severity. Vitamin D insufficiency is assessed by serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D and may relate to musculoskeletal symptoms; vitamin B12 deficiency can cause anemia and neurological symptoms and is more likely with malabsorption or strict vegetarian diets; severe vitamin C deficiency causes scurvy with impaired wound healing and bleeding gums, though it is rare in many settings. Confirmatory laboratory testing and evidence-informed repletion strategies under clinical supervision help restore status while avoiding excess—particularly with fat-soluble vitamins that accumulate.

Food remains the preferred source of vitamins because whole foods deliver complex matrices of nutrients and non-nutrient compounds that support health. Citrus fruits, leafy greens, oily fish, fortified foods, legumes, and animal products each provide characteristic vitamin profiles. Supplements are useful adjuncts when diet alone is insufficient; when chosen, they should specify nutrient form and dose, come from reputable manufacturing practices, and align with professional recommendations.
