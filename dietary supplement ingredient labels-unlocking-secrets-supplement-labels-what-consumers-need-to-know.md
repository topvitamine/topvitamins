## Introduction

Dietary supplements are increasingly common tools for supporting health, but their labels can be difficult to interpret. Unlike prescription drugs, many supplements do not undergo pre-market approval, so a label is often the primary source of information about content, dosage, and safety. This article explains how to assess label transparency, read ingredient lists and Supplement Facts panels, and evaluate common label claims using evidence-based principles.

## Why label transparency matters

Transparent labels disclose exact ingredient amounts, sourcing, and testing rather than relying on vague terms or proprietary blends. Brands that share third-party test results or certifications make it easier to verify purity and potency. Conversely, red flags include unspecified proprietary blends, missing allergen information, and absence of independent testing. ## Deciphering ingredient lists

Ingredient lists are typically ordered by weight. Active ingredients should be listed with their specific forms and amounts (for example, magnesium citrate vs. magnesium oxide). Inactive ingredients—fillers, binders, flavorings—are also important, especially for people with allergies or sensitivities. Watch for common allergens and synthetic additives. If a product uses a proprietary blend, the total weight may be given while individual ingredient quantities remain hidden, making it difficult to assess efficacy or safety.

## Reading the Supplement Facts panel

The Supplement Facts panel provides serving size, servings per container, and the amount of each nutrient per serving (mg, mcg, or IU). Daily Values (DVs) indicate the percent contribution to a typical daily intake but may not reflect individual needs. Also note the chemical form or delivery system for nutrients—some forms have higher bioavailability. Comparing equivalent nutrient forms (e.g., methylcobalamin vs. cyanocobalamin for B12) helps determine likely effectiveness.

## Evaluating label claims

Label claims fall into categories such as nutrient content claims, structure/function claims, and health claims. Structure/function claims (e.g., 'supports bone health') are allowed with notification to regulators but do not require pre-approval; health claims that link a product to disease prevention are strictly regulated and require strong evidence. Treat marketing language with caution and seek corroboration from peer-reviewed studies or independent testing.

## Practical steps for safer choices

- Verify exact ingredient amounts and forms.
- Avoid proprietary blends when specific dosing is essential.
- Check for third-party certifications and available lab reports.
- Review inactive ingredient lists for allergens or undesirable additives.
