Magnesium is essential for hundreds of biochemical reactions, including energy production, muscle function, nerve transmission and bone health. Identifying whether your body needs more magnesium requires combining symptom awareness, dietary review and, when appropriate, medical testing.

Common symptoms that suggest low magnesium include persistent fatigue, muscle cramps or twitches, sleep disturbances, headaches or migraines, and mood changes such as increased anxiety or irritability. Cardiac symptoms (palpitations or irregular heartbeat), numbness or tingling in the extremities, difficulty concentrating, and bone-health concerns can also be associated with prolonged magnesium insufficiency. These signs are nonspecific and can overlap with deficiencies of other nutrients (for example, potassium, calcium or certain B vitamins), so context matters.

A practical first step is a focused dietary assessment: do you regularly consume magnesium-rich foods such as leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes and whole grains? Dietary patterns high in processed foods, excessive alcohol intake or chronic gastrointestinal disorders (which impair absorption) increase the risk of low magnesium. Medications like diuretics, proton pump inhibitors and some antibiotics can also lower magnesium levels.

Laboratory testing can help but has limitations. Serum magnesium is the most commonly measured parameter; normal serum concentrations typically range approximately 0.75–0.95 mmol/L (1.8–2.3 mg/dL). However, only a small fraction of total body magnesium is in the blood, so a normal serum result does not always exclude intracellular deficiency. In select cases clinicians may use erythrocyte magnesium, 24-hour urinary magnesium, or clinical response to supplementation to build the diagnostic picture. Discussing testing options with a healthcare professional is advisable if symptoms are persistent or if you have conditions that affect absorption or excretion.

Management begins with addressing diet and lifestyle: increase intake of magnesium-rich foods, moderate alcohol and caffeine, and manage chronic stress which can increase urinary magnesium loss. For some people—particularly those with malabsorption, medication-related loss, or higher physiological needs—oral magnesium supplements can be considered under medical guidance. Common supplemental forms include magnesium citrate and magnesium glycinate, which differ in absorption and tolerability.

Balance is important: while dietary excess of magnesium is rarely harmful, high supplemental doses can cause gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, nausea) and, in cases of significant kidney dysfunction, more serious toxicity. Therefore, supplementation should be tailored to individual needs and medical history.
