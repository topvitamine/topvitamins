## Who should not take dietary supplements?

Dietary supplements can fill nutritional gaps, but they are not universally safe. Certain groups face higher risks of adverse effects, interactions, or toxicity. This concise review outlines who should avoid or use supplements only under professional supervision, and why evidence-based assessment matters.

For a fuller discussion, see [Who should not take dietary supplements?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/who-should-not-take-dietary-supplements).

### People with chronic medical conditions

Chronic illnesses such as liver or kidney disease, autoimmune disorders, and hemochromatosis alter how the body processes vitamins, minerals, and botanicals. Accumulation of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) or minerals like iron can become toxic if clearance is impaired. Patients with autoimmune disease should be cautious with immune-stimulating herbs. Cancer patients should avoid unmonitored antioxidant use during chemotherapy or radiation unless advised by their oncology team.

### Those taking prescription medications

Supplements frequently interact with pharmaceuticals. Blood thinners (e.g., warfarin) may have potentiated bleeding risk when combined with vitamin E, garlic, ginkgo, or high-dose fish oil. St. John’s Wort can reduce plasma concentrations of many drugs and increase the risk of serotonin syndrome when combined with certain antidepressants. Mineral supplements (calcium, magnesium, iron) may bind antibiotics and reduce their effectiveness. Always inform prescribers about all supplements you use.

### Pregnant and breastfeeding women

Pregnancy requires targeted supplementation (e.g., folic acid, iron) under medical guidance. However, high-dose preformed vitamin A (retinol) is teratogenic, and many herbal products lack safety data in pregnancy; some herbs may induce uterine contractions. Pregnant people should use only prenatal formulations recommended by their obstetric provider and avoid unregulated or unclear products.

### Older adults

Physiological changes in older adults affect drug and nutrient metabolism. Polypharmacy raises the chance of drug–supplement interactions. Declining renal or hepatic function increases the risk of accumulation and toxicity from certain vitamins and minerals. Cognitive impairment may also lead to accidental overdosing or mixing products with overlapping ingredients.

### Individuals with allergies or sensitivities

Supplements can contain allergens (soy, dairy, gluten, shellfish, nuts, fish) or traces from cross-contamination. Botanical ingredients may provoke seasonal allergy cross-reactions (e.g., ragweed sensitivity and echinacea). Read labels carefully and prefer hypoallergenic, certified formulations when necessary.

### How to reduce risk

- Seek testing and professional assessment rather than self-diagnosing deficiencies.
- Disclose all supplements to your healthcare team and pharmacist.
- Prefer products with transparent manufacturing and third-party testing; resources on evaluating supplement quality can help (see <a href='https://topvitamine.github.io/topvitamins/unlocking-third-party-tested-supplements-safer-higher-quality-nutrition'>third-party testing guidance</a> and <a href='https://topvitamine.github.io/topvitamins/how-to-tell-if-supplement-is-good-quality'>quality indicators</a> for supplements). A practical primer is also available: <a href='https://telegra.ph/How-to-Tell-If-a-Supplement-Is-Good-Quality-01-11'>How to Tell If a Supplement Is Good Quality</a>.

A balanced diet, routine medical follow-up, and targeted testing should guide supplementation decisions. When used appropriately and with oversight, supplements can complement—but not replace—core health measures such as nutritious eating, sleep, and regular exercise.
