Vitamin B12 plays a central role in physiological processes that underlie athletic endurance, particularly those related to energy metabolism, red blood cell production, and nervous system function. Athletes and active adults often ask how micronutrients influence performance; while no single nutrient guarantees improved outcomes, understanding how B12 supports key biological systems can help inform training, nutrition, and recovery strategies. At the cellular level, vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is a cofactor for enzymes involved in two essential reactions: the conversion of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA (via methylmalonyl-CoA mutase) and the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine (via methionine synthase). These pathways intersect with energy production and the synthesis of important biomolecules. Succinyl-CoA participates in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, a central metabolic pathway for aerobic ATP production. Adequate B12 status therefore helps maintain metabolic processes that contribute to sustained energy availability during prolonged exercise.

Red blood cell formation is another mechanism linking B12 to endurance. Vitamin B12, together with folate and iron, supports erythropoiesis — the production and maturation of red blood cells. Healthy red blood cell counts and hemoglobin concentration are associated with efficient oxygen transport to working muscles. While B12 deficiency can impair proper red blood cell maturation and lead to megaloblastic anemia, maintaining normal B12 levels is one component of preserving oxygen delivery capacity, which athletes rely on for aerobic performance.

Neurological function and recovery also intersect with athletic demands. B12 contributes to myelin maintenance and neuronal health through its roles in methylation reactions. Optimal nerve conduction supports motor control, coordination, and proprioception — all relevant during training and competition. Chronic B12 insufficiency has been associated with neuropathic symptoms, which may indirectly affect physical performance by impairing neuromuscular function.

Sources of vitamin B12 are primarily animal-derived: meat, fish, dairy, and eggs. Individuals following strict vegetarian or vegan diets, older adults with reduced absorption, and people with certain gastrointestinal conditions may be at greater risk for inadequate intake or absorption. In these cases, fortified foods or supplementation can help achieve sufficient status; monitoring biomarkers such as serum B12, methylmalonic acid, or holotranscobalamin can inform whether intervention is needed.
