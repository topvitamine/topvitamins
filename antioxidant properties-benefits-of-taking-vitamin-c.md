**Introduction**

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water‑soluble micronutrient essential for multiple physiological processes. Humans cannot synthesize it and rely on diet or supplementation to maintain adequate levels. Beyond preventing deficiency, sufficient vitamin C supports immune function, acts as a potent antioxidant, and is required for collagen synthesis—roles that underpin its broad health relevance.

Understanding vitamin C’s mechanisms helps clarify why modest supplementation can be useful when dietary intake is inadequate or needs increase during stress, illness, or recovery.

**Immune support and infection outcomes**

Vitamin C contributes to both innate and adaptive immunity. It accumulates in leukocytes and supports functions such as chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and the clearance of spent immune cells. Meta-analyses suggest regular supplementation can reduce the duration and severity of common respiratory infections, particularly in people under physical stress or with low baseline vitamin C status. **Antioxidant activity and cellular protection**

As an antioxidant, vitamin C neutralizes reactive oxygen species and helps regenerate other antioxidants like vitamin E. This activity reduces oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA, processes implicated in aging and chronic disease. By supporting antioxidant defenses, vitamin C can mitigate cellular stress from environmental exposures (e.g., pollution, UV radiation) and physiological stressors.

**Collagen synthesis and connective tissue health**

Vitamin C is a required cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase enzymes that stabilize collagen. Adequate vitamin C supports wound healing, skin integrity, and joint connective tissue maintenance. Deficiency impairs collagen formation and historically produced scurvy; at physiological doses, vitamin C supports tissue repair and may benefit recovery after injury or surgery.

**Potential roles in chronic disease prevention**

Observational studies associate higher serum vitamin C with favorable cardiovascular markers such as lower blood pressure and improved endothelial function. Laboratory and animal data indicate antioxidant and anti‑inflammatory mechanisms that could influence long‑term disease risk, but large randomized trials yield mixed results. Vitamin C should be considered as one component of a comprehensive dietary and lifestyle approach to disease prevention rather than a standalone cure.

**Practical considerations and safety**

Dietary sources—citrus, berries, peppers, kiwi, and leafy greens—are first‑line sources. Supplementation (e.g., buffered, time‑release, or liposomal forms) can help maintain steady intake when diet is insufficient. Typical supplemental ranges used in studies are 500–1,000 mg/day for immune support; most healthy adults tolerate these amounts well, though very high doses can cause gastrointestinal symptoms and warrant clinical discussion.
