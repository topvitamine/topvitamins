Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble vitamin with well-documented roles in collagen synthesis, antioxidant defense, and immune function. While it is not a panacea, adequate intake of vitamin C helps prevent specific deficiency diseases and contributes to reduced risk or severity of several common health problems through biological mechanisms supported by clinical and epidemiological evidence.

Severe deficiency causes scurvy, a now-rare but historically important disease characterized by bleeding gums, poor wound healing, fatigue, anemia, and connective tissue breakdown. Even modest daily supplementation (often 100–300 mg) can reverse scurvy symptoms and restore normal collagen function within days to weeks. At-risk groups include people with restricted diets, alcohol use disorder, certain neurodevelopmental conditions, and those living in food-insecure settings.

Beyond deficiency prevention, vitamin C supports immune defenses. It contributes to innate and adaptive immunity by enhancing neutrophil function, supporting lymphocyte proliferation, and reducing oxidative damage to immune cells. Randomized trials and meta-analyses indicate that regular vitamin C supplementation can modestly reduce the duration and severity of upper respiratory infections, particularly in individuals under physical stress (for example, endurance athletes or military recruits). For general populations, the preventive effect on the incidence of the common cold is small, but supplementation reliably shortens illness length when taken routinely.

As an antioxidant, vitamin C neutralizes reactive oxygen species and helps regenerate other antioxidants such as vitamin E and glutathione. This antioxidant action is mechanistically relevant to chronic conditions in which oxidative stress contributes to pathogenesis—examples include cardiovascular disease and some aspects of metabolic dysfunction. Observational studies associate higher vitamin C status with improved endothelial function and lower biomarkers of oxidative damage; however, routine high-dose supplementation as a primary preventive strategy for chronic disease remains an area of active research rather than settled clinical guidance.

Respiratory health also benefits from vitamin C’s combined antioxidant and immunomodulatory effects. Supplementation has been linked to reduced bronchial inflammation and improved outcomes in some infectious and inflammatory respiratory conditions. In exercise-induced bronchoconstriction, single doses before exertion have reduced post-exercise symptoms in some trials. These effects are more pronounced in individuals with low baseline vitamin C levels or high oxidative stress exposure (e.g., smokers, people in polluted urban environments).

Recommended intake for adults typically ranges from 75 mg (women) to 90 mg (men) per day, with higher intakes (200–1000 mg) commonly used in studies of immune support. Vitamin C is water soluble and excess amounts are excreted; doses above 2,000 mg/day can cause gastrointestinal discomfort in some individuals. Smokers have increased oxidative needs and are often advised to consume an additional ~35 mg/day to offset depletion.

For practical guidance, a diet rich in citrus fruits, bell peppers, strawberries, kiwi, and cruciferous vegetables provides ample vitamin C for most people. When dietary intake is inadequate, selected supplements may help restore optimal status.
