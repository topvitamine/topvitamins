## Introduction

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in more than 300 enzymatic reactions in the body, supporting energy production, nerve signaling, muscle contraction, and heart rhythm. Although abundant in many tissues—about 50–60% is stored in bone—dietary intake can fall short due to modern eating patterns and soil depletion. Understanding sources, deficiency signs, and appropriate supplementation helps maintain metabolic, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and neurological health.

## What magnesium does

Magnesium acts as a cofactor for enzymes involved in ATP production, DNA/RNA synthesis, and neurotransmitter regulation. It modulates calcium and potassium movement across cell membranes, a mechanism crucial for muscle relaxation and cardiac rhythm. Adequate magnesium contributes to normal blood pressure, bone mineralization, and glucose metabolism, and it influences sleep and mood through interactions with GABA and serotonin pathways.

## Recognizing magnesium deficiency

Deficiency can be subtle: early signs include fatigue, muscle cramps, numbness or tingling, and sleep disturbances. More advanced hypomagnesemia may present with arrhythmias, increased anxiety or depression symptoms, and in extreme cases seizures. Risk factors include gastrointestinal disorders that impair absorption, chronic alcoholism, type 2 diabetes, certain medications (diuretics, proton pump inhibitors), older age, and renal dysfunction.

Serum magnesium tests can be informative but may not reflect total body stores; in some cases, red blood cell magnesium or clinical evaluation adds context.

## Dietary sources and practical intake strategies

Whole foods are the primary recommendation for meeting magnesium needs. Rich sources include leafy greens (spinach, chard), nuts and seeds (almonds, pumpkin seeds), legumes, whole grains (quinoa, brown rice, oats), and fatty fish (salmon, mackerel). Combining magnesium-rich foods with vitamin D and vitamin K sources supports absorption and bone health. ## Supplements: forms and considerations

Supplement forms differ in absorption and effects. Magnesium citrate and malate are often well absorbed; glycinate is noted for lower gastrointestinal side effects and is frequently used when improving sleep or reducing anxiety. Oxide contains more elemental magnesium by weight but has lower bioavailability and may act as a laxative. Magnesium threonate is under study for cognitive effects due to its brain-penetrant properties.

Tolerable upper intake levels for supplemental magnesium are set to limit adverse gastrointestinal effects; people with kidney impairment should exercise caution because impaired excretion can lead to elevated serum magnesium. Magnesium can interact with certain medications (e.g., some antibiotics and bisphosphonates), so timing and clinician consultation are advisable.

## Clinical and lifestyle benefits

Evidence links adequate magnesium with lower blood pressure, improved insulin sensitivity, reduced muscle cramps, and better sleep quality for some individuals. It supports bone structure and may reduce the frequency or severity of migraine in select populations. While supplementation can be helpful when dietary intake is insufficient or needs are increased (e.g., athletic training, certain medical conditions), whole-food strategies remain foundational.
