## Introduction

Vitamin D, the “sunshine vitamin,” is essential for calcium metabolism, bone health, and immune function. Many people get inadequate amounts from sunlight or diet, so identifying reliable food sources is important. Below are ten foods that meaningfully contribute to vitamin D intake, with evidence-based notes on amounts and considerations.

## Top 10 Foods Rich in Vitamin D

1. Salmon (wild-caught)

   Wild salmon is among the richest natural sources of vitamin D. A 100 g serving can provide roughly 360–988 IU depending on the species and season. It also supplies omega-3 fatty acids that support cardiovascular and bone health.

2. Mackerel

   Atlantic and King mackerel deliver approximately 360 IU per 100 g cooked. Mackerel is a nutrient-dense option combining vitamin D with long-chain omega-3s.

3. Sardines (canned)

   A 100 g portion of canned sardines contains about 270 IU and adds calcium and phosphorus, which are important cofactors for bone maintenance.

4. Canned light tuna

   Canned light tuna offers around 230 IU per 100 g and is a convenient shelf-stable source; intake should be moderated in pregnant women and children due to mercury considerations.

5. Cod liver oil

   Traditionally used to prevent deficiency, a teaspoon of cod liver oil can deliver roughly 450–1,360 IU depending on the product. It is also a source of vitamin A and omega-3s; quality and dosing should be considered.

6. Egg yolks

   Eggs contain vitamin D in the yolk (typically 18–40 IU per yolk in conventional eggs). Hens given vitamin D–enriched feed or pasture access can produce eggs with markedly higher levels.

7. UV-exposed mushrooms

   Certain mushrooms (e.g., maitake, portobello) exposed to ultraviolet light synthesize vitamin D2, sometimes providing several hundred IU per 100 g. D2 raises serum vitamin D but may differ in potency and duration compared with D3.

8. Fortified cow’s milk

   Many countries fortify milk with vitamin D (commonly ~100 IU per cup/240 mL). Fortified dairy also supplies calcium, which works synergistically for bone health.

9. Fortified plant milks and orange juice

   Soy, almond, oat milks, and some orange juices are frequently fortified to match dairy levels (roughly 100 IU per serving). These products are important options for people avoiding animal foods.

10. Fortified breakfast cereals and spreads

   Ready-to-eat cereals, margarine-like spreads, and some yogurts may carry added vitamin D (variable amounts). These can complement other sources but typically provide modest contributions per serving.

## Practical notes

- Vitamin D exists as D2 (ergocalciferol) and D3 (cholecalciferol); D3 is generally considered more effective at raising and maintaining serum 25(OH)D levels.
- Populations at risk for low vitamin D include older adults, people with limited sun exposure, individuals with darker skin, and those following strict plant-based diets. Regular monitoring and, when appropriate, supplementation can help achieve recommended serum concentrations.
