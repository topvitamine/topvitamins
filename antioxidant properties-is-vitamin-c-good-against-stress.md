Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential nutrient with well-established roles in immune support, antioxidant defense, and collagen synthesis. Emerging research also explores its influence on the physiological stress response — notably antioxidant protection, neurotransmitter synthesis, and potential modulation of cortisol. Immune support and oxidative protection

Stress, whether psychological or physical, is associated with transient reductions in immune function and increases in reactive oxygen species (ROS). Vitamin C contributes to innate and adaptive immunity by supporting phagocyte function, protecting immune cells from oxidative damage, and maintaining epithelial barrier integrity. As a water-soluble antioxidant, it scavenges free radicals in plasma and intracellular fluids, which can help reduce oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA — damage that is often elevated during chronic stress.

Cortisol and adrenal function

Vitamin C concentrates in the adrenal glands and participates in corticosteroid synthesis. Several small human and animal studies suggest supplementation can attenuate cortisol responses after acute stressors and reduce associated cardiovascular responses (e.g., blood pressure). While findings are promising, the evidence base is still limited by sample sizes and study heterogeneity. Larger, controlled trials are needed to confirm consistent cortisol-lowering effects and clarify optimal dosing strategies.

Practical use and forms

Dietary sources (citrus fruits, berries, bell peppers, leafy greens) can meet typical daily needs, but plasma and tissue concentrations may decline under sustained stress. Clinical research often evaluates supplemental doses in the range of 500–1,000 mg/day for short-term effects on physiological stress markers. The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) is 65–90 mg/day for adults, with an upper limit of 2,000 mg/day to avoid gastrointestinal upset. Supplement formats include conventional tablets, chewables, powders, and liposomal preparations that may alter absorption kinetics.

Evidence summary and recommendations

Current evidence supports biological plausibility for Vitamin C to assist with stress-related physiology: antioxidant protection, neurotransmitter synthesis, and potential modulation of cortisol. However, human trials are not yet uniform in design or outcomes. Practical recommendations emphasize obtaining Vitamin C from a balanced diet and considering moderate supplementation during periods of increased physical or psychological stress. If supplements are considered, staying within established safety limits and consulting a healthcare professional for long-term or high-dose use is advisable.

Conclusion

Vitamin C is a well-tolerated, evidence-supported nutrient that can contribute to a broader stress-management plan through antioxidant activity, immune support, and possible effects on adrenal hormones. It should be viewed as one component of a comprehensive approach that includes sleep, exercise, nutrition, and psychosocial strategies.
