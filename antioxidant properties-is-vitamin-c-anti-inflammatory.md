**Introduction**

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is widely recognized for its antioxidant and immune-supporting roles. Recent research asks whether these properties translate into measurable anti-inflammatory effects. This article summarizes current evidence on how vitamin C may influence inflammation via oxidative stress reduction, immune modulation, and collagen synthesis.

**How vitamin C interacts with inflammation**

Inflammation and oxidative stress are closely linked: reactive oxygen species (ROS) activate signaling pathways (for example, NF-κB) that upregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. Vitamin C is a potent water-soluble antioxidant that scavenges ROS in plasma and cytosol, can regenerate other antioxidants (notably vitamin E), and therefore reduces oxidative triggers of inflammation.

Clinical and experimental studies have reported reductions in biomarkers associated with inflammation — for example, decreases in C-reactive protein (CRP) and IL-6 following supplementation in some populations. High-dose intravenous vitamin C has also been investigated in critically ill patients, where it sometimes attenuates systemic inflammatory responses, though findings vary by study design and patient group.

**Immune modulation and tissue repair**

Vitamin C supports leukocyte function, enhances epithelial barrier integrity, and promotes lymphocyte proliferation and activity. By improving immune efficiency and protecting immune cells from oxidative damage, vitamin C helps keep inflammatory responses proportionate to the threat.

Separately, vitamin C is an essential cofactor for enzymes involved in collagen hydroxylation, a requirement for stable collagen formation. Efficient collagen synthesis supports wound healing and structural tissue integrity, which can reduce chronic inflammation driven by ongoing tissue damage.

**Topical vs oral vitamin C for skin inflammation**

Topical formulations (often L-ascorbic acid) deliver antioxidant activity directly to the epidermis and can blunt UV-induced oxidative damage and local inflammatory reactions. Oral vitamin C contributes systemically, supporting connective tissues and reducing circulating oxidative stress that can exacerbate skin inflammation. In many cases a combined topical and oral approach yields complementary benefits.

**Dosage, safety, and practical considerations**

Recommended daily intakes for general health are typically 75–90 mg for adults, but many studies exploring anti-inflammatory or therapeutic effects use higher doses (commonly 200–1000 mg/day). Vitamin C is water soluble and generally well tolerated; doses above 2,000 mg/day can cause gastrointestinal symptoms in some people. Splitting doses or using sustained-release preparations can improve steady plasma levels.

Individual responses vary by baseline status (deficiency vs adequate intake), the presence of chronic disease, and the form and dose of vitamin C used. Combining vitamin C with other antioxidants or anti-inflammatory nutrients (for example, vitamin D or magnesium) may offer synergistic effects.

**Conclusion**

Evidence indicates vitamin C can reduce inflammation indirectly by limiting oxidative stress, supporting immune regulation, and promoting tissue repair. It is not a standalone cure for inflammatory diseases, but it can be a safe, supportive component of an overall anti-inflammatory strategy when used at appropriate doses and in context with other lifestyle and nutritional measures.
