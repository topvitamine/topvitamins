Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, DNA synthesis, neurological function, and energy metabolism. Deficiency is common in older adults, vegans, vegetarians, and people with malabsorption. Choosing the right form and delivery method can improve outcomes, so it helps to understand the differences between methylcobalamin, cyanocobalamin, adenosylcobalamin, hydroxycobalamin, sublingual products, and injections.

Forms and delivery methods

Methylcobalamin is an active, methylated coenzyme form of B12 that the body can use directly. It supports methylation pathways, neurological health, and homocysteine regulation. Because it does not require conversion, methylcobalamin is often recommended for people with impaired methylation (for example, MTHFR polymorphisms) or neurological symptoms.

Cyanocobalamin is a synthetic, stable, and inexpensive form widely used in supplements and fortified foods. It must be converted in the body to the active forms (methylcobalamin or adenosylcobalamin). For most healthy people this conversion is adequate, but it may be less efficient in older adults or those with liver, kidney, or genetic issues.

Adenosylcobalamin (sometimes called cobamamide) is the mitochondrial form of B12 and plays a central role in cellular energy production. It is less commonly used in consumer supplements but is important for people seeking metabolic support.

Sublingual B12 products, which often contain methylcobalamin or cyanocobalamin, are designed to be absorbed through oral mucosa. This route bypasses some digestive steps and can be helpful for people with low stomach acid or mild malabsorption. In clinical practice, B12 injections—usually methylcobalamin or hydroxocobalamin—are used when rapid repletion or guaranteed absorption is required, such as in pernicious anemia or post-gastric surgery patients.

Comparing effectiveness

Active B12 forms (methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin) are the most bioavailable because they do not require enzymatic conversion. In practice, injections provide the fastest and most complete correction of deficiency. Oral and sublingual supplements can be effective for maintenance and for many people with normal absorption; choice depends on individual factors like age, diet, genetics, and clinical symptoms.

Who should prefer which form?

- Vegans and vegetarians: reliable supplementation with an active form such as methylcobalamin is advisable because plant foods lack B12.  
- Older adults and people with GI conditions: consider sublingual formulations or clinician-supervised injections.  
- Individuals with MTHFR variants or neurological complaints: methylcobalamin (or combined active B12s) may be preferable.

Summary

Active forms of B12 (methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin) are the most bioavailable; cyanocobalamin remains a stable and economical option for many. Sublingual preparations are convenient for rapid absorption when digestive absorption is a concern, while injections are reserved for significant deficiency or malabsorption. Tailor choice to your health status, genetics, diet, and medical advice.
