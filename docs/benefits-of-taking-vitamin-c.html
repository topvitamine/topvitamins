Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential water-soluble nutrient required for multiple biochemical processes. Humans must obtain it from food or supplements. It functions as an antioxidant, a cofactor in enzymatic reactions, and a modulator of immune responses, with implications for tissue repair, cardiovascular health, and resistance to infection.

Immune support and infection recovery

Vitamin C accumulates in immune cells and supports their function. It enhances neutrophil migration and phagocytosis, supports lymphocyte proliferation, and helps limit oxidative damage that occurs during inflammatory responses. Randomized trials and systematic reviews indicate that regular supplementation can modestly reduce the duration and severity of upper respiratory infections, particularly in people under physical stress or with low baseline vitamin C status. Consistent daily intake appears more effective than sporadic high doses for maintaining immune readiness.

Antioxidant activity and cardiovascular effects

As a potent water-soluble antioxidant, vitamin C neutralizes reactive oxygen species and helps regenerate lipid-soluble antioxidants such as vitamin E. Observational studies associate higher vitamin C status with lower blood pressure and improved endothelial function, and some controlled trials report modest improvements in biomarkers related to cardiovascular risk. These findings support a plausible role for vitamin C in mitigating oxidative stress in the vasculature, though it should be considered complementary to established lifestyle and medical approaches for cardiovascular disease prevention.

Collagen synthesis, skin and connective tissue

Vitamin C is an essential cofactor for the enzymes that hydroxylate proline and lysine during collagen formation. Adequate vitamin C supports wound healing, connective tissue integrity, and skin health by promoting proper collagen cross-linking. Both dietary intake and supplementation can aid recovery after injury, improve scar quality, and contribute to skin elasticity and resilience against photoaging.

Metabolic and chronic-disease considerations

There is emerging evidence that vitamin C may influence metabolic health by improving markers such as fasting glucose and insulin sensitivity in certain populations, likely due to its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. Research into cancer prevention and progression shows mixed results: mechanistic and preclinical studies are promising, but large-scale human trials have not established a clear preventive effect. Maintaining adequate antioxidant status remains a reasonable component of broader chronic disease risk reduction strategies.

Practical use and safety

Recommended intakes vary by authority; typical dietary targets for adults range from 75–120 mg/day, with higher demands for smokers, pregnant or lactating individuals, and people under physiological stress. Supplemental doses of 500–1000 mg/day are commonly used and generally well tolerated; very high intakes can cause gastrointestinal upset and may increase oxalate excretion in susceptible individuals. Different supplemental forms (buffered, liposomal, time-release) can affect tolerance and absorption. Vitamin C also enhances non-heme iron absorption, which can be beneficial in some contexts but should be monitored in cases of iron overload.

Decisions about supplementation should be individualized and discussed with a healthcare professional.
