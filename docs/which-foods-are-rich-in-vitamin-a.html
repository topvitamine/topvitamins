Introduction

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble micronutrient essential for vision, immune function, epithelial integrity and cellular communication. It exists as preformed vitamin A (retinol and retinyl esters) in animal products and as provitamin A carotenoids (notably beta-carotene) in plant foods. Because the body cannot synthesize vitamin A de novo, dietary intake from a range of foods is necessary to meet physiological needs.

Key dietary sources

Animal-derived foods provide preformed vitamin A, which is highly bioavailable. Typical high-content items include:

  • Liver (beef, chicken) — very concentrated source of retinol
  • Whole eggs and full-fat dairy
  • Fish liver oils (used historically for deficiency prevention)

Plant sources supply provitamin A carotenoids that the body converts to retinol. Common carotenoid-rich foods include:

  • Orange and yellow vegetables: carrots, sweet potatoes, pumpkin, butternut squash
  • Leafy greens: spinach, kale, collard greens, Swiss chard
  • Fruits with orange/red coloring: mango, cantaloupe, apricot, papaya
  • Red bell peppers and other colorful vegetables

Bioavailability and preparation

Conversion efficiency from beta-carotene to retinol varies by food matrix, individual factors and preparation. A commonly cited approximation is that ~12 micrograms of dietary beta-carotene yields 1 microgram retinol activity equivalent (RAE), but this can change with processing and fat intake. Consuming carotenoid-rich foods with a small amount of dietary fat (olive oil, avocado, nuts) improves absorption. Light cooking (steaming, sautéing) can increase carotenoid bioaccessibility compared with raw consumption for some vegetables like carrots and spinach.

Balancing sources and safety

Combining plant and animal sources helps ensure a balance of retinoids and carotenoids. Very high intake of preformed vitamin A (for example, frequent large portions of liver or high-dose retinol supplements) can lead to toxicity, whereas carotenoids from plant foods are regulated by conversion mechanisms and pose lower toxicity risk. Recommended dietary allowances vary by age, sex and physiological state; adult RDAs are commonly expressed in micrograms RAE per day.

Practical tips

Include a mix of colorful vegetables, leafy greens, a portion of eggs or dairy where appropriate, and occasional small servings of liver for those without contraindications. Simple habits—adding a drizzle of oil to a salad, lightly sautéing greens, or pairing fruit salads with a handful of nuts—can improve carotenoid absorption. Conclusion

Vitamin A intake is best supported through a varied diet that includes both carotenoid-rich plant foods and, where appropriate, animal sources of retinol. Attention to preparation and dietary fat improves bioavailability, while moderation of concentrated preformed vitamin A sources reduces risk of excess.
