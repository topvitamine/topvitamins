Are Eggs High in Vitamin A? What the Evidence Shows

Eggs are a widely consumed, nutrient-dense food. They contain fat-soluble vitamin A primarily in the yolk, but how much they contribute to daily needs depends on egg type, hen diet and cooking method. A typical large egg yolk supplies roughly 64 µg of retinol (about 7–9% of the adult recommended daily allowance), which makes eggs a moderate — not high — dietary source of preformed vitamin A.

Vitamin A forms and bioavailability

Vitamin A in animal foods appears as retinol or retinyl esters (preformed vitamin A), which the body can use directly. Eggs also contain carotenoids such as lutein and zeaxanthin; these support eye health but are not strong provitamin A sources compared with beta-carotene. The fat-rich yolk enhances absorption of retinol and carotenoids, so bioavailability from eggs is high relative to many plant sources.

How much vitamin A is in an egg?

According to food composition databases, a standard large egg (about 50 g including shell) provides approximately 64–75 µg retinol equivalents, concentrated in the ~17 g yolk. By comparison, liver delivers several times this amount per serving, while fortified foods and supplements provide precise, higher doses. Cooking reduces vitamin A modestly (estimates typically range 10–20% loss depending on temperature and time), but much remains available after boiling or light frying.

Which eggs contain more vitamin A?

Hen diet and husbandry are key determinants. Pasture-raised and organic hens with access to diverse forage and carotenoid-rich plants often produce eggs with higher retinol and carotenoid content. Intentionally enriched eggs (through fortified feed) can contain substantially more vitamin A than conventional eggs. Practical implications

For most adults, including eggs regularly contributes useful amounts of bioavailable vitamin A while also providing protein, choline and other micronutrients. Eggs are a good complementary source within a varied diet but are not a replacement for clinical supplementation in deficiency states. Key takeaways

  • Eggs provide moderate amounts of preformed vitamin A, mostly in the yolk (≈64 µg per large egg).
  • Bioavailability from eggs is high due to yolk lipids, and carotenoids like lutein add eye-health benefits.
  • Pasture-raised, organic or feed-enriched eggs often contain more vitamin A than conventional eggs.
  • Eggs are supportive within a food-first strategy but are not a substitute for high-dose therapeutic supplements when needed.
