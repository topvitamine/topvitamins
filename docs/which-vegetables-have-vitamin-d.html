Introduction

Vitamin D supports bone health, immune function and mood regulation, but dietary sources are limited for plant-based eaters. While the body produces vitamin D when exposed to UVB sunlight, geographic location, seasonality and lifestyle can limit this synthesis. Understanding which vegetables and plant-based foods provide vitamin D helps vegetarians and vegans plan more effective strategies for maintaining adequate levels.

Natural plant sources: mushrooms

Mushrooms are the principal natural plant-associated source of vitamin D. When exposed to ultraviolet light, fungal ergosterol converts to vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol). Varieties such as maitake, shiitake, portobello and UV-treated white button mushrooms can contain substantial amounts of D2 per serving, depending on exposure and processing. Scientific studies show that eating UV-exposed mushrooms raises circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D, though D2 has a shorter half-life and may be less potent than animal-derived D3.

Fortified plant foods and practical options

Because most vegetables contain negligible vitamin D, fortified foods are a practical complement. Commonly fortified items include plant-based milks (soy, almond, oat), some breakfast cereals, fortified tofu and selected spreads. Occasionally, pre-packaged salad mixes or ready-to-eat vegetable products are fortified, but this is inconsistent across brands and regions. Always read labels to identify “fortified with vitamin D” and check whether the fortificant is D2 or plant-sourced D3.

Dietary strategies for vegetarians and vegans

Combine UV-exposed mushrooms with fortified foods and dietary fat (vitamin D is fat soluble) to enhance absorption. Meal examples include grilled portobello sandwiches, mushroom-forward stir-fries with a nut or olive oil base, and smoothies made with fortified plant milk. Regular monitoring of blood 25(OH)D can guide whether dietary sources alone are adequate or whether supplementation is advisable.

Key takeaways

Mushrooms exposed to UV light are the most reliable natural plant-associated source of vitamin D. Fortified plant foods provide predictable contributions when labels specify IU or micrograms. Because D2 and D3 differ in potency and persistence, periodic testing and an evidence-based approach to diet and supplementation ensure adequate vitamin D status for those following vegetarian or vegan diets.
