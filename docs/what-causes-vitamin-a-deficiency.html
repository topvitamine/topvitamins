Introduction

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble micronutrient essential for vision, immune function, epithelial integrity and growth. Deficiency remains a public health concern in many regions and can range from mild biochemical insufficiency to severe clinical syndromes such as xerophthalmia and increased infection risk. Understanding the common causes helps target prevention and treatment efforts.

1. Inadequate dietary intake

A primary cause of low vitamin A status is insufficient intake of preformed vitamin A (retinol) from animal foods or provitamin A carotenoids from plants. Diets dominated by staple grains with low diversity, restrictive vegetarian or vegan patterns without careful planning, and avoidance of animal-source foods can reduce intake. Conversion of carotenoids (e.g., beta-carotene) to retinol is variable between individuals and is influenced by genetics, nutritional status and meal composition, so plant-rich diets do not always guarantee adequate bioavailable vitamin A.

2. Fat malabsorption and digestive disorders

Because vitamin A is fat-soluble, conditions that impair dietary fat absorption also reduce vitamin A uptake. Chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, cholestatic liver disease, small bowel resections and other malabsorptive states can lead to clinically significant deficiency despite reasonable dietary intake. Use of very-low-fat diets or medications that affect bile production or fat emulsification may further impair absorption.

3. Increased physiological demand and illness

Periods of rapid growth (infancy and childhood), pregnancy and lactation increase vitamin A requirements. Acute and chronic infections drive metabolic turnover and can deplete liver stores; measles and diarrheal diseases are notable contributors to deficiency-related morbidity in children. Immune compromise and chronic inflammation also alter vitamin A kinetics and tissue distribution, making repletion more challenging.

4. Socioeconomic, cultural and behavioral factors

Food insecurity, limited access to diverse foods, cultural food taboos and misinformation about dietary fats can all reduce vitamin A intake or absorption. Public-health interventions such as food fortification, targeted supplementation and dietary education address many of these barriers. For individuals, combining carotenoid-rich vegetables with a source of dietary fat improves carotenoid absorption and conversion.

5. Risk to vision: xerophthalmia and night blindness

Severe deficiency manifests in the eye as night blindness, conjunctival changes and ultimately corneal damage (xerophthalmia). Early recognition and prompt therapeutic dosing of vitamin A can reverse many visual deficits; however, prolonged deficiency risks irreversible vision loss.

Practical considerations

Assessment of risk should combine dietary history, clinical signs and, when available, biochemical measures. Supplementation strategies differ by context: high-dose periodic dosing is used in many child health programs, while individualized regimens address malabsorption or pregnancy.
