Introduction

Minerals are inorganic elements essential for cellular function, structural integrity, and metabolic regulation. Because the body cannot synthesize them, daily intake through food, fortified products, or supplements is necessary to maintain electrolyte balance, bone health, immune function, and enzyme activity. This article summarizes seven minerals commonly regarded as foundational to daily health—calcium, magnesium, potassium, zinc, iron, selenium, and iodine—and offers practical guidance on intake and sources.

Key Functions and Recommended Daily Intakes

Each mineral plays distinct physiological roles and has established reference intakes that vary by age and sex. Typical adult daily targets are roughly: calcium 1,000–1,200 mg; magnesium 310–420 mg; potassium 3,500–4,700 mg; zinc 8–11 mg; iron 8–18 mg (higher for menstruating women); selenium 55 µg; and iodine 150 µg. These values provide a framework for planning diet and supplements but should be adapted to individual needs.

Food Sources and Absorption Considerations

Whole foods are primary sources and often contain cofactors that improve mineral absorption. Good examples include dairy, leafy greens, and fortified plant milks for calcium; nuts, seeds, and whole grains for magnesium; fruits, potatoes, and legumes for potassium; seafood and legumes for zinc and iron; brazil nuts and fish for selenium; and seaweed or iodized salt for iodine. Absorption can be affected by food components (phytates reduce iron and zinc uptake) and by interactions between minerals (high calcium can impair iron absorption; excessive zinc can reduce copper status), so varied dietary patterns help minimize competitive effects.

Identifying Deficiencies and When to Test

Deficiency symptoms often appear gradually and can be nonspecific: fatigue, muscle cramps, hair loss, poor wound healing, or thyroid dysfunction. Laboratory assessments—serum or urine tests—are useful for confirming status and guiding supplementation. Persistent or multiple symptoms warrant consultation with a healthcare professional for targeted testing and interpretation.

Safe Supplementation and Balancing Intake

Supplements can help when diet alone is insufficient, particularly in populations with elevated needs such as pregnant individuals, older adults, or those with restrictive diets. Choose bioavailable forms (for example, magnesium citrate or glycinate rather than oxide) and avoid exceeding established upper intake levels to prevent toxicity. Staggering supplements—such as separating iron from calcium—reduces absorption competition. Conclusion

Maintaining adequate intake of calcium, magnesium, potassium, zinc, iron, selenium, and iodine supports fundamental physiological functions. Prioritize a diverse, nutrient-dense diet, consider testing when symptoms arise, and use supplementation judiciously and evidence-based to address confirmed gaps. Combining food-first strategies with informed supplementation helps sustain balanced mineral status over the long term.
