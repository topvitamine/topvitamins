What side effects can vitamin C cause?

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential, water-soluble nutrient known for antioxidant activity, immune support, and collagen synthesis. When obtained from food, it is generally safe. However, supplemental vitamin C—especially at high doses—can cause side effects, interact with medical conditions or tests, and in rare cases lead to more serious complications. This article summarizes common, uncommon, and potentially toxic effects to help readers use vitamin C more safely.

Common side effects

The most frequent problems from supplemental vitamin C are gastrointestinal. Large single doses often cause nausea, abdominal cramping, bloating and osmotic diarrhea because unabsorbed ascorbic acid draws water into the intestine. Splitting a daily dose into smaller amounts and choosing buffered forms (for example, calcium ascorbate) can reduce acidity-related stomach upset. Persistent GI symptoms that do not improve when lowering the dose should prompt medical evaluation.

Interactions and test interference

Vitamin C can affect laboratory tests and interact with certain medications. High plasma concentrations may interfere with some glucose meters and urine glucose tests, potentially producing false readings. It can also enhance iron absorption, which is undesirable in iron-overload disorders such as hemochromatosis or thalassemia. People taking blood thinners, chemotherapy agents, or certain cardiovascular drugs should discuss supplementation with their clinician to avoid unintended interactions.

Overdose and toxicity

Although true toxicity is rare because vitamin C is excreted in urine, chronic intake above the Tolerable Upper Intake Level (2,000 mg/day for adults) increases risk of adverse effects. One notable concern is the conversion of excess vitamin C into oxalate, which can contribute to calcium oxalate kidney stone formation in susceptible individuals. Those with reduced kidney function should be cautious, as impaired clearance can lead to accumulation of metabolites.

Allergic and rare reactions

True allergy to ascorbic acid is extremely uncommon. Reported allergic-like reactions are more often due to inactive ingredients in supplements (fillers, colorants, flavorings). Symptoms such as hives, swelling, or respiratory difficulty require immediate medical attention. Rare adverse events include hemolytic anemia in people with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency; high doses may increase oxidative stress in these patients and precipitate hemolysis.

Practical guidance

To reduce risk: favor dietary sources when possible, adhere to recommended daily amounts, avoid chronic megadoses, and review supplement labels for additives.
