What Deficiency Causes Blurry Vision?

Blurry vision can result from refractive errors, eye disease, or transient issues like dry eye. Less recognized, however, are nutritional deficiencies that impair visual function. Certain vitamins, minerals, and fatty acids are essential for retinal health, tear production, and optical clarity. Deficits in these nutrients can produce reversible symptoms if detected early, or contribute to long-term damage when prolonged.

Key Nutrients Linked to Blurry Vision

Several specific deficiencies are most commonly associated with blurred or compromised vision:

  • Vitamin A: Critical for formation of rhodopsin and maintenance of the ocular surface. Deficiency can cause night blindness, dry eyes (xerophthalmia), and corneal damage, which may manifest as blurring or reduced contrast sensitivity.
  • Omega-3 fatty acids (DHA/EPA): DHA is a major structural fatty acid in retinal membranes. Low omega-3 status is linked to dry eye and altered photoreceptor function, contributing to fluctuating or blurry vision.
  • B vitamins (B2, B12): Riboflavin (B2) supports ocular metabolism and tear production; B12 deficiency can produce optic neuropathy and visual disturbances, including blurring and color changes.
  • Antioxidants (vitamin C, vitamin E, lutein, zeaxanthin): These protect against oxidative stress in the lens and retina. Inadequate levels are associated with accelerated cataract formation and macular changes that reduce sharpness.
  • Zinc and magnesium: Zinc helps transport vitamin A to the retina; magnesium influences neuromuscular control of accommodation. Shortages can impair visual acuity and adaptation to changing light.
  • Vitamin D: Emerging evidence links low vitamin D with ocular surface disease and some retinal conditions, though mechanisms remain under investigation.

How Nutritional Causes Are Identified

Clinical evaluation begins with a comprehensive eye exam to rule out refractive error, intraocular pressure issues, or ocular pathology. If nutritional causes are suspected, blood tests can assess levels of vitamin A, B12, vitamin D, and relevant antioxidants, as well as markers of omega-3 status. A dietary history helps identify inadequate intake, restrictive diets, or malabsorption conditions that increase deficiency risk.

Dietary Sources and Management

Addressing deficiencies typically involves optimizing dietary intake and, when necessary, targeted supplementation under medical supervision. Good food sources include liver, eggs, and dairy for preformed vitamin A; leafy greens and orange vegetables for provitamin A carotenoids; fatty fish and algae for DHA/EPA; citrus fruits and peppers for vitamin C; nuts and seeds for vitamin E; and legumes, meat, and whole grains for zinc and B vitamins. Clinical formulas used in age-related eye disease studies combine antioxidants with zinc and carotenoids to support macular health.
