Introduction

Vitamin A is essential for vision, immune defense, epithelial integrity, and cellular differentiation. Deficiency can develop gradually and present with signs that range from mild visual changes to severe ocular and systemic effects. Early recognition supports timely evaluation and targeted nutritional strategies.

Common Clinical Signs

The most characteristic early symptom is night blindness (nyctalopia): difficulty adapting to low-light conditions due to impaired rod photoreceptor function. As deficiency progresses, ocular surface changes such as xerosis (conjunctival and corneal dryness) and Bitot’s spots — foamy, whitish patches on the conjunctiva — may appear. In severe cases, corneal ulcers or keratomalacia can cause irreversible vision loss.

Dermatologic manifestations include hyperkeratosis, where skin becomes dry, rough, and scaly. Because Vitamin A supports mucosal and epithelial barriers, deficiency also correlates with increased susceptibility to respiratory and gastrointestinal infections, and in children can contribute to growth faltering and fatigue.

When to Suspect Deficiency

Consider Vitamin A deficiency when multiple signs co-occur: night vision problems, unexplained persistent dry eyes, chronic skin dryness not responsive to topical therapy, and recurrent infections. High-risk groups include young children, pregnant or breastfeeding women, older adults, and people with malabsorption disorders or very restrictive diets.

Assessment and Diagnostic Tools

Diagnosis combines clinical evaluation, dietary history, and laboratory tests. The most widely used laboratory indicator is serum retinol; concentrations below 0.70 µmol/L (20 µg/dL) are generally considered deficient, but inflammation and infection can alter levels. Eye examinations (including slit-lamp assessment) detect surface changes and Bitot’s spots when present. A thorough dietary review helps identify chronic low intake of preformed Vitamin A (retinol) or provitamin A carotenoids.

Management Considerations

Addressing deficiency typically involves dietary optimization and, when indicated, supplementation under medical supervision. Preformed Vitamin A (retinol) is more bioavailable but carries a higher risk of toxicity at excessive doses; carotenoids like beta-carotene are converted to retinol according to physiological need and have a lower toxicity risk. Management plans should account for absorption issues (fat malabsorption, cholestatic liver disease) and potential interactions with other fat-soluble vitamins.

Because nutrient status and clinical context vary, follow-up testing and symptom monitoring are important. If supplementation is used, healthcare providers usually recommend specific dosing regimens and monitoring to avoid hypervitaminosis A.
