Why don't doctors recommend magnesium?

Magnesium is essential for hundreds of enzymatic reactions, yet clinicians often hesitate to recommend supplements routinely. Several practical and clinical reasons underlie this caution: diagnostic limitations, non-specific symptoms, variability in absorption, and the potential for adverse effects in certain populations.

Diagnostic challenges

Standard serum magnesium tests reflect only about 1% of total body stores and can appear normal even when intracellular or bone levels are low. Because routine blood panels rarely include magnesium, physicians must rely on clinical judgment and targeted testing (such as red blood cell magnesium or magnesium loading tests) to identify deficiency. This uncertainty reduces the likelihood of broad prescribing.

Non-specific symptoms and competing diagnoses

Symptoms of low magnesium—fatigue, muscle cramps, anxiety, sleep disturbances, and palpitations—overlap with many common conditions like thyroid disease, anemia, or mood disorders. Clinicians typically prioritize ruling out those conditions first, which can delay or obscure recognition of a nutritional cause.

Absorption and contributing factors

Even when intake seems adequate, absorption can be limited by gastrointestinal disorders (celiac disease, Crohn’s, chronic diarrhea), certain medications (proton pump inhibitors, diuretics), alcohol use, and age-related changes in gut function. Soil depletion and food processing have also reduced magnesium content in many staples. Risks and side effects

Oral magnesium supplements can cause gastrointestinal side effects—most commonly diarrhea—especially with certain forms like magnesium oxide or citrate. More serious risks include hypermagnesemia in people with impaired renal function, which can lead to hypotension, muscle weakness, and cardiac disturbances. Drug–nutrient interactions with diuretics, antibiotics, and other agents further complicate blanket recommendations.

Why diet-first approaches are common

Many providers prefer dietary strategies because food sources supply magnesium along with fiber, vitamins, and cofactors that support absorption and overall health. Foods rich in magnesium include leafy greens, legumes, nuts, seeds, whole grains, and fatty fish. Dietary approaches also avoid the dosage uncertainties and regulatory variability associated with over-the-counter products.

When supplementation is appropriate

Supplementation may be warranted for patients with confirmed deficiency, malabsorption syndromes, certain chronic illnesses, or ongoing medication use that increases losses. In those situations, individualized dosing under medical supervision—sometimes guided by specialized testing—reduces risk and improves outcomes. Summary

Doctors’ restraint in routinely recommending magnesium reflects a balance between uncertain diagnostics, symptom overlap, absorption variability, and safety concerns. A food-first approach and targeted testing for at-risk individuals tend to be preferred.
