Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble micronutrient required for multiple biochemical functions in humans. Because the body cannot synthesize it, vitamin C must be obtained from the diet or supplements. Its well-established roles include antioxidant protection, support for immune cells, and acting as an essential cofactor in collagen synthesis—functions that link it to skin health, wound healing, and general physiological resilience.

What vitamin C does

As an antioxidant, vitamin C neutralizes reactive oxygen species in the body’s aqueous compartments (blood plasma and intracellular fluids), helping to limit oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA. It also regenerates other antioxidants such as vitamin E, contributing to a broader defense network.

In immunity, vitamin C influences both innate and adaptive responses. It supports the function and migration of neutrophils and lymphocytes, helps maintain epithelial barrier integrity, and modulates inflammatory signaling. Clinical trials indicate that regular vitamin C intake can reduce the duration and severity of some respiratory infections, particularly in populations under high physical or environmental stress.

Collagen, tissue repair and skin

Vitamin C is a necessary cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases, enzymes required to stabilize collagen molecules. Adequate levels are therefore important for wound healing, connective tissue maintenance, and preserving skin structure. Topical vitamin C formulations can offer localized benefits for photoaging, while oral intake supplies systemic support for deeper dermal layers.

Disease prevention and practical considerations

Observational and interventional studies have linked sufficient vitamin C status with improved vascular function, reduced markers of oxidative stress, and lower inflammatory markers—factors relevant to chronic disease risk mitigation. However, vitamin C is not a cure for chronic illnesses; it is a supportive nutrient that can be part of a multifactorial prevention strategy that includes diet, physical activity, and medical oversight.

Dietary sources such as citrus fruits, berries, bell peppers, and leafy greens typically meet recommended intakes for most people. Supplement forms (ascorbic acid, sodium ascorbate, liposomal ascorbic acid) differ in absorption characteristics; liposomal and time-release preparations may improve circulating availability in some circumstances. Typical recommended dietary allowances are 75 mg/day for adult women and 90 mg/day for adult men, while supplemental doses commonly range higher depending on individual needs and tolerance. Doses above 2,000 mg/day can cause gastrointestinal side effects and should be avoided without professional guidance.

Conclusion

Vitamin C is an essential nutrient with multiple physiological roles including antioxidant defense, immune support, and collagen production. Adequate intake from diet and, when appropriate, supplements supports overall health, while safety and dosage decisions should be guided by clinical context and professional advice.
