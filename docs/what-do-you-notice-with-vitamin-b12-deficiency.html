What do you notice with a vitamin B12 deficiency?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, DNA synthesis, and nervous system function. Deficiency can develop slowly and present with a mix of physical, neurological, and cognitive signs. Early recognition helps guide testing and appropriate intervention to prevent long-term damage.

Common signs and symptoms

Physical symptoms often include persistent fatigue, pallor or mild jaundice, shortness of breath on exertion, lightheadedness, and a swollen or tender tongue. Because B12 is critical for erythropoiesis, deficiency frequently causes megaloblastic anemia, which may explain some of the fatigue and breathlessness.

Neurological and cognitive features can be more specific and concerning: paresthesia (pins and needles in hands and feet), balance and coordination problems, slowed cognitive processing, memory lapses, and mood changes such as depression or irritability. If untreated for a prolonged period, some neurological damage may become irreversible, so prompt assessment is important.

Why deficiency occurs

Causes fall into two broad categories: inadequate intake and impaired absorption. Strict vegetarian or vegan diets without fortified foods or supplements often lack sufficient B12. Absorption problems are common with conditions that impair intrinsic factor production (pernicious anemia), damage the ileum (Crohn’s disease, resection), or reduce gastric acid (atrophic gastritis or long-term proton pump inhibitor use). Certain medications such as metformin may also reduce B12 absorption.

How it is diagnosed

Diagnosis typically combines clinical assessment with laboratory testing. Serum B12 is a common first test, but borderline levels may require measurement of methylmalonic acid (MMA) or homocysteine to detect functional deficiency. Neurological examination and, when indicated, nerve conduction studies or cognitive testing can clarify the extent of neurologic involvement.

Treatment approaches

Treatment depends on cause and severity. Dietary correction and oral supplementation are effective for many people with dietary insufficiency. High-dose oral cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin can be sufficient when absorption is intact. For those with malabsorption or severe neurologic symptoms, intramuscular injections are often used initially. Addressing underlying conditions (for example, treating celiac disease or reviewing medications) is a key part of long-term management.
