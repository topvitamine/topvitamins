Who Should Take Multivitamins?

Multivitamins are concentrated combinations of vitamins and minerals designed to support daily nutrient needs. In populations where diets are inconsistent or restricted, these supplements can help reduce the risk of deficiency and support processes such as immune function, energy metabolism, and bone health. However, whether an individual should take a multivitamin depends on personal diet, life stage, medical conditions, and lab-confirmed deficiencies.

People most likely to benefit include older adults, pregnant people, strict vegetarians or vegans, and those with limited access to a varied diet. Older adults often experience decreased absorption of nutrients like vitamin B12 and vitamin D, while pregnant people require increased folate and iron for fetal development. Vegetarians and vegans may lack reliable dietary sources of B12 and iron, which makes targeted supplementation useful for maintaining normal physiological function.

Multivitamins can also serve as a practical daily nutritional boost for busy lifestyles. When meal quality varies because of work demands, travel, or other constraints, a moderate-dose multivitamin can help fill small gaps in micronutrient intake—particularly B-vitamins for energy metabolism and minerals like magnesium for muscle and nervous system support.

For immune support, nutrients such as vitamins C, D, zinc, and selenium play complementary roles in maintaining barrier defenses and immune cell function. Individuals at higher risk of infection or those with documented low vitamin D status may find a multivitamin that includes these nutrients beneficial. Correcting diagnosed deficiencies typically involves targeted supplementation guided by laboratory tests. Blood measures such as 25-hydroxyvitamin D, ferritin, and serum B12 (or methylmalonic acid when indicated) help clinicians tailor dose and form. For practical advice on evaluating supplement quality, see an assessment of supplement sourcing and testing in the article on assessing supplement quality.

When selecting a multivitamin, prefer transparent labeling, bioavailable nutrient forms (for example, methylcobalamin for B12 or methylated folate), and doses aligned with established recommendations. Avoid high-dose single nutrients unless specifically prescribed, since excess intake—particularly of fat-soluble vitamins or certain minerals—can create adverse effects.

In summary, multivitamins are not a universal requirement but a useful tool when dietary intake is insufficient, when increased physiological needs arise, or when specific deficiencies are diagnosed. Decisions should be evidence-based, individualized, and made in partnership with healthcare professionals to ensure safety and efficacy.
