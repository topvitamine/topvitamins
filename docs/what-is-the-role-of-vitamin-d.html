Vitamin D is a fat-soluble nutrient central to multiple physiological processes, commonly called the “sunshine vitamin” because skin synthesis is triggered by UVB exposure. Its primary and best-established role is promoting calcium and phosphorus absorption to support skeletal development and maintenance. Beyond bone health, accumulating evidence links vitamin D status to immune regulation, muscle function, and the modulation of inflammatory pathways. Synthesis, Metabolism and Mechanism

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is produced in the skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol after UVB exposure; it is then hydroxylated in the liver to 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D), the major circulating form measured in clinical practice. The kidney converts 25(OH)D to the active hormone 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), which binds to vitamin D receptors (VDRs) on target cells and modulates gene expression. This endocrine and paracrine signaling underlies effects on intestinal calcium transporters, immune cells, and muscle tissue.

Bone Health and Calcium Absorption

By upregulating calcium-binding proteins in the small intestine, active vitamin D increases dietary calcium uptake, supporting bone mineralization and reducing fracture risk. Inadequate vitamin D impairs calcium absorption, which can lead to rickets in children and osteomalacia or contribute to osteoporosis in adults. Clinical guidance often pairs vitamin D assessment with calcium intake optimization and, when appropriate, supplementation tailored to laboratory values and individual risk factors.

Immune Function and Clinical Observations

Immune cells express VDRs and can locally convert 25(OH)D to calcitriol, enabling autocrine and paracrine immunomodulatory effects. Observational studies and some randomized trials suggest that maintaining sufficient vitamin D status is associated with fewer respiratory infections and modulated inflammatory responses. While research is ongoing, maintaining recommended serum levels is a reasonable component of immune health strategies.

Recognizing Deficiency and When to Test

Signs of deficiency are often nonspecific and can include fatigue, muscle weakness, bone pain, and increased susceptibility to infections. Populations at higher risk include older adults, people with limited sun exposure, individuals with darker skin pigmentation, and those with malabsorption disorders. Serum 25(OH)D measurement is the standard test; levels below 20 ng/mL are generally considered deficient, while many clinicians aim for 30–50 ng/mL depending on comorbidities.

Sunlight, Diet and Supplementation Considerations

Sun exposure is the most efficient source of vitamin D, but geographic location, season, sunscreen use, and lifestyle can limit endogenous production. Dietary sources (fatty fish, fortified foods) contribute but are often insufficient alone. Supplementation provides a controlled route to maintain levels; common adult dosing recommendations range from 600 to 2000 IU daily depending on individual needs and clinical advice. Periodic monitoring helps avoid under- or overdosing.
