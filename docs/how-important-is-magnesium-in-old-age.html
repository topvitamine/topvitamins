How important is magnesium in old age?

Magnesium is a vital mineral involved in more than 300 enzymatic reactions, including energy production, muscle and nerve function, and bone maintenance. In older adults, physiological changes—reduced dietary intake, diminished intestinal absorption, and increased urinary losses—make maintaining adequate magnesium status more challenging. Understanding these dynamics helps guide dietary choices, clinical monitoring, and, where appropriate, supplementation.

Clinical and population studies indicate that many individuals over 60 do not meet recommended magnesium intakes. Subclinical deficiency can present with nonspecific signs such as fatigue, muscle cramps, irregular heartbeat, and cognitive changes, which frequently overlap with common conditions of aging. Because symptoms are subtle and serum magnesium is a limited marker of total body stores, a careful clinical assessment and, when indicated, follow-up testing are useful.

Magnesium supports several systems that are particularly relevant to healthy aging. Cardiovascular benefits include contribution to normal heart rhythm and blood pressure regulation. Skeletal benefits stem from magnesium’s role in bone mineralization and in activating vitamin D, which in turn influences calcium metabolism. Neurologically, magnesium affects neurotransmitter balance and synaptic plasticity, factors that can influence sleep, mood, and cognitive resilience in later life.

Absorption efficiency declines with age because of reduced gastric acidity and alterations in gut health. Common medications prescribed to older adults—such as diuretics and proton pump inhibitors—can further deplete magnesium. Dietary limitations related to chewing difficulties, loss of appetite, or restricted food choices also reduce intake of magnesium-rich foods like green leafy vegetables, nuts, seeds, legumes, and whole grains.

Practical strategies to support magnesium status include emphasizing magnesium-rich whole foods, optimizing meal composition (including healthy fats that aid mineral absorption), ensuring adequate hydration, and addressing gut health. For clinicians and caregivers, an individualized approach is recommended: assess dietary patterns and medications, consider targeted laboratory testing when deficiency is suspected, and review the risk–benefit profile of supplemental magnesium when dietary measures are insufficient.

When supplementation is considered, different magnesium salts vary in bioavailability and tolerability. Forms such as magnesium citrate, glycinate, and malate are commonly chosen for their absorption characteristics and digestive comfort.
