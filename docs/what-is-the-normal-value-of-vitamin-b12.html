What Is the Normal Value of Vitamin B12?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation and nervous system function. Clinicians typically measure total serum B12 to screen for deficiency, but interpretation requires context. Reference ranges vary by laboratory and assay, and single serum measurements may not fully capture functional status.

Common reference ranges and clinical context

Most laboratories report serum vitamin B12 in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL) or picomoles per liter (pmol/L). A commonly used categorization is: deficient <200 pg/mL (<150 pmol/L), borderline 200–300 pg/mL (150–220 pmol/L), and adequate >300 pg/mL (>220 pmol/L). Functional markers: MMA and homocysteine

Because serum B12 includes inactive binding forms, clinicians often measure methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine when results are borderline or symptoms suggest deficiency. Elevated MMA is a sensitive indicator of inadequate active B12 at the cellular level. Homocysteine rises in deficiency of B12, folate or B6 and can provide complementary information.

Symptoms and at-risk groups

Early signs of low B12 can be non-specific: fatigue, cognitive slowing, paresthesia, and mood changes. Prolonged deficiency may cause megaloblastic anemia and irreversible neurologic damage. Risk factors include older age, strict vegetarian or vegan diets, pernicious anemia, gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., celiac disease, Crohn’s), bariatric surgery, and long-term use of metformin or proton pump inhibitors.

Forms of B12 and absorption considerations

Dietary B12 is found in animal-source foods; those avoiding these foods should rely on fortified products or supplements. Common supplemental forms include cyanocobalamin, methylcobalamin, adenosylcobalamin and hydroxocobalamin. Absorption depends on intrinsic factor and gastric acidity; alternative delivery routes (sublingual, intranasal, intramuscular) may be used when oral absorption is impaired. Interpreting high-normal or elevated values

High serum B12 without supplementation can sometimes reflect liver disease, myeloproliferative disorders or other conditions and merits clinical evaluation. Conversely, high values from supplementation are usually cleared by the kidneys but should be interpreted in the full clinical context.

Practical monitoring and lifestyle notes

Monitoring frequency depends on risk: annual checks may suffice for most adults, while higher-risk individuals require more frequent follow-up. Dietitians and clinicians consider symptoms, dietary history and functional markers together.
