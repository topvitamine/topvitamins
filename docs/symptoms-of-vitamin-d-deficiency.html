What are the symptoms of a vitamin D deficiency?

Vitamin D is essential for calcium and phosphorus balance, immune regulation, muscle function, and aspects of brain health. Because the body primarily produces vitamin D after sun exposure, limited sunlight, certain medical conditions, darker skin pigmentation, age, and some medications can contribute to low levels. Recognizing common clinical signs can prompt laboratory testing (serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D) and appropriate management.

Common physical signs

Bone pain, tenderness, or a deep aching sensation—often around the shins, hips, ribs, and spine—can signal inadequate vitamin D. In adults, chronic deficiency may lead to osteomalacia (poor bone mineralization) and contribute to osteoporosis-related fracture risk in older adults. Children with severe deficiency can develop rickets, characterized by delayed growth and bone deformities.

Muscular symptoms include weakness, heaviness in the limbs, difficulty climbing stairs, and nocturnal cramps. Vitamin D receptors are present in muscle tissue, and deficiency can impair calcium handling in muscle cells, reducing strength and coordination. Fatigue that persists despite adequate sleep is a frequent, nonspecific complaint associated with low vitamin D and merits evaluation when accompanied by other signs.

Immune and infection-related findings

Vitamin D influences innate and adaptive immunity. Observational studies have linked low vitamin D levels with increased rates of upper respiratory tract infections. A 2017 systematic review reported that supplementation reduced the risk of acute respiratory infections, particularly among individuals with baseline deficiency. Recurrent infections, slow wound healing, or prolonged recovery from illness may therefore prompt consideration of vitamin D status in a broader clinical assessment.

Mood and cognitive effects

Research shows vitamin D receptors are active in brain regions involved in mood and cognition. Associations have been observed between low vitamin D and symptoms such as low mood, seasonal affective symptoms, poor concentration, and subjective "brain fog." Randomized trials suggest potential benefits of supplementation for mood in people with deficiency, though results vary and should be interpreted alongside other psychological and social factors.

Diagnosis and safe correction

Definitive assessment requires a blood test measuring 25(OH)D. When deficiency is confirmed, clinicians may recommend supplementation, often preferring vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) for its greater potency in raising serum levels compared with D2. Co-nutrients such as magnesium and vitamin K support vitamin D metabolism and calcium utilization. Because vitamin D is fat-soluble, excessive dosing can cause toxicity; monitoring and individualized dosing are important.
