Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, DNA synthesis, and nervous system maintenance. Older adults are at increased risk of deficiency due to reduced stomach acid, medication use, gastrointestinal disorders, and dietary changes. Early recognition of symptoms can prevent progression to severe neurological damage and anemia.

Common symptoms to watch for

Symptoms of B12 deficiency in seniors often develop gradually and can be mistaken for normal aging. Typical findings include persistent fatigue and weakness, pallor or shortness of breath on exertion, numbness or tingling in the hands and feet (paresthesia), balance problems, and unsteady gait. Cognitive changes — such as memory lapses, slowed thinking, confusion, and mood disturbances — are also common and may mimic or worsen age-related cognitive decline.

Neurological and cognitive signs

B12 is crucial for myelin synthesis; prolonged deficiency can cause demyelination leading to neuropathy, impaired coordination, and increased fall risk. Cognitive effects range from mild memory difficulties to more pronounced executive dysfunction. Early supplementation can reverse some deficits, but neurological damage may become irreversible if deficiency persists for many months.

Hematological and gastrointestinal features

Because B12 is necessary for healthy red blood cell production, many older adults present with macrocytic anemia, fatigue, and reduced exercise tolerance. Gastrointestinal symptoms such as loss of appetite, weight loss, glossitis (inflamed tongue), constipation, or diarrhea may also occur. Reduced gastric acid, long-term acid suppression therapy, and surgical alterations of the stomach or intestines can impair B12 absorption.

Causes and diagnostic considerations

Pernicious anemia, an autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor, is a notable cause among seniors and requires different management than simple dietary deficiency. Laboratory testing typically includes serum B12, complete blood count, and, when needed, methylmalonic acid or homocysteine to detect early functional deficiency. Intrinsic factor antibody testing helps diagnose pernicious anemia.

Management approaches

Management depends on cause and severity. Oral high-dose B12 can be effective through passive absorption for many patients, while intramuscular injections are indicated when absorption is impaired (for example, pernicious anemia). Methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin are forms often considered for neurological support. Regular monitoring of levels and symptoms is important to guide ongoing dosing.
