What to Eat for Vitamin A Deficiency

Vitamin A is essential for vision, immune function, and epithelial health. When levels are low, early signs include night blindness and dry eyes, and prolonged deficiency can lead to more serious ocular and systemic problems. Addressing a deficiency focuses on supplying bioavailable retinol from animal sources and provitamin A carotenoids from plants, while considering absorption and safety.

For rapid repletion of vitamin A stores, retinol-rich animal foods are most effective because the body uses preformed vitamin A directly. Liver (beef, chicken, or lamb) contains the highest concentrations; a small serving can correct deficiency quickly but should be consumed sparingly to avoid excess. Egg yolks, full-fat dairy, and certain fatty fish also provide usable retinol and are appropriate regular choices when balanced within a varied diet.

Plant-based precursors, primarily beta-carotene, offer a safer long-term strategy. Brightly colored vegetables and fruits—such as sweet potatoes, carrots, pumpkin, butternut squash, red bell peppers, mango, and cantaloupe—are rich in carotenoids that the body converts to vitamin A as needed. Dark leafy greens like spinach and kale supply beta-carotene plus lutein and zeaxanthin, carotenoids that support retinal health independent of vitamin A conversion.

Maximizing absorption is important for both retinol and beta-carotene. Because vitamin A is fat-soluble, consume these foods with a small amount of dietary fat (olive oil, avocado, nuts, or full-fat dairy). Cooking certain vegetables—roasting, steaming, or lightly sautéing—breaks down cell walls and improves carotenoid bioavailability. For individuals with malabsorption syndromes or liver disease, medical evaluation is necessary because dietary strategies alone may not restore adequate levels.

Eye-specific nutrition benefits from combining sources. Pairing beta-carotene–rich vegetables with eggs or a modest oil-based dressing supports conversion and uptake. Including foods that supply zinc, omega-3 fatty acids, and antioxidants (for example, fatty fish for DHA, legumes or meat for zinc, and colorful produce for antioxidants) complements vitamin A’s role in retinal metabolism and epithelial integrity.

Supplements can play a role when dietary intake is insufficient or when rapid repletion is clinically indicated. Retinol-containing supplements must be used cautiously due to the risk of hypervitaminosis A, especially in pregnancy. Beta-carotene supplements avoid retinol toxicity but have been linked to adverse outcomes in specific groups (notably smokers), so food-based carotenoids are usually preferable. Tailor approaches to individual risk factors—pregnancy, smoking status, malabsorption, and chronic disease—under clinical supervision to optimize outcomes.
