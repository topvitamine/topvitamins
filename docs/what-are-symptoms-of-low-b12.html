What are the symptoms of low B12?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell production, DNA synthesis, and neurological function. When levels fall below recommended ranges, symptoms can be gradual and varied, affecting hematologic, neurologic, cognitive, and psychiatric domains. This concise, evidence-informed summary describes common and important signs of B12 deficiency, possible causes, and approaches to evaluation.

Common signs and early symptoms

Early manifestations are often nonspecific. Fatigue and generalized weakness are among the most frequently reported complaints and relate to impaired red blood cell formation and reduced oxygen delivery. Patients may also notice pallor or, less commonly, jaundice due to increased red cell breakdown. Shortness of breath on exertion and lightheadedness can accompany the anemia.

Neurological and sensory symptoms

B12 deficiency can affect peripheral nerves and the central nervous system. Typical neurologic features include numbness, tingling, or burning sensations in the hands and feet (peripheral neuropathy), gait instability, and impaired coordination. Cognitive changes such as memory lapses, slowed thinking, concentration difficulties, and a subjective “brain fog” are also described. In severe or prolonged deficiency, mood disturbances (depression, irritability) and visual disturbances from optic nerve involvement may occur.

Mouth, gastrointestinal, and other signs

Glossitis (a swollen, smooth, or sore tongue) and recurrent mouth ulcers are relatively characteristic mucosal findings. Some individuals report reduced appetite, weight loss, or gastrointestinal discomfort if an underlying malabsorptive condition exists. Because B12 is involved in homocysteine metabolism, elevated homocysteine may be observed and has been associated with cardiovascular risk in some studies.

Causes and risk groups

Deficiency arises from inadequate intake (e.g., strict vegan diets), malabsorption (pernicious anemia, atrophic gastritis, bariatric surgery, inflammatory bowel disease), and certain medications (long-term proton pump inhibitors, metformin). Older adults are at increased risk due to reduced gastric acid production. Understanding these causes helps determine whether oral supplementation or parenteral therapy and further investigation are appropriate. Evaluation and treatment considerations

Diagnosis typically relies on serum B12 concentration supported by functional markers such as methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine when results are equivocal. A complete blood count may show macrocytic (megaloblastic) anemia, but neurologic symptoms can occur without anemia. Treatment options include high-dose oral B12, sublingual formulations, or intramuscular injection depending on etiology and absorption; methylcobalamin is a commonly used bioavailable form. When to seek testing

If persistent fatigue, new numbness or tingling, unexplained cognitive changes, or oral symptoms appear, laboratory testing is appropriate to exclude deficiency. For more on identifying patterns of deficiency and testing strategies, consult materials on how to identify vitamin deficiencies.
