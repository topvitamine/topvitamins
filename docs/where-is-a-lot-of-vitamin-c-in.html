Where is a lot of vitamin C in?

Common Foods and Supplements That Deliver Vitamin C

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble vitamin essential for collagen synthesis, antioxidant protection, iron absorption, and immune function. Because humans cannot synthesize it, regular dietary intake is required. Many whole foods provide ample vitamin C, and targeted nutritional supplements can help fill gaps when dietary intake is inadequate.

Citrus fruits are classic sources: a medium orange typically supplies around 70 mg of vitamin C, while grapefruit, lemons and tangerines also contribute meaningful amounts. However, several non-citrus foods contain equal or higher concentrations.

Vegetables with high vitamin C

Bell peppers are among the richest vegetable sources. One cup of chopped red bell pepper can provide over 150 mg of vitamin C—more than twice what a medium orange offers. Broccoli is another strong contributor; a cup of raw chopped broccoli supplies roughly 80 mg. Light steaming preserves vitamin C better than prolonged boiling, and it can improve bioavailability for some people.

Fruits with concentrated vitamin C

Kiwi is notable for its high ascorbic acid content: a medium kiwi often provides about 70 mg, rivalling or exceeding many citrus fruits. Strawberries are also important sources; one cup of halved strawberries supplies close to 90 mg. These fruits also supply fiber, flavonoids and other antioxidants that complement vitamin C’s effects.

When fresh produce is limited, dietary supplements offer a predictable dose. For practical information on supplement choices and formulations, including buffered, timed-release and liposomal options, see the overview on vitamin C supplements.

How food and supplements compare

Whole foods provide vitamin C alongside fiber, phytonutrients and micronutrients. For example, bell peppers add carotenoids and broccoli contributes glucosinolates. Supplements can be useful for consistent intake—particularly during illness, periods of increased need, or when dietary variety is limited—but they do not replicate the full nutrient matrix of whole foods.

Practical tips

Consume a mix of colorful fruits and vegetables daily: red peppers, broccoli, kiwi and strawberries are efficient ways to meet or exceed typical recommended intakes. Use minimal water and shorter cooking times to preserve vitamin C. When choosing supplements, consider formulation and dose relative to individual needs and consult a healthcare professional if you have conditions that affect absorption or increase requirements.

Overall, balancing diverse whole foods with informed supplement choices provides a reliable strategy to maintain adequate vitamin C levels and support general health.
