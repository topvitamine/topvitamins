Where is vitamin D found?

Introduction

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble nutrient central to bone health, calcium balance, immune function, and other physiological processes. Natural synthesis in the skin from UVB radiation is the primary source for many people, but diet, fortified foods, and supplements are important contributors — especially where sunlight is limited. This article summarizes the main sources of vitamin D and factors that influence availability.

Sunlight and skin synthesis

Exposure to ultraviolet B (UVB) light triggers conversion of 7-dehydrocholesterol in the epidermis to previtamin D3, which the body converts to vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol). Effective synthesis depends on latitude, season, time of day, skin pigmentation, age, and sunscreen use. Individuals at higher latitudes or with darker skin tones typically require longer exposure to generate comparable amounts of vitamin D. Because excessive exposure increases skin cancer risk, balancing brief unprotected exposure with sun protection is recommended, and relying solely on sunlight may be inadequate year-round.

Natural food sources

Only a limited number of foods contain meaningful amounts of vitamin D naturally. Fatty fish such as salmon, mackerel, sardines, and tuna are among the richest sources of vitamin D3. Egg yolks and certain organ meats also contain vitamin D, albeit at lower levels. Some mushrooms produce vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) when exposed to UV light — a relevant plant-based option, though D2 is generally less effective than D3 at raising serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentrations.

Fortified foods

Food fortification is a public-health strategy to increase population vitamin D intake. Commonly fortified items include milk, plant-based milk alternatives, breakfast cereals, and some yogurts and juices. Levels vary by country and product, so checking food labels helps estimate contribution to daily intake. Fortified foods can meaningfully complement dietary sources, particularly where natural dietary intake is low.

Supplementation

Supplemental vitamin D is an effective and reliable option to maintain adequate status, particularly during seasons or in regions with limited UVB availability. Vitamin D3 supplements are typically preferred for their greater potency and longer maintenance of serum levels compared with D2. Recommended intakes vary by age, health status, and local guidelines; clinicians often advise blood testing (serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D) to tailor dosing. Caution is warranted to avoid chronic excessive intake — many authorities set a tolerable upper intake around 4,000 IU/day for most adults.

Environmental and lifestyle influences

Urban living, air pollution, clothing choices, time spent indoors, and even glass windows (which block UVB) reduce cutaneous vitamin D production. Seasonal deficits are common in higher latitudes. Adapting strategies — combining sensible sun exposure, vitamin D–rich foods, fortified items, and supplements when appropriate — helps maintain sufficient levels across diverse settings.
