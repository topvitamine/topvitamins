Where to find vitamins K, B9, and B12?

Vitamins K, B9 (folate), and B12 (cobalamin) are essential micronutrients with distinct physiological roles: K supports blood clotting and bone metabolism, folate is critical for DNA synthesis and fetal development, and B12 is necessary for nerve function and red blood cell formation. This article summarizes reliable dietary sources, practical absorption tips, and when supplementation may be appropriate.

Primary dietary sources

Vitamin K exists mainly as K1 (phylloquinone) in leafy green vegetables and K2 (menaquinones) in fermented and animal-derived foods. Good K1 sources include kale, spinach, collard greens, and broccoli. Fermented foods such as natto and certain cheeses provide K2 forms that may be more bioactive for bone and vascular health.

Folate (vitamin B9) is abundant in legumes, leafy greens, and some fruits. Lentils, spinach, asparagus, and oranges are consistently cited in dietary surveys as folate-rich. Many countries also fortify staple grains with folic acid (the synthetic form), which helps raise population intakes and reduce neural tube defects.

Vitamin B12 is predominantly found in animal products: meat (especially liver), fish, shellfish (clams, sardines), eggs, and dairy. Plant-based eaters should rely on fortified foods (fortified plant milks, nutritional yeast, and cereals) or consider supplementation, since natural plant sources do not reliably provide active B12.

Absorption and food combinations

Bioavailability differs by nutrient and food matrix. Vitamin K is fat‑soluble, so consuming leafy greens with a source of dietary fat (olive oil, avocado, nuts) improves absorption. Folate from foods is better absorbed when meals include vitamin C–rich items, which help maintain folate stability. B12 absorption depends on intrinsic factor produced in the stomach and on gastric acidity; older adults and people with certain gastrointestinal conditions may have impaired B12 uptake.

When to consider supplementation

Supplements can be helpful when dietary intake is inadequate or absorption is compromised. Examples include pregnancy (increased folate needs), strict vegan diets (B12 risk), and malabsorption syndromes or medications that affect gastric acid. Clinical decisions should be guided by blood tests (serum folate, serum B12, methylmalonic acid, or homocysteine when indicated) and by qualified healthcare professionals.

Practical tips

  • Include a cup of cooked leafy greens daily for K1 and folate.
  • Use small amounts of healthy fats in salads or sautés to enhance vitamin K uptake.
  • Vegans should check labels for fortified B12 in cereals, plant milks, and nutritional yeast.
  • Individuals aged 60+ or with gastrointestinal disorders should monitor B12 status and discuss supplementation options with a clinician.
