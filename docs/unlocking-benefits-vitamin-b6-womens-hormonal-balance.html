Unlocking the Hidden Benefits of Vitamin B6 for Women's Hormonal Balance

Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) plays several biochemical roles that are relevant to women's hormonal health. It is a water-soluble B vitamin involved in amino acid metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, and the regulation of steroid and thyroid-related pathways. Because these processes intersect with mood, menstrual function, and energy metabolism, a modest increase in B6 intake—through diet or supplementation—has been investigated as a potential support for hormonal balance.

How Vitamin B6 interacts with hormonal systems

Biochemically, Vitamin B6 serves as a coenzyme for enzymes that synthesize serotonin, dopamine and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). These neurotransmitters influence the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, which in turn regulates reproductive hormones. B6 also participates in one-carbon metabolism and estrogen catabolism, pathways that can affect estrogen balance when nutrient status is suboptimal.

Evidence for PMS and mood support

Clinical studies indicate that B6 supplementation can reduce some premenstrual symptoms, particularly mood-related complaints such as irritability and depressive symptoms. Systematic reviews and randomized trials have reported benefit at supplemental doses commonly ranging from 25 to 100 mg per day, though effect sizes vary and study designs are heterogeneous. Improvements are plausibly linked to enhanced serotonin synthesis and modulation of prolactin production.

Stress, energy metabolism and thyroid considerations

Chronic stress perturbs cortisol and can indirectly disrupt reproductive hormones; B6’s role in neurotransmitter production may support stress resilience and emotional regulation. Additionally, B6 is involved in macronutrient metabolism—helping convert carbohydrates, fats and proteins into usable energy—which supports overall vitality. While B6 does not directly treat thyroid disease, adequate status supports metabolic pathways that are relevant to thyroid hormone action.

Practical guidance and safety

Dietary sources of B6 include poultry, fish, potatoes, bananas and fortified cereals. For individuals considering supplements, the active coenzyme form pyridoxal-5-phosphate (P5P) may offer improved bioavailability. Typical supplemental ranges used in studies are 25–100 mg/day; however, long-term intakes above 100–200 mg/day have been associated with sensory neuropathy in rare cases. Consultation with a healthcare professional is recommended before starting supplementation.
