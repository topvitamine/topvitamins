Introduction

Magnesium is essential for hundreds of biochemical reactions, including nerve and muscle function, energy metabolism, and bone maintenance. Many people can raise magnesium intake effectively through whole foods. Vegetables — particularly leafy greens, legumes, and certain seeds often consumed with vegetable dishes — are reliable plant-based sources of this mineral.

Top magnesium-rich vegetables

Among commonly eaten vegetables, cooked spinach is one of the richest sources of magnesium, with roughly 150–160 mg per cooked cup. Swiss chard and beet greens provide comparable levels per serving, while kale and collard greens deliver modest amounts that add up across meals. Other vegetables with meaningful magnesium content include artichokes (about 70–80 mg per medium boiled artichoke), green peas (around 60 mg per cup), broccoli, and potatoes when eaten with the skin.

Seeds often paired with vegetable dishes, for example pumpkin seeds, are exceptionally dense in magnesium (well over 100 mg per ounce) and can dramatically boost a meal’s total mineral content.

Bioavailability and cooking considerations

Plant magnesium bioavailability can be affected by antinutrients such as oxalates and phytates. Leafy greens like spinach contain oxalates that bind minerals, which can reduce absorption. Light cooking methods such as steaming or sautéing typically lower oxalate levels and can improve mineral availability compared with raw consumption. Avoid prolonged boiling that leaches minerals into cooking water unless that water is consumed (for example, in soups).

Practical meal ideas and daily intake

Adults generally require between 310–420 mg of magnesium per day depending on age and sex. A diet that includes multiple servings of magnesium-rich vegetables along with seeds, legumes, whole grains, and nuts can meet these targets. Example combinations: spinach or Swiss chard in morning smoothies, a lunch salad with kale and pumpkin seeds, and a dinner featuring steamed artichokes, peas, or broccoli.

Evidence-based perspective

Population studies link higher dietary magnesium with improved cardiovascular outcomes, better blood pressure control, and support for bone health when consumed alongside calcium and vitamin D. For absorption and overall nutrient balance, varied whole-food sources are generally preferable to relying exclusively on supplements. Summary

Spinach and other leafy greens are among the highest-magnesium vegetables, with artichokes, peas, and certain seeds also contributing substantially. Light cooking, dietary variety, and pairing vegetables with magnesium-dense seeds and legumes improve overall intake and bioavailability.
