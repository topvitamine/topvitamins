What Medications Does Vitamin C Interfere With?

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is widely used for immune support and as an antioxidant. While generally safe at recommended intakes, vitamin C can alter the absorption, metabolism, or effectiveness of certain medications and supplements. Understanding these interactions helps patients and clinicians minimize unintended effects.

Common interaction categories

Interactions of clinical relevance include anticoagulants and blood thinners, some chemotherapy agents, statins, iron supplements, and a range of oral medications whose absorption depends on gastric pH or specific transport mechanisms. A concise overview of documented and theoretical interactions is available in the article what medications does vitamin C interfere with.

Anticoagulants and blood thinners

Case reports and limited studies suggest high doses of vitamin C may influence warfarin therapy by altering vitamin K metabolism or changing liver enzyme activity, potentially causing INR fluctuations. Doses above 1,000 mg/day have been implicated more often than lower intakes. Patients on anticoagulants should inform their clinician before initiating high-dose vitamin C and maintain regular coagulation monitoring.

Cancer treatments

Chemotherapy often relies on oxidative mechanisms to kill cancer cells. Because vitamin C is an antioxidant, high systemic concentrations—particularly from intravenous administration—could theoretically reduce chemotherapy efficacy for agents that generate reactive oxygen species. Evidence is mixed and depends on dose, route, and timing; therefore, oncology teams should be consulted before patients take supplemental vitamin C during active treatment.

Statins and liver metabolism

Vitamin C may have modest effects on oxidative stress and inflammatory markers associated with statin therapy. Interactions through liver enzymes are possible but generally not strong; typical dietary or low supplemental doses are unlikely to significantly impair statin efficacy. Still, clinicians should consider overall antioxidant use when assessing lipid management strategies.

Iron and mineral supplements

Vitamin C enhances non-heme iron absorption by reducing ferric to ferrous iron, improving oral iron therapy efficacy. This effect is beneficial for iron-deficient patients but undesirable in iron-overload conditions such as hemochromatosis. High vitamin C can also alter stomach acidity and influence the solubility of other minerals, so timing and dose matter.

Other interactions and practical guidance

Vitamin C can affect the absorption or stability of certain antibiotics, oral contraceptives, and NSAIDs. Simple measures reduce risk: separate doses of medication and supplements by one to two hours, avoid chronic megadoses without supervision, and disclose all supplement use to prescribing clinicians.
