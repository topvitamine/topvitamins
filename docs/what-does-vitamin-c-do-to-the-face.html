Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) supports multiple physiological processes that influence facial skin health. As a water-soluble antioxidant and an essential cofactor in collagen synthesis, dietary vitamin C contributes to skin brightness, structural integrity, and protection against environmental stressors. This article summarizes evidence-based effects of oral vitamin C on the face and practical considerations for supplementation. For a full guide, see What does vitamin C do to the face.

Nutritional role and systemic support

Humans cannot synthesize vitamin C and must obtain it from diet or supplements. It neutralizes reactive oxygen species that damage cellular components and helps regenerate other antioxidants. Regulatory bodies such as EFSA set daily intake recommendations around 80 mg for adults, although some clinical contexts may justify higher doses under professional supervision. Oral intake provides systemic distribution to all skin layers, complementing topical formulations that act locally.

Skin brightening and pigment regulation

Vitamin C interferes with tyrosinase activity, a key enzyme in melanin synthesis, which can reduce emergence of new hyperpigmentation and help fade existing dark spots over time. Improved microcirculation and enhanced cellular turnover associated with adequate vitamin C intake can also result in a clearer, more even appearance. These effects develop gradually and are best observed with consistent intake over weeks to months.

Collagen synthesis and structural benefits

As a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases, vitamin C stabilizes and cross-links collagen fibers, which supports dermal firmness and elasticity. Dietary vitamin C also helps limit activation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that break down collagen in response to UV and pollution. Clinical studies have reported improvements in skin elasticity and texture with sustained higher dietary or supplemental vitamin C.

Antioxidant protection and anti-aging mechanisms

Environmental factors—UV radiation, airborne pollutants, and lifestyle-related oxidative stress—generate free radicals that accelerate visible aging. Vitamin C acts in aqueous environments throughout the body to scavenge these radicals, reduce inflammation, and support barrier function. Maintaining adequate systemic antioxidant levels is associated with fewer photoaging signs and improved skin tone, especially when combined with sun protection and other nutrients.

Forms, absorption, and practical guidance

Bioavailability varies by form: liposomal vitamin C, sodium ascorbate, or food-derived sources (e.g., acerola) may have different absorption profiles and tolerability compared with plain ascorbic acid. Gastrointestinal side effects can occur at high oral doses, so individualized dosing and professional advice are prudent. Oral supplementation complements topical application rather than replacing it.
