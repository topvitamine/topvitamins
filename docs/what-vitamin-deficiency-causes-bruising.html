Quick overview: Several nutrient deficiencies can increase susceptibility to bruising, most notably vitamin K, iron, and vitamin C. Understanding the role each plays in vessel integrity and clotting helps distinguish nutritional causes from primary bleeding disorders.

How nutrients affect bruising

Bruising follows blood leaking from damaged capillaries into surrounding tissues. Efficient clot formation, healthy vessel walls and resilient connective tissue all limit this leakage. Vitamin K is central to activating clotting factors; iron supports red blood cell production and platelet function; vitamin C is required for collagen synthesis that strengthens vessel walls. Deficits in any of these areas can make bruising more frequent or slow healing.

Vitamin K: clotting and bruising

Vitamin K deficiency directly impairs the clotting cascade, so even minor trauma may produce larger or longer-lasting bruises. Causes include poor dietary intake, malabsorption syndromes, prolonged antibiotic use that disrupts gut bacteria, and newborn physiology. If vitamin K deficiency is suspected, prothrombin time (PT) is a key laboratory test.

Iron and vascular resilience

Iron deficiency anemia reduces the number and function of red blood cells and can be associated with altered platelet activity. People with iron deficiency may notice more frequent bruising, pallor, fatigue or delayed wound healing. Assessment typically includes hemoglobin, ferritin and serum iron measurements.

Vitamin C and connective tissue

Vitamin C is essential for hydroxylation of collagen, a protein that gives blood vessels structural strength. Severe deficiency (scurvy) causes fragile capillaries and spontaneous bruising or gum bleeding. Even subclinical low intakes can impair wound repair and vessel integrity over time.

Other contributors

Zinc, vitamin D, vitamin E and omega-3 fatty acids can influence skin health, inflammation and coagulation. For example, high doses of omega-3s may modestly increase bleeding tendency. Medications, inherited bleeding disorders and platelet abnormalities remain important differential diagnoses and should be ruled out when bruising is unexplained.

Practical approach

Evaluation generally starts with a clinical history, medication review and basic laboratory tests such as a complete blood count (CBC), PT/INR and aPTT, ferritin and selected vitamin assays. When to seek medical evaluation

Seek professional assessment if bruising is recurrent, occurs without clear trauma, or accompanies other signs such as significant bleeding from cuts, blood in urine or stool, persistent fatigue, or a family history of bleeding disorders. Targeted testing helps separate nutritional causes from hematologic disease and informs appropriate dietary or medical management.
