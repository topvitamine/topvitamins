Introduction
As people enter their 60s, physiological changes affect nutrient absorption and metabolic demands. Age-related declines in gastric acid, altered body composition, reduced sun exposure, and chronic medications can all influence vitamin status. This short guide highlights key vitamins important after 60, practical dietary considerations, and safety points to discuss with a healthcare professional.

Key vitamins for adults over 60

Vitamin D: Important for calcium absorption, bone health, immune function, and mood regulation. Older skin produces less vitamin D from sunlight and time outdoors often decreases, so monitoring serum 25(OH)D and supplementing when indicated helps reduce fracture risk.

Vitamin B12: Required for red blood cell formation and neurological function. Reduced gastric acid and use of medications like proton-pump inhibitors impair absorption. Sublingual or methylcobalamin forms and periodic blood testing are common approaches to ensure adequate status.

B vitamins (B6, folate): These support energy metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, and cardiovascular health. Consider methylated folate (L‑methylfolate) if genetic variants affect folate metabolism.

Vitamin K (K2): Works with vitamin D to direct calcium into bone and away from arterial deposition. Vitamin K2 (menaquinone) is the form most commonly associated with bone and vascular benefits in older adults.

Vitamins C and E: Antioxidant nutrients that support immune competence, connective tissue maintenance, and cellular protection from oxidative stress. Dietary sources remain preferable, with supplementation considered when intake is inadequate.

Dietary sources and practical tips

A food-first approach is recommended: fatty fish, fortified dairy or alternatives, eggs, leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes, and whole grains provide many of the above vitamins in bioavailable forms. Fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are best absorbed with meals containing healthy fats. For beverage-based magnesium support and related choices, see drinks high in magnesium.

Older adults following plant-based diets should be mindful of nutrients that are less abundant or less bioavailable in plants. A review of common gaps is available in the discussion of vegan vitamin considerations and an accompanying summary on Vegan Vitamins on Telegra.ph.

When supplements are appropriate and what to monitor

Supplements can be useful when dietary intake, absorption, or laboratory results indicate insufficiency. Common monitoring targets include serum 25(OH)D, B12, folate, and relevant minerals. Be cautious with fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) because they can accumulate; dosing should be evidence-based and periodically reviewed.

Interactions are clinically important: vitamin K affects anticoagulant therapy, and high doses of vitamin E may alter bleeding risk. Maintain an up-to-date list of medications and supplements for any clinician visits. For additional composition references, product databases such as Topvitamine can provide ingredient information, though clinical decisions should rely on lab results and professional guidance.

Conclusion

After 60, attention to vitamin status supports bone health, cognition, immunity, and overall functional capacity. Regular assessment, a balanced diet, and targeted supplementation when indicated help manage age-related nutrient risks.
