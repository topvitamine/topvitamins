Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is an essential water-soluble nutrient with several distinct physiological roles. It is required for DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation, nervous system maintenance, and intermediary metabolism that supports cellular energy. Because humans cannot synthesize B12, dietary intake or supplementation is necessary to sustain normal function over a lifetime.

Core biological functions

At a biochemical level, B12 acts as a cofactor in two important reactions: the conversion of homocysteine to methionine, which supports methylation reactions and neurotransmitter synthesis, and the conversion of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA, which feeds into energy-producing pathways. These pathways explain why deficiency can affect cognition, mood, and fatigue levels.

In the nervous system, B12 contributes to myelin formation—the insulating sheath around nerve fibers that enables rapid signal transmission. Insufficient B12 impairs myelin repair and can lead to sensory disturbances, weakness, and gait instability. In the bone marrow, B12 is necessary for normal red blood cell maturation; deficiency produces megaloblastic anemia characterized by large, inefficient erythrocytes and symptoms such as pallor and exertional breathlessness.

Risk factors and absorption

B12 absorption is complex and requires intrinsic factor produced by gastric parietal cells. Conditions that reduce gastric acidity, impair intrinsic factor, or damage the terminal ileum (where B12 is absorbed) increase deficiency risk. Common risk groups include older adults, people with autoimmune gastritis, those who have had bariatric surgery, individuals with Crohn’s disease, long-term users of proton pump inhibitors or metformin, and people following strict vegetarian or vegan diets.

Forms, testing, and evidence-based supplementation

Common supplemental forms include cyanocobalamin and methylcobalamin; methylcobalamin is the bioactive form often preferred for neurological support. Determining deficiency typically involves serum B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA), and homocysteine measurements, which together provide a clearer clinical picture than serum B12 alone. When deficiency is confirmed, treatment strategies vary from high-dose oral or sublingual supplementation to intramuscular injections in cases of severe malabsorption. Co-nutrients such as folate and vitamin B6 influence homocysteine metabolism, and omega-3 fatty acids or magnesium may support neurological health in complementary roles.
