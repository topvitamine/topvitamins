Quick Answer

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in over 300 enzymatic reactions, including energy production, muscle contraction, and nervous system function. Among whole foods, seeds (especially pumpkin seeds), nuts (such as almonds and cashews), dark leafy greens (notably spinach and chard), legumes, and some whole grains are consistently highest in magnesium per serving.

Why Dietary Magnesium Matters

Magnesium contributes to cellular energy (ATP) synthesis, bone mineralization, and regulation of blood pressure and blood glucose. Dietary sources are generally preferable to isolated supplementation because whole foods provide cofactors—fiber, vitamin K, potassium, and phytonutrients—that support absorption and overall metabolic balance. However, some people may require supplements due to restricted diets, increased losses, or medical conditions that affect absorption.

Top Food Sources

  • Seeds: Pumpkin seeds are among the densest, offering high mg per 100 g and easy to add to meals.
  • Nuts: Almonds and cashews provide substantial magnesium along with healthy fats and protein.
  • Leafy greens: Cooked spinach and Swiss chard deliver high bioavailable magnesium for plant-based diets.
  • Legumes: Black beans, lentils, and chickpeas supply magnesium plus fiber and plant protein.
  • Whole grains & pseudo-grains: Quinoa and brown rice are steady contributors to intake.
  • Fruits & vegetables: Avocado and bananas add moderate magnesium while contributing potassium and fiber.

Practical Tips for Increasing Intake

Include a portion of magnesium-rich foods at multiple meals: sprinkle seeds on salads or yogurt, snack on a small handful of nuts, add cooked spinach to soups and smoothies, and include legumes in main dishes. Light steaming or sautéing of vegetables can preserve minerals better than prolonged boiling. When to Consider Testing or Supplementing

Symptomatic signs that can prompt evaluation include persistent muscle cramps, fatigue, irritability, or sleep disturbances. These symptoms can overlap with other nutrient deficiencies; for instance, low vitamin D can produce similar signs—see a summary of symptoms of low vitamin D and how they may interact with mineral status. Evidence-Based Considerations

Bioavailability varies by food matrix and by magnesium form in supplements; magnesium citrate, glycinate, and malate are generally better absorbed than oxide. Most healthy adults can meet needs through diverse whole foods, but clinical conditions (e.g., gastrointestinal disorders, prolonged diuretic use) may increase requirements.
