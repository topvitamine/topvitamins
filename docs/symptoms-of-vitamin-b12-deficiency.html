Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell production, nervous system integrity, and DNA synthesis. Because humans cannot synthesize B12, it must be obtained from diet or supplements. Deficiency can progress subtly and lead to hematological and neurological complications. Early recognition of common signs helps guide testing and treatment.

Common Symptoms

Symptoms of B12 deficiency are variable and may develop over months to years. Key manifestations include:

  • Fatigue and weakness: Impaired red blood cell formation reduces oxygen delivery, causing persistent tiredness and reduced exercise tolerance.
  • Neurological symptoms: Peripheral neuropathy presents as tingling, numbness, balance problems, and muscle weakness due to myelin disruption.
  • Cognitive changes: Memory lapses, poor concentration, and slowed thinking can occur and sometimes mimic dementias.
  • Glossitis and oral signs: Inflamed, smooth, or swollen tongue, mouth ulcers, and altered taste are common mucosal indicators.
  • Pernicious anemia: An autoimmune cause of B12 malabsorption that leads to macrocytic anemia with pallor, shortness of breath, and more pronounced fatigue.

Diagnosis and When to Test

Diagnosis typically combines clinical assessment with laboratory tests: serum B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA), homocysteine, and complete blood count. MMA and homocysteine are useful when serum B12 is borderline because they better reflect functional deficiency. Healthcare providers may also test for intrinsic factor antibodies when pernicious anemia is suspected.

At-Risk Groups and Contributing Factors

Groups at increased risk include older adults, strict vegans, people with gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., celiac disease, Crohn’s, post-gastric surgery), and those on certain medications such as metformin or proton pump inhibitors. Identifying risk factors helps prioritize screening and prevention strategies. For related guidance on choosing appropriate supplement dosages, see determining the right supplement dosage.

Treatment Options and Prognosis

Treatment depends on the cause and severity. Oral or sublingual supplementation is effective when absorption is adequate; intramuscular injections are used for severe deficiency or malabsorption. Therapeutic dosing is often much higher than daily requirements and is tailored by clinicians. Neurological symptoms may improve with prompt treatment, but prolonged deficiency can cause irreversible nerve damage.

Related Considerations

B12 deficiency can overlap with deficiencies in other nutrients and with conditions such as low vitamin D; learning to recognize overlapping symptom patterns is useful (symptoms of low vitamin D). Conclusion

Common indicators include fatigue, paresthesia, cognitive changes, glossitis, and signs of anemia. Objective testing guides diagnosis, and early treatment improves outcomes.
