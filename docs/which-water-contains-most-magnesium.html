Which water contains the most magnesium?

Magnesium in drinking water varies widely with geology, source type and treatment. Understanding which waters naturally contain the most magnesium helps people seeking to increase their intake through hydration rather than supplements alone. This article summarizes typical concentrations across common water types and highlights sources that are often richest in magnesium.

Natural mineral and spring waters generally offer the highest magnesium concentrations because they interact directly with subsurface rocks and sediments over long periods. Groundwater flowing through magnesium-bearing formations—such as dolomite, serpentine, or certain volcanic layers—dissolves and transports magnesium into aquifers. Some bottled mineral waters list magnesium values exceeding 50 mg/L, and a few therapeutic brands report values above 100 mg/L.

By contrast, treated tap water and heavily filtered water often contain lower magnesium. Municipal supplies reflect local geology and treatment choices: in regions with hard water, tap water can provide notable magnesium (sometimes 10–50 mg/L), whereas softened or reverse-osmosis-treated water may be depleted of divalent minerals and have magnesium concentrations near zero. Home filtration systems vary; carbon filters typically leave minerals unchanged, but reverse osmosis or distillation remove most dissolved ions.

Examples of water types and typical magnesium ranges are useful for comparison. Mineral and spring waters commonly range from about 30 to 150+ mg/L depending on the source. Tap water varies from roughly 1 to 50 mg/L regionally. Filtered and purified bottled waters often fall near the low end of that spectrum, unless they are specifically labeled as mineral or spring waters.

Some well-known mineral and spring waters are frequently cited in chemical analyses for their magnesium content. These natural options can meaningfully contribute to daily magnesium intake: a liter of water at 50 mg/L supplies 50 mg of magnesium, which is a substantial fraction of the 300–400 mg/day recommended for many adults. Highly mineralized therapeutic waters may be used under medical guidance for specific deficiencies.

When selecting a water source for magnesium, check the label or local water quality report for a mineral analysis. Bottled mineral and spring waters typically include detailed mineral charts; municipal water utilities publish annual quality reports.
