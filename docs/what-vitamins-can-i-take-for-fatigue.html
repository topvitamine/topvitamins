What Vitamins Can I Take for Fatigue?

Fatigue is a common, multifactorial symptom that can reflect lifestyle factors, medical conditions, or nutrient deficiencies. Evidence indicates that several vitamins and minerals play direct roles in cellular energy production, neurotransmitter synthesis, and sleep regulation. Addressing low levels of specific nutrients can reduce tiredness for many people, but lab testing and clinical context are important for accurate diagnosis and safe supplementation.

Key nutrients linked to energy

Some nutrients have stronger links to fatigue in clinical studies:

  • Vitamin B12: Required for red blood cell formation and neurologic function; deficiency commonly causes lethargy and cognitive slowing. For more on B12’s broader health effects, see Vitamin B12 and heart health.
  • Iron: Central to oxygen transport; iron-deficiency anemia is a well-established cause of physical fatigue. Testing of ferritin and hemoglobin is recommended before starting iron therapy.
  • Vitamin D: Observational studies associate low vitamin D status with increased fatigue and mood symptoms; correction of deficiency may improve energy in some individuals.
  • Magnesium: Cofactor in ATP synthesis and nervous system regulation; low magnesium can contribute to muscle fatigue, cramps, and poor sleep.
  • Vitamin C and CoQ10: Support antioxidant capacity and mitochondrial function, which can indirectly affect perceived energy and recovery.

Genetic and metabolic considerations

Genetic differences, such as MTHFR variants, influence how B-vitamins are metabolized. Individuals with these variants may require different forms or doses of folate and B12; practical strategies are discussed in resources on MTHFR mutations and B12 strategies and a detailed MTHFR discussion on Telegraph.

Supplement choices and safety

Choose bioavailable forms where evidence supports superior uptake (for example, methylcobalamin for B12 or chelated magnesium). Avoid empiric high-dose regimens without testing; excessive iron, vitamin A, or other fat-soluble vitamins carries toxicity risk. If fatigue is persistent or severe, blood tests (CBC, ferritin, 25(OH)D, B12, folate, magnesium) and a clinical evaluation are appropriate.

Adjuncts and lifestyle

Non-vitamin approaches often complement nutritional support. Regular sleep, graded physical activity, balanced macronutrients, and stress management enhance mitochondrial efficiency and nutrient utilization. Certain supplements such as CoQ10, omega-3s, or adaptogens may help specific fatigue patterns, but their benefits are typically modest and context-dependent.
