What are the neurological symptoms of vitamin B12 deficiency?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for nervous system integrity, myelin maintenance, and neurotransmitter synthesis. Early and progressive deficiency can produce a spectrum of neurological symptoms that may precede or occur alongside hematological changes. Common early signs

Initial neurological complaints are often sensory and subtle: paresthesia (tingling or “pins and needles”) in the hands and feet, numbness, and a burning sensation in the extremities. Patients may also report altered temperature perception, heightened sensitivity to touch, or a crawling sensation under the skin. These symptoms reflect peripheral nerve involvement due to impaired myelin synthesis.

Motor and balance disturbances

As deficiency progresses, motor symptoms can develop. Muscle weakness, reduced coordination, and gait instability are common. Impaired proprioception—difficulty sensing limb position—leads to unsteadiness, particularly in low-light conditions or on uneven surfaces. Older adults with these signs have a higher fall risk and should be evaluated promptly.

Cognitive and psychiatric effects

Vitamin B12 deficiency can affect cognition and mood. Manifestations range from difficulty concentrating and “brain fog” to memory impairment, depression, irritability, and in severe cases, psychosis or dementia-like symptoms. These changes are linked to disrupted methylation pathways and altered neurotransmitter synthesis.

Severe complications

Long-standing, untreated deficiency can cause subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord, characterized by demyelination of dorsal and lateral columns. Clinically this presents with progressive sensory loss, spasticity, and, in advanced stages, paralysis. Optic nerve involvement causing visual disturbances has also been reported. Some neurological damage may become irreversible if treatment is delayed.

Diagnostic approach

Diagnosis incorporates serum B12 measurements plus functional biomarkers such as methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine, which often rise before serum B12 declines markedly. A neurological exam assessing vibration sense, reflexes, coordination, and gait helps differentiate B12-related neuropathy from other causes.

Prevention and adjunctive considerations

Risk groups include older adults, strict vegetarians and vegans, and people with absorption disorders. While targeted B12 supplementation is the mainstay for prevention and treatment, attention to overall nutrient status supports recovery. Takeaway

Neurological symptoms of B12 deficiency are varied and can be reversible if identified early. Clinicians and individuals should monitor risk factors and use laboratory testing when neurological signs are present to guide timely intervention.
