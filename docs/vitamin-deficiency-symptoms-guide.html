Introduction

Vitamins are essential micronutrients that support metabolism, immune function, tissue repair, and neurological health. Deficiencies can develop gradually and present with non-specific symptoms such as fatigue or more specific signs like night blindness or easy bruising. Understanding common deficiency patterns helps guide testing, dietary changes, and appropriate supplementation under clinical supervision.

Common vitamin deficiencies and characteristic symptoms

Vitamin D deficiency often presents with bone pain, proximal muscle weakness, and generalized fatigue. Because vitamin D regulates calcium absorption and bone remodeling, prolonged insufficiency may contribute to osteomalacia in adults and increased fracture risk. Risk factors include limited sun exposure, darker skin pigmentation, and conditions that impair conversion or absorption.

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) deficiency typically causes fatigue, pallor from megaloblastic anemia, paresthesia (tingling) in hands and feet, gait disturbances, and cognitive changes. Long-standing deficiency may lead to irreversible neurological damage. Populations at risk include older adults, those with malabsorptive disorders, and people following strict vegetarian or vegan diets without reliable supplementation sources.

Vitamin A deficiency classically causes night blindness and xerosis (dryness) of the conjunctiva and cornea; it can also impair epithelial barrier function and increase susceptibility to infections. Beta‑carotene–rich plants and preformed retinol from animal sources are dietary contributors, while fat malabsorption increases risk.

Vitamin C deficiency results in impaired collagen synthesis, producing easy bruising, bleeding gums, poor wound healing, and joint pain—features of scurvy when severe. Milder insufficiency can reduce immune resilience and increase frequency of minor infections. Smoking and low fruit/vegetable intake are common contributors.

Vitamin E deficiency is uncommon but may cause neuromuscular symptoms such as muscle weakness, ataxia, and peripheral neuropathy, particularly in individuals with fat malabsorption or genetic transport defects. Vitamin K deficiency manifests primarily as bleeding tendencies—easy bruising, prolonged bleeding from minor wounds, or in newborns, a risk of hemorrhage—because vitamin K is required for clotting factor activation.

Folate (vitamin B9) deficiency mirrors B12 in producing megaloblastic anemia and can cause fatigue, irritability, and—during pregnancy—increased risk of neural tube defects in the fetus. Other B vitamins and micronutrients (for example, B6, biotin, and pantothenic acid) can cause more subtle symptoms such as dermatitis, hair changes, mood disturbances, or burning sensations when deficient.

Assessment and practical considerations

Clinical suspicion should be confirmed with targeted laboratory tests (serum 25‑hydroxyvitamin D, B12 levels and methylmalonic acid when appropriate, serum ferritin, complete blood count, and specific vitamin assays). Nutrient deficits often coexist, so a broader nutritional assessment can be useful.

When deficiencies are identified, treatment should be individualized and monitored. Dietary modification is the first-line approach; supplementation is considered when intake or absorption is inadequate or when rapid repletion is required.

Conclusion

Recognizing patterns of symptoms—such as bone and muscle complaints with vitamin D deficiency or neurological signs with B12 deficiency—can expedite diagnosis and management. Coordinate testing and treatment with a healthcare professional to ensure safe, evidence-based correction of deficiencies.
