Introduction

Vitamin D is essential for multiple physiological processes, including bone mineralization, muscle function, immune modulation, and aspects of brain chemistry. Deficiency is common where sunlight exposure is limited, dietary intake is low, or absorption is impaired. Recognizing early signs helps guide testing and appropriate corrective measures. For a detailed overview of the most common presentations, see the discussion on symptoms of low vitamin D.

Bone pain and skeletal symptoms

One of the clearest clinical manifestations of low vitamin D is bone pain or diffuse skeletal aching. Insufficient vitamin D reduces intestinal calcium absorption, which can lead to osteomalacia in adults and rickets in children. Patients often describe a dull, persistent ache in the hips, pelvis, lower back, ribs, or legs. In at-risk populations—older adults, those on long-term corticosteroids, or with malabsorption—this symptom can herald accelerated bone loss and increased fracture risk.

Muscle weakness and falls

Vitamin D affects muscle cell function via vitamin D receptors. Deficiency may present as subtle proximal muscle weakness—difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, or decreased balance—contributing to a higher risk of falls, especially in older adults. Clinical trials suggest that restoring vitamin D to adequate levels can improve muscle performance and reduce fall incidence when combined with exercise and rehabilitation.

Fatigue and low energy

Chronic fatigue is frequently reported among individuals with low vitamin D. The vitamin participates in mitochondrial function and cellular energy metabolism; low serum levels have been associated in observational studies with greater perceived tiredness. While fatigue is nonspecific and multifactorial, persistent unexplained lethargy merits checking serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D as part of a broader evaluation.

Mood changes and cognitive symptoms

There is growing evidence linking low vitamin D with mood disturbances, including depressed mood and seasonal affective patterns. Vitamin D receptors in brain regions involved in mood regulation and potential effects on neurotransmitter synthesis provide biologic plausibility. Supplementation may be a useful adjunctive strategy in deficient individuals but should not replace standard mental health care when indicated.

Immune function and infection susceptibility

Vitamin D helps regulate innate and adaptive immune responses. Low levels have been associated with increased susceptibility to respiratory infections and prolonged recovery times. Randomized trials and meta-analyses indicate modest protective effects of regular vitamin D supplementation against acute respiratory tract infections, particularly in individuals starting with low baseline levels.

Assessment and adjunct considerations

Diagnosis relies on serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D measurement. Management may include targeted supplementation, attention to dietary sources, and safe sunlight exposure where appropriate. Conclusion

Recognizing symptoms such as bone pain, muscle weakness, fatigue, mood changes, and increased infections can prompt appropriate testing and management. Clinical context matters: laboratory confirmation and individualized planning guide safe and effective correction of deficiency.
