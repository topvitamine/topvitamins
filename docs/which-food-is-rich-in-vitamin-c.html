Which food is rich in vitamin C?

Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential water-soluble nutrient involved in collagen synthesis, immune support, antioxidant protection, and enhanced iron absorption. Because humans cannot synthesize vitamin C, dietary intake is necessary to prevent deficiency and support physiological functions. Food sources provide ascorbic acid together with fibers and phytochemicals that influence absorption and biological activity, so prioritizing whole foods is generally recommended.

Top natural sources

Several fruits and vegetables provide particularly high concentrations of vitamin C. Guava is one of the most vitamin C-dense fruits commonly available, with over 200 mg per 100 g in many varieties. Red bell peppers deliver roughly 150–200 mg per cup (chopped), exceeding many citrus fruits on a per-serving basis. Kiwi and strawberries are also notable: a single kiwi can supply around 60–90 mg, and a cup of strawberries often provides near the adult recommended intake.

Other valuable sources include broccoli, Brussels sprouts, and kale, which combine vitamin C with fiber, minerals, and phytochemicals such as sulforaphane and carotenoids. Tomatoes and citrus fruits (oranges, lemons, grapefruit) remain accessible options; one medium orange supplies approximately 60–80 mg of vitamin C.

How food compares with supplements

Absorption of vitamin C from food is efficient at moderate intakes: studies estimate roughly 70–90% absorption when daily intake is between 30 and 180 mg. At much higher supplemental doses, absorption declines and excess is excreted in urine. Whole foods offer matrices of co‑nutrients that may modulate antioxidant activity and immune effects, while supplements can be useful in specific situations such as restricted diets, malabsorption, or elevated physiological need.

Practical tips to maximize vitamin C intake

Consume a variety of colorful fruits and vegetables to ensure adequate intake and a spectrum of phytochemicals. Prioritize fresh, ripe produce and favor raw or lightly cooked preparations: vitamin C is water‑soluble and heat‑sensitive, so steaming or brief sautéing preserves more ascorbic acid than prolonged boiling. Pairing vitamin C‑rich foods with iron-containing plant foods (legumes, dark leafy greens) enhances non‑heme iron absorption.

Evidence-based perspective

Population studies and clinical research support the role of vitamin C in immune function, wound healing, and antioxidant defense, but effects vary by baseline nutritional status and dose. Supplementation can shorten the duration of some respiratory infections in certain groups, yet routine high‑dose supplementation offers limited additional benefit for well‑nourished individuals. Conclusion

A diet emphasizing guava, red bell peppers, kiwi, strawberries, cruciferous vegetables, and citrus fruits reliably provides vitamin C along with complementary nutrients.
