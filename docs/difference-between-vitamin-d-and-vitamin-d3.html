What is the difference between vitamin D and vitamin D3?

Vitamin D is a collective term for a group of fat-soluble secosteroids that support calcium absorption, immune function, and many cellular processes. The two forms most commonly referenced in nutrition are vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) and vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol). When people ask about “vitamin D” versus “vitamin D3,” they are usually distinguishing the general category from the specific form cholecalciferol, which is the form produced in human skin and generally performs better in clinical studies.

Forms and metabolic activation

Both D2 and D3 are inactive precursors that require two hydroxylation steps to become the active hormone calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D). The first hydroxylation in the liver produces 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D), the circulating marker commonly measured to assess status. The kidney then converts 25(OH)D to 1,25(OH)2D, the hormonally active molecule. Although D2 and D3 follow the same pathway, D3 has a higher affinity for the vitamin D binding protein and tends to raise and maintain 25(OH)D concentrations more effectively.

Sources and bioavailability

Vitamin D3 is synthesized in skin exposed to UVB radiation and is found in animal-derived foods such as fatty fish and egg yolks. D2 is produced by ultraviolet irradiation of plant sterols and occurs in some mushrooms and fortified foods. Vegan D3 sourced from lichen is now available and offers a plant-based option that mirrors the efficacy of animal-derived D3. Why the distinction matters clinically

Randomized and observational studies indicate that D3 is generally more potent than D2 at raising serum 25(OH)D and maintains levels longer. This distinction affects dosing decisions: a given dose of D3 is more likely to achieve target blood concentrations than the same dose of D2. People with liver or kidney impairment, fat malabsorption, or those taking interacting medications may require individualized approaches because these conditions alter activation or absorption.

Practical considerations: testing and supplementation

Serum 25(OH)D testing is the accepted method to evaluate vitamin D status. Deficiency thresholds vary slightly by guideline, but concentrations below 20 ng/mL commonly indicate deficiency. Maintenance dosing often falls in the 800–2000 IU/day range for adults, adjusted according to baseline levels, body weight, and clinical context. Oil-based D3 formulations are typically better absorbed, while water-dispersible forms can help those with fat absorption issues.

In brief, the practical difference is that vitamin D3 is typically the preferred supplement form due to greater potency and longer persistence in blood, while “vitamin D” can refer to either D2 or D3. Individual needs vary, so assessment and monitoring of serum 25(OH)D are useful when initiating or adjusting supplementation.
