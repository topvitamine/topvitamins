Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell production, myelin maintenance, and DNA synthesis. Deficiency can cause megaloblastic anemia, neuropathy, and cognitive decline. While dietary insufficiency is a common cause, a range of diseases and medical interventions can impair B12 absorption or utilization. Understanding these conditions helps clinicians choose appropriate testing and supplementation strategies.

Autoimmune Causes: Pernicious Anemia and Gastritis

Pernicious anemia is an autoimmune condition in which antibodies target intrinsic factor or gastric parietal cells, preventing efficient B12 uptake in the terminal ileum. Similarly, autoimmune gastritis reduces stomach acid and intrinsic factor production, impairing release of B12 from dietary proteins. These disorders typically require lifelong monitoring and often bypass the gut through parenteral or very high-dose oral supplementation.

Malabsorption Syndromes

Disorders that damage the small intestinal mucosa or interfere with digestive processes can produce B12 deficiency despite adequate intake. Celiac disease and tropical sprue reduce absorptive surface area in the ileum. Chronic pancreatitis diminishes pancreatic enzymes needed to separate B12 from food proteins. Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) may consume B12 or interfere with its absorption. In many of these cases, treatment of the underlying disorder is combined with targeted B12 repletion.

Gastrointestinal Disease and Surgery

Structural changes from inflammatory bowel disease (especially Crohn’s disease affecting the terminal ileum), ileal resection, gastrectomy, and bariatric surgeries (e.g., Roux-en-Y gastric bypass) commonly precipitate deficiency. Reduced intrinsic factor, altered transit time, and loss of absorptive tissue all contribute. Postoperative surveillance and routine supplementation are recommended in guidelines for patients who have undergone these procedures.

Dietary Insufficiency and Vulnerable Populations

Strict vegetarian and vegan diets lacking fortified foods place individuals at risk because natural B12 sources are mainly animal-derived. Older adults may also have reduced gastric acid secretion, diminishing B12 release from food. Social determinants such as limited food access can further increase risk. Preventive strategies include fortified foods and evidence-based supplementation regimens.

Clinical Approach and Supplementation Considerations

Diagnosis relies on serum B12, methylmalonic acid, and homocysteine levels, plus assessment for autoantibodies when autoimmune disease is suspected. Treatment choices depend on the cause: intramuscular or high-dose oral/sub-lingual B12 can restore levels rapidly, particularly when malabsorption or intrinsic factor deficiency is present. Long-term maintenance may be needed in chronic disease.

Conclusion

Multiple disease processes—autoimmune, inflammatory, structural, infectious, and dietary—can cause B12 deficiency by altering absorption or intake. Clinicians should evaluate underlying causes to tailor supplementation and monitoring. Early identification and appropriate repletion reduce the risk of irreversible neurological injury.
