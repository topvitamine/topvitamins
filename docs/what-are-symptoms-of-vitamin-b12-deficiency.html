What are the symptoms of vitamin B12 deficiency?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell production, myelin maintenance, DNA synthesis, and energy metabolism. Because it must be obtained from diet or supplements, deficiency can develop in people with limited animal-food intake, malabsorption, certain medications, or age-related changes in gastric function. Recognizing common signs early supports timely evaluation and treatment.

Common clinical features

Symptoms of B12 deficiency span hematologic, neurologic, cognitive, and oral manifestations. Typical complaints include persistent fatigue and generalized weakness due to impaired red blood cell formation and reduced cellular energy production. Patients may also experience shortness of breath, paleness, or dizziness consistent with anemia.

Neurological signs often involve peripheral neuropathy: tingling, numbness, or a “pins and needles” sensation in the hands and feet. Impaired balance, gait instability, and decreased coordination can occur when myelin sheaths are affected. In advanced or prolonged deficiency, motor weakness and reduced reflexes are possible.

Cognitive effects range from subtle “brain fog,” poor concentration, and slowed processing to more pronounced memory deficits. In older adults, unrecognized B12 deficiency can mimic or contribute to cognitive decline. Mood changes such as irritability or depressive symptoms have also been associated with low B12 status.

Oral findings can be an early clue: glossitis (a smooth, swollen, or tender tongue), mouth ulcers, and angular cheilitis (cracks at the corners of the mouth) are commonly reported in deficient individuals.

Pernicious anemia and risk factors

Pernicious anemia is an autoimmune condition that impairs intrinsic factor production and leads to chronic B12 malabsorption; it is a major cause of clinically significant deficiency. Other risk groups include strict vegetarians and vegans, older adults with reduced gastric acid, individuals with gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., celiac disease, Crohn’s), and people taking long-term proton pump inhibitors or metformin.

Diagnosis and management considerations

Diagnosis relies on clinical assessment and laboratory testing (serum B12, methylmalonic acid, homocysteine, and complete blood count). Early detection improves the chances of symptom reversal, especially for neurological complaints. Treatment options include oral supplementation, sublingual preparations, or intramuscular injections depending on cause and severity of malabsorption.
