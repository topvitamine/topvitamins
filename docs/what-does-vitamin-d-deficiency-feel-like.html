What does vitamin D deficiency feel like?

Vitamin D deficiency often develops gradually and can produce a mix of physical and psychological symptoms. Commonly reported experiences include persistent fatigue, muscle weakness, bone or joint pain, and an increased susceptibility to infections. Because these signs overlap with many other conditions, clinical testing is important to determine whether low vitamin D contributes to symptoms.

Typical symptoms and why they occur

Vitamin D helps regulate calcium metabolism, immune responses, and aspects of neuromuscular function. When levels are low, impaired calcium absorption can produce bone tenderness, aching in the ribs, hips, or lower back, and in severe cases contribute to osteomalacia in adults or rickets in children. Muscle weakness—especially in the thighs and proximal limbs—can reduce endurance and make routine tasks more tiring.

Fatigue, mood and cognition

Many people with inadequate vitamin D report generalized fatigue and low energy that do not fully respond to rest. There is growing evidence linking low vitamin D status with mood changes, including symptoms of depression and anxiety, possibly via effects on neurotransmitter synthesis and inflammation. These associations are not proof of causation, but correcting a deficiency can be one component of a broader strategy to address mood and cognitive complaints.

Immune and skin-related signs

Vitamin D supports innate and adaptive immune function. Recurrent respiratory infections or slower recovery from common illnesses may be observed in those with low levels. Skin issues such as dryness or worsening eczema have also been reported; vitamin D receptors are present in skin cells and influence barrier function and inflammation.

Who is at higher risk?

Risk factors include limited sun exposure, darker skin pigmentation, advanced age, obesity, and conditions that impair fat absorption. Pregnant people, infants who are exclusively breastfed without supplementation, and individuals living at high latitudes during winter months are also prone to deficiency.

Assessment and practical steps

The most reliable way to confirm deficiency is a serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D test. Interpretation of results should consider laboratory reference ranges and individual clinical context. Restoring levels and follow-up

Correction strategies typically combine safe sun exposure, dietary sources, and targeted supplementation under medical supervision. Follow-up testing helps ensure levels have normalized and symptoms are monitored.
