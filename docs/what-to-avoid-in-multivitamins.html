Multivitamins can help fill dietary gaps, but not all formulations are equally beneficial. Some products contain ingredients that offer no nutritional value and may reduce nutrient absorption, provoke sensitivities, or contribute to long-term health concerns. This article outlines common additives to avoid and provides practical guidance for choosing cleaner, more effective supplements.

Artificial fillers and manufacturing aids

Fillers and lubricants are used to improve production and appearance, yet they do not contribute nutrients and can sometimes impede absorption. Examples include magnesium stearate, microcrystalline cellulose, and titanium dioxide. Magnesium stearate can form a hydrophobic coating that may reduce solubility for some ingredients, while titanium dioxide is under scrutiny in several regions due to potential nanoparticle-related risks. Prefer formulations that list simple, minimally processed binders such as rice flour or that explicitly state “no unnecessary fillers.”

Synthetic dyes and colorants

Synthetic colors (e.g., FD&C Red No. 40, Yellow No. 5, Blue No. 1) are common in gummies and chewables to improve visual appeal. These additives have been associated with allergic responses and behavioral sensitivity in some individuals, particularly children. When possible, choose dye-free products or those colored with plant-based alternatives such as beet, turmeric, or spirulina extracts.

Added sugars and sweeteners

Gummy vitamins and flavored tablets may contain glucose syrup, sucrose, or high-fructose corn syrup to mask unpleasant tastes. Regular consumption of added sugars contributes to excess caloric intake, dental decay, and metabolic risk. For routine supplementation, capsules or tablets without added sugar are often preferable; if taste is a concern, look for products sweetened naturally with minimal non-caloric sweeteners like stevia or monk fruit.

Unnecessary chemical additives

Preservatives, artificial flavors, and some synthetic antioxidants (e.g., parabens, propyl gallate) are sometimes included for shelf stability or flavor. These compounds are non-nutritive and have been linked in some studies to endocrine or hypersensitivity effects. Prioritize products that list fewer extraneous ingredients and that provide transparent sourcing information.

Low-bioavailability nutrient forms

The chemical form of vitamins and minerals affects how well they are absorbed and used. Oxide forms of minerals (such as magnesium oxide) and certain synthetic vitamin forms (like cyanocobalamin vs. methylcobalamin) can be less bioavailable for some people. Look for chelated minerals (e.g., magnesium bisglycinate, zinc picolinate) and methylated B vitamins (e.g., methylcobalamin, L-5-MTHF) when bioavailability is a priority.

How to select a cleaner product

Carefully read the ingredient list and Supplement Facts panel; avoid products with long lists of excipients and artificial additives.
