Which vitamins after age 60?

Aging alters nutrient needs and absorption, making certain vitamins especially important after age 60. Physiological changes—reduced skin synthesis of vitamin D, diminished stomach acid affecting B12 absorption, polypharmacy, and lower appetite—can create gaps that influence bone strength, cognitive function, immune resilience, and antioxidant defenses. The following evidence-based overview summarizes priority nutrients, common deficiencies, and practical considerations for older adults.

Bone health and musculoskeletal support

Vitamin D and calcium remain foundational for preserving bone mineral density and reducing fracture risk. Adults over 60 commonly benefit from vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) to support intestinal calcium uptake; many guidelines recommend daily intakes in the range of 600–800 IU, with higher therapeutic doses prescribed for deficiency under medical supervision. Adequate magnesium and vitamin K (particularly K2) also support bone remodeling and calcium utilization.

Cognitive function and neurologic protection

Vitamin B12, B6, and folate are central to nerve health and homocysteine metabolism. B12 deficiency is prevalent in older adults and can manifest as memory problems, neuropathy, or mood changes; assessment via blood tests allows targeted correction using oral, sublingual, or injectable forms as indicated. Vitamin E, a fat-soluble antioxidant, supports neuronal membranes, and omega-3 fatty acids—especially DHA—contribute to structural brain health though they are not classified as vitamins.

Immune support and infection resilience

Immunosenescence increases susceptibility to infections. Vitamin D modulates innate and adaptive immunity, while vitamin C supports epithelial barriers and leukocyte function. Zinc is critical for T-cell activity and wound healing. Addressing deficiencies in these nutrients can improve vaccine responses and recovery from acute illness.

Antioxidants and cellular aging

Oxidative stress contributes to age-related cellular damage. Antioxidant nutrients—vitamin C, vitamin E, carotenoids (lutein and zeaxanthin), selenium, and coenzyme Q10—help neutralize free radicals and support mitochondrial function. Lutein and zeaxanthin are also linked to retinal protection and may reduce the risk of age-related macular degeneration.

Identifying and correcting deficiencies

Common deficiencies after 60 include vitamin B12 and vitamin D; iron, folate, magnesium, and zinc may also be low depending on diet and health conditions. Symptoms such as fatigue, bone pain, neuropathy, or cognitive decline warrant laboratory evaluation. Routine screening and individualized supplementation based on results are preferable to blanket high-dose use.

Practical guidance

Choose evidence-based formulations with bioavailable forms (e.g., methylcobalamin for B12, cholecalciferol for D3). Coordinate supplementation with meals to enhance absorption of fat-soluble vitamins and consider divided dosing for tolerance. Consult a healthcare professional before beginning new supplements, especially if using medications that interact with nutrients (e.g., vitamin K and anticoagulants).
