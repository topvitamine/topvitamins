What is the best form of B12 to take?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, DNA synthesis, nervous system health, and energy metabolism. Deficiency is common among vegetarians, older adults, and people with gastrointestinal conditions, so choosing an appropriate B12 supplement is important. This article summarizes the main chemical forms and delivery methods, and highlights practical factors to consider when selecting a B12 strategy.

Comparing the main chemical forms

Methylcobalamin is an active, methylated coenzyme form used directly in methylation reactions and neurotransmitter synthesis. It is often preferred for neurological support and for individuals with methylation-related genetic variants (for example, MTHFR). Adenosylcobalamin is another active form whose primary role is in mitochondrial energy metabolism; it is less common in supplements but valuable for cellular energy support.

Cyanocobalamin is a stable, synthetic form widely used in fortified foods and budget supplements. It requires conversion in the body to active forms; this typically works well in healthy people but can be less efficient in older adults, those with liver or kidney impairment, or genetic differences affecting conversion.

Hydroxocobalamin, often used in clinical settings, has good tissue retention and is commonly used in injection formulations. Each form has trade-offs between stability, cost, and direct bioavailability.

Delivery methods: oral, sublingual, and injections

Oral tablets are convenient and effective for many people with normal digestion. Sublingual lozenges or sprays (sublingual B12) allow absorption across oral mucosa and can be helpful for those with low stomach acid or mild malabsorption. In clinical practice, intramuscular injections provide the fastest and most complete correction of deficiency, bypassing gastrointestinal absorption entirely—this is the standard approach for pernicious anemia or significant malabsorption.

Active B12 vs. synthetic forms

Active B12 refers to methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin, which can be used directly by cells without conversion. These forms are often recommended when rapid biochemical effect is desired or when conversion capacity may be impaired. Cyanocobalamin remains a pragmatic choice for population-level fortification and for people without specific absorption or conversion concerns because of its stability and lower cost.

Practical factors when choosing a supplement

Consider health status (e.g., gastrointestinal disease, gastric surgery), age, diet (vegans and strict vegetarians typically need routine supplementation), genetic factors (MTHFR variants), and budget. People with clear malabsorption or pronounced neurological symptoms should discuss testing and potential injection therapy with a clinician. Those seeking convenient daily support may prefer sublingual methylcobalamin or combined active B12 formulations.
