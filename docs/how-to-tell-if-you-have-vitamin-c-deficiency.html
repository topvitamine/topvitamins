How can you tell if you have a vitamin C deficiency?

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is essential for collagen synthesis, immune function, iron absorption, and antioxidant protection. Because the human body cannot synthesize vitamin C, insufficient dietary intake or impaired absorption can lead to deficiency. Recognizing early signs and confirming low levels with testing helps prevent complications such as poor wound healing, fatigue, and in severe cases scurvy.

Common symptoms and early warning signs

Deficiency often begins with non-specific symptoms: persistent tiredness, irritability, reduced exercise tolerance, and slower healing of minor cuts or bruises. As levels decline further, connective tissue-related signs appear—bleeding or swollen gums, easy bruising, corkscrew-shaped body hair, and dry or rough skin. Increased susceptibility to infections and prolonged recovery from respiratory illnesses are also commonly reported.

Who is at risk?

Risk factors include diets low in fresh fruits and vegetables, cigarette smoking (which increases oxidative stress and vitamin C turnover), older age with limited food access or appetite, alcohol dependence, and gastrointestinal disorders that impair absorption (for example, Crohn’s disease or after some bariatric surgeries). People under chronic physiological stress or recovering from major illness can also have elevated vitamin C requirements.

How deficiency is confirmed

Clinical suspicion based on symptoms should prompt a healthcare consultation. The standard laboratory test measures plasma ascorbic acid concentration. Levels below approximately 0.2 mg/dL (11 µmol/L) are generally considered deficient, and levels under ~0.4 mg/dL may already impair bodily functions. A complete blood count can identify coexisting anemia, while physical examination focuses on skin, gums, and wound status.

Management principles

Treatment begins with increasing intake of vitamin C–rich foods (citrus fruits, kiwi, bell peppers, strawberries, and leafy greens) and addressing contributing causes such as smoking or malabsorption. Short-term repletion with oral vitamin C supplements restores plasma levels quickly; many symptoms improve within days to weeks. Very high doses (above the tolerable upper intake level of 2000 mg/day) can cause gastrointestinal upset in some people, so dosing should be individualized.

When to seek care

Seek medical evaluation if you notice persistent bleeding gums, frequent unexplained bruising, slow wound healing, or repeated infections. These signs can reflect vitamin C deficiency but may also indicate other conditions that require different treatments. A clinician can order plasma ascorbate testing and rule out alternative causes like iron or B12 deficiency.
