What are the symptoms of a magnesium deficiency?

Magnesium is a key mineral involved in hundreds of enzymatic reactions, energy production, nerve signalling, and muscle function. When intake or absorption falls short, a range of symptoms can emerge that affect the musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, neurological, and psychological systems. Recognizing these signs early can help guide evaluation and management.

Common symptoms

Muscle cramps and spasms are among the most frequent early indicators of low magnesium. Magnesium helps muscles relax after contraction; insufficient levels can lead to hyperexcitability of muscle fibers, producing painful cramps, twitches, or persistent spasms, especially in the legs.

Chronic fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance may reflect impaired cellular energy metabolism. Magnesium is required for ATP synthesis, and deficiency can cause ongoing tiredness, weakness, and difficulty concentrating that does not fully resolve with rest.

Cardiac manifestations include palpitations, skipped beats, or other arrhythmias. Magnesium influences electrical stability in cardiac cells and works with potassium and calcium to maintain rhythmic contractions, so disturbances in magnesium balance can present as irregular heartbeat or fluttering sensations.

Neurological symptoms can include frequent headaches or migraines. Clinical studies suggest lower intracellular magnesium levels are associated with increased migraine frequency and severity; supplementation is sometimes used as a preventive measure under clinical guidance.

Mood changes are also reported: anxiety, irritability, sleep disruption, and low mood can accompany deficiency because magnesium affects neurotransmitter systems such as GABA and serotonin.

Who is at higher risk?

Older adults, people with gastrointestinal disorders that impair absorption, those on long-term diuretics or proton pump inhibitors, heavy alcohol consumers, and athletes with high sweat losses are more likely to develop low magnesium. Chronic stress and some metabolic conditions (for example, poorly controlled diabetes) can also deplete magnesium stores.

Assessment and management

Routine serum magnesium testing may not always reflect total body or intracellular magnesium status, so clinicians often interpret results alongside symptoms and risk factors. Dietary modification to include dark leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes, and whole grains is a foundational strategy. In some cases, oral supplementation or targeted clinical interventions are appropriate; formulations differ in absorption and tolerability.

Because supplementation and nutrient interactions can affect other medications or electrolytes, consulting a healthcare professional is advised before beginning a regimen. When to seek care

Seek medical evaluation for persistent muscle cramps, new or worsening palpitations, recurrent migraines, or marked mood changes. These symptoms can overlap with other conditions, so a clinician can assess magnesium status in context and recommend appropriate testing or treatment.
