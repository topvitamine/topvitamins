Why don't doctors tell you to take magnesium?

Magnesium is essential for hundreds of enzymatic reactions, from energy production to nerve and muscle function. Yet many clinicians do not routinely advise supplementation. Understanding why requires reviewing clinical testing limitations, variability in absorption, evidence for benefit, and practical safety considerations.

Clinical detection and testing challenges

One practical reason magnesium is rarely recommended by default is that standard serum magnesium tests are imperfect. Serum levels reflect only about 1% of total body magnesium and can remain normal despite intracellular depletion. Because routine blood work may not detect deficiency, clinicians often prioritize conditions with clear diagnostic markers, referring nutrient assessment to targeted evaluation when symptoms or risk factors exist.

Evidence, benefits, and when supplementation helps

Clinical trials and meta-analyses indicate magnesium supplementation can improve sleep, reduce migraine frequency, support blood pressure control, and reduce muscle cramps in deficient individuals. Benefits are most consistent when there is evidence or suspicion of low magnesium status — for example, in people with chronic diarrhea, diuretic use, poorly controlled diabetes, or gastrointestinal disease that impairs absorption. Absorption and formulation matter

Not all magnesium forms are equal. Magnesium citrate and glycinate are generally better absorbed and better tolerated than magnesium oxide, which is more likely to cause laxative effects. Absorption can also be reduced by high phytate diets, low stomach acid, alcohol, and certain medications. Dividing doses or taking magnesium with meals can improve uptake in people with suboptimal gut function.

Safety and dosing considerations

Dietary magnesium from food sources carries minimal risk, but supplemental magnesium requires caution. Regulatory guidance often cites a tolerable upper intake for supplemental magnesium around 250 mg/day for adults in some regions, though therapeutic doses used in studies (300–600 mg/day) are common under supervision. The kidneys excrete excess magnesium, so people with impaired renal function need individualized advice. Interactions with antibiotics or anticoagulants also merit spacing doses and clinical oversight.

System-level influences on recommendations

Medical education historically emphasizes pharmacology and acute care; nutrition training can be limited. The pharmaceutical model of care and time-constrained consultations push clinicians toward treatments with standardized dosing and monitoring pathways. Additionally, concerns about unsupervised supplement use and potential interactions contribute to conservative recommendations.

How to approach magnesium thoughtfully

If you suspect low magnesium—symptoms like muscle cramps, persistent fatigue, restless sleep, or frequent migraines—discuss targeted testing and safe supplementation strategies with a clinician or registered dietitian.
