What is vitamin B12 used for?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is an essential water-soluble vitamin with key roles in human physiology. It is required for red blood cell production, DNA synthesis, and the maintenance of the nervous system. Because the body cannot synthesize B12, intake through diet or supplementation is necessary to prevent deficiency and its potentially irreversible consequences.

B12 participates in biochemical reactions that support energy metabolism and neurological function. It assists in converting homocysteine to methionine, a step important for methylation reactions and neurotransmitter production, and it contributes to the breakdown of certain fatty acids for entry into the Krebs cycle. These processes underpin why B12 status influences fatigue, cognition, and mood.

Signs of B12 deficiency often develop slowly and can be nonspecific. Common early symptoms include persistent fatigue, weakness, pale skin, and glossitis. As deficiency progresses, neurological manifestations such as numbness or tingling in the hands and feet, balance difficulties, memory problems, and mood changes may appear. Because some nerve damage can become permanent if untreated, timely diagnosis is important.

Absorption of B12 is a multistep process that begins in the stomach with the binding of B12 to intrinsic factor, followed by uptake in the ileum. Several factors impair this cascade: age-related reduction in gastric acid, autoimmune conditions like pernicious anemia, gastrointestinal diseases (e.g., Crohn’s, celiac disease), surgical resections, and certain medications including metformin and proton pump inhibitors. Dietary patterns also matter—natural B12 is largely confined to animal foods, so vegetarians and vegans are at increased risk without fortified foods or supplements.

Supplement forms include oral tablets, sublingual preparations, nasal sprays, and intramuscular injections. In individuals with severe absorption problems or pernicious anemia, injections provide a direct route into circulation and rapidly restore stores. For others, high-dose oral or sublingual supplements can be effective because a small percentage of B12 is absorbed passively. Many people take B12 to support energy, but the effect depends on baseline status. Those who are deficient frequently report notable improvements in fatigue and concentration after correction. In contrast, people with adequate B12 levels are unlikely to experience additional energizing effects. Testing typically begins with serum B12; more sensitive measures such as methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine can clarify functional status. Management is individualized: correcting deficiencies may require injections or high-dose oral therapy, while prevention for at-risk groups may rely on fortified foods and routine supplementation. In summary, vitamin B12 is used primarily to support erythropoiesis, neurological integrity, DNA synthesis, and metabolic energy pathways. Awareness of absorption factors, at-risk populations, and appropriate testing helps ensure adequate status and prevents the serious outcomes associated with prolonged deficiency.
