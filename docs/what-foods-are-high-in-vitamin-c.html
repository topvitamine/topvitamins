Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential, water-soluble nutrient involved in collagen synthesis, antioxidant protection, and immune function. Adults generally require about 75–90 mg per day, and because the human body cannot synthesize or store large amounts of vitamin C, regular dietary intake is important. For a concise overview of sources and dietary recommendations, see this comprehensive guide to what foods are high in vitamin C.

Top fruit sources

Fruits commonly cited for high vitamin C content include guava, kiwi, strawberries, papaya, oranges, and acerola cherries. Guava contains substantially more vitamin C than many citrus fruits, while acerola and camu camu are notable for extremely high concentrations (often used in powdered supplements). Kiwifruit and strawberries offer both vitamin C and complementary phytonutrients, such as polyphenols and fiber, which support absorption and overall health.

Vegetables and savory options

Vegetables can provide substantial vitamin C without added sugars. Red bell peppers, chili peppers, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, and kale are reliable sources. For example, red bell peppers often contain more vitamin C per serving than oranges. Preparing vegetables with minimal water and heat—such as steaming or light sautéing—helps retain vitamin C, which is heat- and water-sensitive.

Concentrated and traditional sources

Less common but potent sources include rose hips and certain tropical fruits. Rose hips and acerola cherries have been used traditionally to address deficiency due to their high ascorbic acid levels. Food-first approach and when to consider supplements

A food-first strategy supplies vitamin C together with fibers, bioflavonoids, and other cofactors that aid absorption and metabolic effects. However, certain situations—restricted diets, smoking, malabsorption, or increased physiological needs—may justify supplemental support. Practical tips for maximizing intake

Prioritize whole fruits and vegetables, include a variety of colors across meals, and avoid prolonged boiling. Combine vitamin C sources with plant-based iron sources (e.g., spinach with citrus) to enhance non-heme iron absorption. Storing produce appropriately and consuming it soon after purchase also helps preserve vitamin C levels.

Conclusion

Regular intake of vitamin C-rich fruits and vegetables supports immune function, collagen production, and antioxidant defenses. A balanced diet emphasizing diverse plant foods is an effective strategy; targeted supplementation may be appropriate in specific circumstances.
