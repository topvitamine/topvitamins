What is the food richest in vitamin C?

Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble nutrient essential for collagen synthesis, iron absorption, immune support, and antioxidant defense. Because humans cannot synthesize or store large amounts of Vitamin C, regular dietary intake from diverse foods is important. This article summarizes the foods highest in Vitamin C, practical preservation tips, and how whole foods compare with supplements.

Top natural sources

While many familiar fruits and vegetables contain Vitamin C, a few plant foods are exceptional. Among fresh produce, red bell peppers and guava stand out—red bell peppers provide roughly 190 mg per 100 g, and guava can offer around 200–250 mg per 100 g depending on variety. Several lesser-known superfoods exceed common fruits: acerola cherry is one of the richest whole-food sources, with up to 1,600 mg per 100 g in fresh berries, and camu camu (native to the Amazon) can contain very high levels as well.

Other accessible choices include kiwifruit (about 60–70 mg per medium fruit), strawberries (≈59 mg/100 g), broccoli (≈89 mg/100 g), and Brussels sprouts (≈85 mg/100 g). Leafy greens such as kale and parsley also contribute meaningful amounts, particularly when eaten raw.

Superfoods and concentrated forms

Due to their exceptional Vitamin C density, some superfoods are commonly consumed in powdered form—acerola, camu camu, and amla (Indian gooseberry) are typical examples. These powders allow small serving sizes to deliver large amounts of ascorbic acid and are often used in smoothies or teas. Whole foods versus supplements

Whole foods provide Vitamin C alongside fiber, flavonoids, and other phytonutrients that may enhance absorption and biological activity. Supplements can be useful when dietary intake is insufficient, during periods of increased need, or when fresh produce is unavailable. Liposomal and buffered formulations can improve tolerance and absorption for some individuals. Preservation and preparation tips

Vitamin C is heat- and water-sensitive. To retain content: prefer raw consumption, use light steaming rather than prolonged boiling, minimize surface area before cooking, and store produce chilled and covered. Spread smaller Vitamin C-rich servings across meals to improve retention and absorption, since very large single doses are excreted more readily.

Practical recommendations

Incorporate a variety of fruits and vegetables daily—berries, citrus, peppers, leafy greens, and tropical fruits—to reliably meet Recommended Dietary Allowances (75 mg/day for women, 90 mg/day for men). For seasonal or situational gaps, consider evidence-informed supplementation alongside whole-food sources. Conclusion

Acerola, camu camu, and guava rank among the highest Vitamin C foods, but a practical approach emphasizes varied whole foods—peppers, berries, citrus, and leafy greens—combined with thoughtful preparation and, when appropriate, supplementation.
