Which dietary supplements should not be taken together?

Dietary supplements can support nutrition and health, but combining multiple products without guidance can cause reduced effectiveness or harmful effects. Interactions occur through competitive absorption in the gut, shared metabolic pathways (including liver enzymes), or additive physiological effects. Awareness of common problem pairs and practical timing strategies can reduce risks while preserving benefits.

How supplement interactions occur

Three main mechanisms explain most interactions: absorption interference (minerals competing for transporters), metabolic interactions (ingredients processed by the same liver enzymes), and efficacy alteration (additive or opposing physiological actions). Individual factors—such as age, health conditions, concurrent medications, and dose—determine how significant an interaction will be. Consulting a clinician or pharmacist is especially important for people on prescription medicines.

Major supplement combinations to avoid or manage

Iron and calcium: Calcium can reduce the absorption of non-heme iron by competing for intestinal uptake. To preserve iron status, separate doses by 2–4 hours and consider taking iron with vitamin C to enhance absorption.

St. John’s Wort and antidepressants: St. John’s Wort affects neurotransmitter systems and induces liver enzymes, creating a high risk of serotonin syndrome and reduced efficacy of many prescription drugs. Avoid combining herbal serotonin enhancers with antidepressant medications and disclose herbal use to your prescriber.

Vitamin K and anticoagulants: Vitamin K is central to clotting factor synthesis and can counteract warfarin and similar blood thinners. Patients should aim for consistent vitamin K intake and coordinate dosing and INR monitoring with their clinician rather than abruptly changing intake.

Magnesium (and other minerals) with certain antibiotics: Magnesium, calcium, iron, and zinc can chelate tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones, forming insoluble complexes that reduce antibiotic absorption. Space antibiotic doses at least 2 hours before or 4–6 hours after these minerals.

High-dose vitamin E and blood-thinning agents: Excessive vitamin E can impair platelet function and amplify bleeding risk when combined with aspirin, NSAIDs, or anticoagulants. Stay within recommended intakes and review cumulative blood-thinning effects if using multiple products with antiplatelet or anticoagulant action.

Other noteworthy cautions

Herbs such as ginkgo can increase bleeding risk alongside aspirin; licorice root may worsen potassium loss when used with diuretics; and niacin plus statins may increase liver and muscle toxicity. The label rarely lists all possible interactions, so proactive review with a health professional is essential.

Practical guidance

Space potentially competing minerals apart, keep intake consistent for nutrients that affect medications (like vitamin K), and always inform providers about herbal and dietary supplement use.
