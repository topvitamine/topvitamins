Who Should Not Take Omega-3?

Omega-3 fatty acids—primarily EPA and DHA—offer proven benefits for cardiovascular and cognitive health, but they are not universally appropriate. Certain medical conditions, procedures, and medication regimens can make omega-3 supplementation risky or require careful monitoring. This article summarizes evidence-based considerations so individuals and clinicians can weigh benefits against potential harms.

Key groups that need caution

People taking anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications (for example warfarin, heparin, or dual antiplatelet therapy) should consult a clinician before starting omega-3s because EPA and DHA have mild antiplatelet effects that can increase bleeding risk. Individuals with diagnosed bleeding disorders such as hemophilia or thrombocytopenia are also at higher risk.

Anyone scheduled for surgery or invasive procedures is generally advised to pause high-dose omega-3 supplements 1–2 weeks beforehand to reduce intraoperative bleeding risk. Similarly, a history of peptic ulcer disease or gastrointestinal bleeding warrants medical review prior to use.

People with known fish or shellfish allergies should avoid fish oil products; plant-based alternatives such as algal oil may be safer options. Those with complex immune suppression (for example post-transplant immunosuppressive therapy) should discuss omega-3 use with their specialist because omega-3s can modulate immune responses.

Medical conditions and drug interactions

Patients with diabetes should be supervised when starting omega-3s: while some trials show neutral or modest benefits for glycemic control, individual responses vary and adjustments to glucose-lowering therapy may be needed. Individuals on multiple blood pressure medications should monitor for additive hypotensive effects.

High-dose omega-3 supplementation has been associated in some studies with increases in LDL cholesterol and, rarely, with a higher incidence of atrial fibrillation in susceptible populations. People with existing cardiac arrhythmias or those taking antiarrhythmic drugs should obtain cardiology input before beginning high EPA/DHA regimens.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Omega-3 (especially DHA) supports fetal brain and retinal development and is commonly recommended in pregnancy at modest doses. However, source purity matters: prenatal omega-3 should come from low-contaminant, third-party–tested products to avoid mercury and PCBs. Typical recommendations for general adult intake are 250–500 mg combined EPA+DHA daily, and some guidelines advise pregnant women to ensure ~200–300 mg of DHA daily; higher intakes should be supervised. For more on broader nutrient choices in pregnancy and supplement alternatives, see guidance on vegan vitamin D3 versus traditional supplements.

Dosage and monitoring

Adverse effects are more likely at higher doses. Regulatory bodies note that intakes up to about 5 g/day of EPA+DHA are generally tolerated in adults, but routine supplementation is usually effective at much lower doses (250–500 mg/day) and doses above 2–3 g/day should be managed by a clinician. Watch for prolonged bleeding, unexplained bruising, persistent gastrointestinal upset, or new allergy-like symptoms and report them to a provider.

In summary, omega-3 supplementation can be beneficial but is not appropriate for everyone. Decisions should be individualized, taking into account medication use, medical history, pregnancy status, and product quality, and made in consultation with a healthcare professional.
