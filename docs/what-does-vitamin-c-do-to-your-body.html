Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble nutrient essential for multiple biochemical processes. Because humans cannot synthesize or store it in large amounts, regular dietary intake is necessary to maintain physiologic functions. Beyond its reputation for immune support, vitamin C contributes to antioxidant defense, collagen production, iron absorption, and skin health.

Key physiological roles

Antioxidant protection: Vitamin C donates electrons to neutralize free radicals, reducing oxidative damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids. It also helps regenerate other antioxidants such as vitamin E and glutathione, supporting broader cellular defense systems.

Collagen synthesis and tissue repair: As a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase enzymes, vitamin C stabilizes collagen cross-links. Adequate vitamin C supports wound healing, vascular integrity, and connective tissue health in skin, cartilage, and bone.

Immune function: Immune cells accumulate vitamin C, which supports barrier function, phagocyte activity, and lymphocyte proliferation. Clinical reviews indicate that while vitamin C does not completely prevent common colds, supplementation can reduce symptom duration and severity in some populations, particularly those with low baseline levels or high physical stress.

Iron absorption: Vitamin C enhances the uptake of non-heme iron by reducing ferric to ferrous iron in the gut, improving bioavailability from plant-based foods. Combining iron-rich meals with vitamin C sources can be a practical strategy for individuals at risk of iron deficiency.

Forms, intake, and safety

Common supplemental forms include ascorbic acid, buffered formulations, and liposomal vitamin C, which may offer improved bioavailability. Recommended intakes vary by region and life stage; for adults in the EU the typical reference is around 80 mg/day, though some contexts use higher intakes for therapeutic support. Because vitamin C is water-soluble, excess is usually excreted, but very large doses can cause gastrointestinal upset. Consultation with a healthcare professional is advised before beginning high-dose supplementation.

Skin and aging

Vitamin C supports skin structure through collagen synthesis and antioxidant activity. Topical and oral approaches can be complementary: topical formulations target superficial oxidative damage and pigmentation, while oral intake provides systemic support for dermal repair. Consistent intake is associated with improved skin tone and reduced indicators of photoaging in clinical studies.

Practical takeaways

Maintain vitamin C through a varied diet rich in fruits and vegetables, and consider supplementation when dietary intake is insufficient or needs are increased (e.g., during illness, recovery, or elevated oxidative stress). Because vitamin C interacts with other nutrients and physiological systems, personalized guidance from a healthcare provider is recommended when addressing deficiency or using high-dose protocols.
