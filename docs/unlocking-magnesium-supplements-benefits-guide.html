Introduction

Magnesium is a fundamental mineral involved in hundreds of biochemical reactions, from energy production to nerve transmission and bone health. Modern diets, gastrointestinal issues, and certain medications can reduce magnesium intake or absorption, making supplementation a consideration for some individuals. This article summarizes when supplementation may be warranted, how to enhance absorption, dosing considerations, and how to choose an appropriate form.

When to Consider Supplementation

Signs that magnesium status may be suboptimal include muscle cramps, persistent fatigue, irritability, sleep disturbances, and palpitations. Certain groups face higher risk: athletes with high sweat losses, pregnant people with increased requirements, older adults with reduced absorption, and individuals with malabsorptive disorders. Laboratory testing such as serum magnesium, erythrocyte magnesium, or a magnesium loading test can help clarify status; discuss testing with a clinician before initiating prolonged high-dose supplementation. Maximizing Absorption

Bioavailability depends on both the chemical form and the gastrointestinal environment. Forms such as magnesium citrate and glycinate are better absorbed than magnesium oxide. Vitamin D status supports intestinal magnesium uptake, while very high doses of calcium or zinc taken simultaneously can compete for absorption. Gut health—affected by conditions like inflammatory bowel disease or chronic diarrhea—also impacts uptake; interventions that support the microbiome (dietary fiber, prebiotics, probiotics) may indirectly improve mineral status.

Practical Dosage Considerations

Recommended intakes vary by age and sex, commonly around 400–420 mg/day for adult men and 310–320 mg/day for adult women from all sources. Supplemental magnesium is often used at 200–400 mg/day for short-term correction under medical supervision. Because high supplemental doses can cause diarrhea and abdominal cramping, many guidelines recommend not exceeding 350 mg/day of supplemental magnesium without clinician oversight. Adjustments may be indicated for athletes, pregnant individuals, or those with impaired absorption.

Choosing the Right Form

Select a magnesium form aligned with your goals: citrate for occasional constipation and general repletion, glycinate for sleep and calm with low laxative effect, threonate when cognitive effects are a priority, and chloride or transdermal products for topical applications. Consider cost, tolerability, and any coexisting conditions or medications that could interact with magnesium.
