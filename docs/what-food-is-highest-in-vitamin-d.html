What Food Is Highest in Vitamin D?

Vitamin D is essential for bone health, immune function, and muscle performance, yet dietary sources are limited. Understanding which foods provide the most vitamin D helps individuals plan diets and determine when supplementation or fortified products are necessary. This article summarizes the highest natural sources, practical meal strategies, and where to find additional guidance.

Top natural sources

Fatty fish are consistently the richest natural sources. Wild-caught salmon, trout, mackerel, and sardines deliver substantial amounts of vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol), the form most effective at raising blood 25(OH)D concentrations. For example, a 100 g serving of wild salmon often provides several hundred to over 1,000 IU, depending on the species and season.

Cod liver oil is another concentrated source; a single tablespoon can supply well over 1,000 IU, but it also contains vitamin A, so intake should be balanced to avoid excess. Egg yolks contain modest amounts that increase if hens are pasture-raised or fed vitamin D–enriched diets. For plant-based diets, UV-exposed mushrooms offer vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol), which can meaningfully contribute to intake when appropriately treated.

Fortified foods and practical strategies

Because few foods naturally supply high vitamin D levels, fortification plays a critical role. Milk, plant-based milks, orange juice, and many breakfast cereals are commonly fortified to provide routine intake opportunities. Including fortified items at breakfast or with snacks can help bridge gaps, especially in winter or for individuals with limited sun exposure.

Combining diet and supplementation

Dietary sources plus sensible supplementation often produce the most reliable results. Supplements provide a consistent dose to correct or maintain adequate serum levels, particularly in northern latitudes, for older adults, or for people with malabsorption. When using supplements, vitamin D3 is generally preferred for its greater potency and longer duration in circulation compared with D2. Taking vitamin D with a fat-containing meal improves absorption because it is fat-soluble.

Who may need extra help?

People at risk of low vitamin D include older adults, individuals with limited sun exposure, people with darker skin, those who cover their skin for cultural reasons, and some individuals on plant-based diets. Practical resources on determining who might benefit from dietary supplements can be found in guidance about who needs dietary supplements.
