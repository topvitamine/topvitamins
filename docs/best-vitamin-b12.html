What is the best vitamin B12?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation and nervous system function. Determining the “best” B12 depends on absorption capacity, clinical need and dietary preference rather than a single universal product. This article summarizes the evidence on common B12 forms, absorption factors, at‑risk populations and practical considerations for choosing an appropriate supplement.

Forms of B12 and bioavailability

The two most commonly studied supplemental forms are methylcobalamin and cyanocobalamin. Methylcobalamin is a bioactive form incorporated directly into metabolic pathways involved in nerve function and methylation, which supports its theoretical advantage for neurological health. Cyanocobalamin is a synthetic, highly stable form that reliably raises serum B12 and is often used in clinical and public‑health settings because of predictable potency and lower cost. Hydroxocobalamin is another medically used form, particularly in injections, with a longer intramuscular half‑life.

Delivery methods and absorption

Absorption of B12 depends on intrinsic factor and healthy gastrointestinal function. Oral tablets require normal gastric acid and intrinsic factor to be optimally absorbed; therefore oral dosing may be less effective for people with pernicious anemia, gastric surgery or chronic proton pump inhibitor use. Sublingual lozenges and sprays can bypass some digestive barriers through mucosal uptake. Intramuscular injections deliver B12 directly to the systemic circulation and are the standard for severe deficiency or documented malabsorption.

Who should consider supplementation?

Groups at higher risk of deficiency include older adults (reduced gastric acid), vegans and strict vegetarians, individuals after bariatric surgery, and people taking long‑term metformin or acid‑reducing medications. Symptoms can range from fatigue and anemia to neuropathy and cognitive changes; laboratory markers such as serum B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine help clarify status.

Choosing the best option for you

For general maintenance in individuals with normal absorption, either methylcobalamin or cyanocobalamin at recommended doses will typically maintain adequate levels. For neurological symptoms or when tissue uptake is a primary concern, clinicians may prefer methylcobalamin. In cases of malabsorption or severe deficiency, supervised intramuscular therapy is most effective. Discuss testing and dosing with a healthcare professional to tailor choice and frequency.
