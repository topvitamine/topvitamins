Introduction

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble micronutrient with essential roles in vision, immune competence, cellular differentiation, and epithelial integrity. Understanding vitamin A—from biochemical forms to dietary sources and safety considerations—is foundational for evidence-based nutrition. For a focused overview of its properties and uses in supplementation, consult this comprehensive guide to vitamin A.

Forms and physiological roles

Vitamin A occurs as preformed retinoids (retinol, retinal, retinoic acid) in animal foods and as provitamin A carotenoids (notably beta-carotene) in plants. Retinal participates in the visual cycle by forming rhodopsin in photoreceptor cells, while retinoic acid regulates gene expression and cell differentiation. Beta-carotene provides antioxidant benefits and is converted to retinol according to physiological need.

Dietary sources and supplementation

Food sources of preformed vitamin A include liver, whole dairy, and eggs; provitamin A carotenoids are abundant in orange and dark-green vegetables such as carrots, sweet potatoes, spinach, and kale. Supplements offer measured doses as retinyl esters or as beta-carotene. When evaluating products, consider the form provided, the amount in micrograms retinol activity equivalents (RAE), and potential interactions with other nutrients.

Benefits and clinical considerations

Clinically, adequate vitamin A supports night vision, mucosal barrier function, and cellular immunity. Beta-carotene contributes antioxidant protection against oxidative stress, while retinoids are used therapeutically in dermatology for disorders of keratinization and photoaging. Populations with increased needs or impaired absorption—infants, pregnant or lactating women, people with fat-malabsorption syndromes, and some strict plant-based eaters—may require targeted assessment to ensure sufficiency.

Risks: deficiency and toxicity

Vitamin A deficiency can present with night blindness, xerosis, and increased infection risk; it remains a public health issue in some regions. Conversely, chronic excessive intake of preformed vitamin A causes hypervitaminosis A, with potential hepatotoxicity, increased intracranial pressure, and teratogenic risk in pregnancy. Regulatory bodies set tolerable upper intake levels; monitoring total intake from foods, fortified products, and supplements is important for safety.

Conclusion

Vitamin A occupies a central role in nutrition science, with distinct forms that influence both benefit and risk. Evidence-based choices—grounded in knowledge of dietary sources, biochemical activity, and product quality—support safe and effective intake.
