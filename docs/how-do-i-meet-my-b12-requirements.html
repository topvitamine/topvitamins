Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, neurological function, and DNA synthesis. Because it occurs naturally only in animal-derived foods, people with restricted diets, older adults, and those with gastrointestinal disorders are at increased risk of inadequate intake or poor absorption. This article summarizes practical, evidence-based approaches to meet B12 requirements through diet, fortified foods, testing, and appropriate supplementation.

Know the recommended intake and who is at risk

Recommended dietary allowances vary by age and physiological status: adults generally require about 2.4 µg/day, with modestly higher amounts during pregnancy and lactation. Populations with elevated risk include vegans, strict vegetarians, older adults with reduced gastric acidity, and people with conditions such as pernicious anemia, celiac disease, or prior gastrointestinal surgery. Regular monitoring is prudent for these groups, and tests such as serum B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA), and homocysteine provide the best assessment of functional status.

Prioritize reliable dietary sources

Natural, bioavailable sources of B12 include shellfish (clams, oysters), organ meats, oily fish, beef, eggs, and dairy. For those who consume these foods regularly, dietary intake may be sufficient. For people following plant-based diets, fortified foods—such as fortified plant milks, fortified cereals, and nutritional yeast—are practical sources. When reviewing packaged foods, check labels for the type and amount of B12 provided.

Choose supplements appropriately

Supplementation is often necessary for vegans, older adults, or anyone with malabsorption. Common oral forms include cyanocobalamin and methylcobalamin; methylcobalamin is a bioactive form, while cyanocobalamin requires metabolic conversion. Oral doses in supplements frequently exceed the RDA to account for limited absorption, and alternative delivery routes (sublingual, nasal, or intramuscular injections) are used when gastrointestinal absorption is impaired. Work with a healthcare provider to determine dose and format, and monitor response with laboratory testing.

Optimize absorption and consider interacting factors

B12 absorption depends on stomach acid and intrinsic factor. Factors that reduce absorption include long-term use of proton pump inhibitors or antacids, autoimmune destruction of intrinsic factor, alcohol use, and gastric surgery. Strategies to support absorption include taking supplements with a meal when appropriate and addressing modifiable contributors (for example, reviewing acid-reducing medication use with a clinician). In confirmed malabsorption, parenteral or high-dose oral regimens bypassing standard absorption pathways may be indicated.

Monitoring and prevention

Preventive care includes routine risk assessment and periodic testing for at-risk individuals. Early symptoms of deficiency—fatigue, cognitive changes, paresthesia, and anemia—warrant prompt evaluation.
