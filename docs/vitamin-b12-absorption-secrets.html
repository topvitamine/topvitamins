Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, neurological function, and DNA synthesis. Despite its importance, absorption is complex and can be disrupted by diet, gastrointestinal conditions, medications, and age. This article summarizes the physiological steps of B12 uptake, key factors that affect absorption, reliable vegan sources, and evidence-based supplementation methods to maintain adequate status.

How B12 Is Absorbed

Dietary B12 is typically bound to protein in food. In the stomach, gastric acid and pepsin release B12, which then binds to intrinsic factor (IF), a glycoprotein secreted by parietal cells. The IF–B12 complex is recognized by receptors in the ileum and actively transported into enterocytes. A small proportion of very high oral doses can be absorbed passively without IF via diffusion, which underpins some high-dose oral supplementation strategies.

Factors That Reduce Absorption

Several conditions impair normal absorption: atrophic gastritis, long-term use of proton pump inhibitors or H2 blockers, bariatric surgery, ileal resection, inflammatory bowel disease, and autoimmune pernicious anemia that reduces IF production. Age-related hypochlorhydria (low stomach acid) is common in older adults and can reduce B12 release from food. Interactions with other nutrients and antibiotics affecting gut flora may also influence status.

Intrinsic Factor and Clinical Considerations

Intrinsic factor is central to physiological B12 uptake; without it, oral B12 bound to IF cannot be absorbed efficiently. In cases of IF deficiency or pernicious anemia, intramuscular or high-dose oral therapy circumvents the defect. Clinical assessment commonly includes serum B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA), and homocysteine measurements to clarify functional deficiency and guide treatment choices.

Vegan Sources and Supplementation

Natural plant sources of bioavailable B12 are limited. Vegans should rely on fortified foods (plant milks, cereals, nutritional yeast) or supplements containing cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin. Because passive absorption of high oral doses is inefficient (roughly ~1% of a large dose), recommended supplemental regimens often provide higher amounts to ensure adequate uptake. Supplement Delivery Methods

Common methods include oral tablets, sublingual preparations, intramuscular injections, and emerging transdermal or nasal options. Oral high-dose supplements are effective for many with intact gut function; injections are preferred when IF is absent or when rapid repletion is required. Monitoring and Practical Tips

Assessment of symptoms (fatigue, neuropathy, cognitive changes) together with laboratory tests informs treatment. Regular monitoring after initiating therapy ensures targets are met and helps adjust dosing. When choosing supplements, consider personal absorption risks, medication interactions, and guidance from healthcare professionals rather than relying solely on labeled claims. Conclusion

Effective management of vitamin B12 status requires understanding absorption mechanisms, identifying risk factors, and selecting appropriate supplementation routes. Clinical testing and individualized approaches remain the best means to prevent deficiency and its consequences.
