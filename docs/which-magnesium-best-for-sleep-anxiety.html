Which magnesium is best for sleep and anxiety?

Magnesium supports nervous system function, muscle relaxation, and sleep regulation through effects on GABA, melatonin synthesis, and stress hormone modulation. Evidence from randomized trials and clinical reviews suggests magnesium supplementation can improve sleep quality and reduce symptoms of mild anxiety, but the form of magnesium matters for absorption, tolerability, and specific outcomes.

Comparing common forms

Magnesium glycinate is often recommended for sleep and anxiety because it combines magnesium with the amino acid glycine, which itself has calming properties and is well absorbed with minimal laxative effect. Magnesium threonate has attracted interest for its ability to cross the blood–brain barrier and may offer cognitive and emotional benefits, while magnesium taurate pairs magnesium with taurine and is sometimes used for nervous system and cardiovascular support.

Magnesium citrate is well absorbed but may cause loose stools in sensitive people; magnesium oxide has low bioavailability and is primarily useful as an antacid or laxative rather than for sleep or anxiety support. Topical forms such as magnesium chloride can provide localized muscle relaxation but yield variable systemic absorption.

Mechanisms relevant to sleep and anxiety

Magnesium influences GABAergic signaling, helps regulate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and contributes to melatonin metabolism—pathways that are directly relevant to sleep onset, sleep architecture, and stress resilience. Clinical studies, including trials in older adults, have reported improvements in sleep time and efficiency with magnesium supplementation, although responses vary by baseline status, dose, and formulation.

Practical guidance

For sleep, many clinicians suggest taking a glycinate form 1–2 hours before bedtime; typical supplemental elemental magnesium intakes used in studies range from about 200–400 mg per day for adults. Start with a lower dose to assess tolerance, especially if using citrate or oxide forms that can affect bowel habits. If you take multiple supplements, be mindful of interactions—magnesium can affect absorption of certain medications and minerals.

Safety note

Magnesium supplementation is generally safe within recommended ranges but can interact with medications (e.g., certain antibiotics, bisphosphonates) and may cause gastrointestinal side effects. Consult a healthcare professional for personalized dosing, especially for people with kidney disease, pregnant people, or those taking prescription medications.
