What are the symptoms of magnesium deficiency?

Magnesium is an essential mineral that supports hundreds of biochemical reactions in the body, including energy production, muscle and nerve function, and regulation of heart rhythm. Deficiency is relatively common and can present with diverse, often subtle symptoms that overlap with other conditions. This article summarizes the most characteristic signs, the physiological basis, and how to explore further.

Common clinical signs

Muscle-related symptoms are among the most frequent manifestations. Individuals with low magnesium may experience muscle cramps, spasms, tremors, or persistent tightness. Night-time leg cramps and occasional eyelid twitches are typical early indicators. These arise because magnesium helps regulate calcium and potassium movement across cell membranes, which is critical for normal muscle contraction and relaxation.

Fatigue and low energy are another hallmark. Magnesium is required for the production and activation of ATP, the cellular energy molecule. When magnesium is insufficient, cellular energy metabolism becomes less efficient, leading to persistent tiredness, reduced exercise tolerance, and cognitive symptoms such as brain fog or difficulty concentrating.

Cardiac and neurological signals

Magnesium plays a central role in cardiac electrophysiology. Deficiency can contribute to palpitations, skipped beats, or more significant arrhythmias in susceptible individuals. These symptoms warrant medical assessment, particularly when accompanied by dizziness or shortness of breath.

Neurologically, low magnesium is associated with increased nervous system excitability, which may present as anxiety, irritability, mood swings, or sleep disturbances. Magnesium influences GABAergic neurotransmission and melatonin regulation, mechanisms that link it to sleep quality and stress responses.

Who is at risk and why it matters

Certain groups are more vulnerable: older adults, people with gastrointestinal disorders that impair absorption, those on long-term diuretics or proton pump inhibitors, individuals with alcoholism, athletes with high sweat loss, and pregnant women. Dietary patterns that rely heavily on processed foods can also reduce magnesium intake because of lower mineral content in refined foods and depleted agricultural soils.

Assessment and management

Diagnosis typically combines clinical assessment with laboratory tests. Serum magnesium can be measured, but most magnesium is intracellular or in bone, so normal serum levels do not always exclude deficiency. Clinicians may evaluate symptoms, dietary history, medication use, and, if needed, more specialized tests.

Management focuses on correcting underlying causes and ensuring adequate intake through diet and, when appropriate, supplementation. Food sources high in magnesium include leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes, and whole grains. If supplements are considered, discussing options and dosing with a healthcare professional is recommended.
