Introduction

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble micronutrient essential for vision, immune function, and cell differentiation. Assessing vitamin A status is important for people with symptoms such as night blindness, recurrent infections, or for those with medical conditions that impair absorption. Testing is also relevant for individuals who use dietary supplements, since recent intake can influence blood measurements and complicate interpretation.

Which tests are used?

The primary laboratory measure is the serum retinol test, which quantifies circulating retinol and is widely used to screen for deficiency. Because serum retinol is homeostatically maintained until liver stores are depleted, clinicians sometimes supplement this test with Retinol Binding Protein (RBP) or liver function panels to improve diagnostic accuracy. In specific contexts, functional assessments (for example, clinical evaluation for nyctalopia or conjunctival impression cytology) may complement biochemical testing.

Timing and supplement use

Recent supplementation can transiently raise serum retinol. To reduce this effect, clinicians often ask patients to avoid vitamin A supplements for several days prior to testing, if medically appropriate. When planning tests, report all supplements and fortified foods to the laboratory or provider so results can be interpreted in context. Guidance on reviewing product information and labels can be useful; see this supplement labeling guide for practical considerations.

Interpreting results

Serum retinol concentrations are typically reported in micrograms per deciliter (mcg/dL). Values below commonly used cutoffs (often cited as <20 mcg/dL) indicate deficiency, with intermediate values considered marginal. Interpretation should consider inflammation, protein status, and liver disease, all of which can depress circulating retinol independent of stores. Providers may order inflammatory markers (such as CRP) alongside retinol to distinguish true deficiency from inflammatory redistribution of vitamin A.

Comprehensive assessment

A full vitamin A status assessment combines biochemical tests, clinical signs, dietary history, and risk factors (e.g., malabsorption, bariatric surgery, chronic liver disease). For people uncertain about multiple potential deficiencies, resources on recognizing nutrient shortfalls can help prioritize testing; a practical overview is available on identifying vitamin deficiencies and a complementary summary on how to identify which vitamins you may be lacking.

When to repeat testing

After initiating dietary changes or guided supplementation, many clinicians recommend rechecking serum retinol and related markers within 3–6 months to confirm improvement or to detect excessive accumulation. Reassessment intervals depend on baseline severity, underlying causes, and the form and dose of any supplements used.

Summary

Serum retinol is the standard screening tool but has limitations; combining biochemical measures with clinical assessment and dietary review provides the most reliable evaluation. Disclose supplement use before testing and follow clinician advice on timing and follow-up to ensure accurate diagnosis and safe management.
