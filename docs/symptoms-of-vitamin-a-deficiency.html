Introduction

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble nutrient essential for vision, immune function, epithelial integrity, and cellular communication. Deficiency can arise from low dietary intake, malabsorption, liver disorders, or increased physiological demand. Early recognition of signs allows timely nutritional or clinical intervention and can prevent progression to severe complications.

Key ocular signs and night blindness

One of the earliest and most specific signs of insufficiency is impaired adaptation to low light (night blindness). This occurs because vitamin A derivatives are required to regenerate rhodopsin in retinal rod cells. Patients commonly report difficulty driving at night, longer adjustment times in dim environments, or blurred vision under low illumination. Progressive deficiency may produce conjunctival dryness, Bitot’s spots (foamy plaques on the conjunctiva), corneal ulceration, and in extreme cases, keratomalacia with risk of permanent visual loss.

Immune and mucosal effects

Vitamin A maintains mucosal barriers and supports both innate and adaptive immune responses. Insufficient stores can manifest as increased susceptibility to respiratory and gastrointestinal infections, prolonged recovery from illnesses, and delayed wound healing. In vulnerable groups—infants, pregnant women, and those with malabsorption—these effects contribute substantially to morbidity.

Skin, hair and epithelial symptoms

Because vitamin A regulates epithelial cell growth and differentiation, deficiency often shows on skin and hair. Typical findings include dry, scaly skin, follicular hyperkeratosis (rough bumps from plugged follicles), flaky scalp, and increased hair shedding. These dermatologic signs often improve after nutritional repletion once underlying causes are addressed.

Early vision changes and functional impact

Beyond night blindness, other early visual complaints can include photophobia, difficulty focusing in changing light, and a general decline in visual acuity, particularly under dim conditions. Such symptoms may be subtle but affect daily tasks and safety; routine eye examinations and assessment of dietary intake can help detect insufficiency before irreversible damage occurs.

Assessment and the role of supplementation

Diagnosis typically combines clinical signs with dietary and medical history; serum retinol measurement can aid assessment but has limitations in acute illness. In many settings, targeted supplementation or dietary modification is effective in reversing early symptoms. It is important to balance treatment because excessive intake of preformed vitamin A carries toxicity risk. Evidence-based guidance and safe formulations should be followed.

Conclusion

Vitamin A deficiency manifests across vision, immunity, and epithelial tissues. Early symptoms—night blindness, dry eyes, recurrent infections, and rough skin—should prompt dietary evaluation and clinical assessment. Interventions should be evidence-based and mindful of safety limits.
