Introduction

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble micronutrient that supports multiple physiological systems including vision, immune competence, skin integrity, cellular differentiation, and antioxidant protection. Deficiency can impair night vision, increase infection risk, and slow tissue repair, while balanced intake as part of a varied diet helps maintain normal function across life stages.

Vision and ocular function

Vitamin A is a key component of visual pigments in the retina. Its aldehyde form, retinal, combines with opsin proteins to form rhodopsin, which enables vision in low-light conditions. Adequate vitamin A also preserves the health of the cornea and conjunctival membranes; prolonged deficiency can result in xerophthalmia and irreversible visual loss in severe cases.

Immune support

At a cellular level, vitamin A modulates both innate and adaptive immunity. It maintains epithelial and mucosal barriers that act as first-line defenses and influences the differentiation and function of lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells. In populations with low baseline vitamin A status, supplementation has been associated with reductions in morbidity and mortality from infectious diseases, especially among young children.

Skin health and tissue repair

Vitamin A regulates epithelial cell proliferation and differentiation, which is central to maintaining skin structure and accelerating wound healing. Retinoids—topical and systemic derivatives of vitamin A—modify gene expression to reduce comedone formation, regulate sebum, and stimulate collagen production, contributing to improvements in acne and photoaged skin when used appropriately.

Cell growth, development, and reproductive health

Through nuclear retinoic acid receptors, vitamin A influences gene transcription pathways that guide embryonic development, organogenesis, and bone growth. Both deficiency and excess during pregnancy can have significant consequences; therefore, clinical guidance is important to ensure safe and sufficient intake during gestation.

Antioxidant roles

Provitamin A carotenoids, such as beta-carotene, contribute to antioxidant defenses by quenching free radicals and protecting lipid membranes from peroxidation. While preformed retinol has more limited direct antioxidant activity, carotenoids support redox balance as part of a broader antioxidant network with vitamins C and E.

Practical considerations and further reading

Vitamin A is available as preformed retinol from animal sources and as provitamin A carotenoids from plants; the latter are converted to active vitamin A by the body according to need, reducing toxicity risk. EFSA and other authorities provide age- and life-stage-specific intake recommendations and upper limits to avoid adverse effects.
