Introduction

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in more than 300 enzymatic reactions in the human body. It supports energy production, nerve transmission, muscle contraction and relaxation, and the maintenance of normal heart rhythm. Because a large proportion of total body magnesium is stored in bone and muscle tissue rather than blood, intake and tissue status are often more informative than a single serum measurement.

How magnesium works and dietary needs

Biologically, magnesium acts as a cofactor for enzymes that synthesize ATP, replicate DNA and regulate neurotransmitters. Recommended intakes vary by age, sex and life stage; many adults require roughly 300–420 mg daily depending on guidelines. Whole foods are the primary source, and habitual consumption of processed foods and declines in soil mineral content have contributed to marginal intakes in some populations.

Signs and causes of deficiency

Magnesium deficiency can develop from inadequate dietary intake, impaired absorption (for example, in some gastrointestinal disorders), or increased losses (diuretics, excessive alcohol use, uncontrolled diabetes). Early signs are often nonspecific—fatigue, muscle cramps, weakness, and paresthesia—while more severe deficiency may lead to arrhythmias, neuromuscular irritability and metabolic disturbances.

Forms of supplements and practical considerations

Supplemental magnesium comes in several chemical forms with differing absorption and tolerability. Magnesium glycinate is often preferred for minimizing gastrointestinal effects and may aid sleep and relaxation. Magnesium citrate and oxide have a greater laxative effect; citrate is more bioavailable than oxide. Magnesium threonate is under investigation for potential cognitive effects due to central nervous system penetration, while malate is sometimes chosen for muscle and energy support. When considering supplementation, interaction with medications and kidney function should be assessed, and doses from supplements are commonly discussed relative to tolerable upper intake levels set by regulatory bodies.

Dietary sources and meal strategies

Rich dietary sources include leafy greens (spinach, chard), nuts and seeds (almonds, pumpkin seeds), legumes, whole grains, and fatty fish. Combining magnesium-rich foods with nutrients that support absorption—such as vitamin D—can be beneficial. Health benefits beyond muscles

Beyond muscle function and recovery, magnesium contributes to cardiovascular stability, bone health through its role in calcium and vitamin D metabolism, and neurological function including mood regulation. Emerging research examines effects on migraine frequency, premenstrual symptoms and insulin sensitivity. Conclusion

Magnesium is a widely relevant mineral with roles across metabolic, neuromuscular and cardiovascular systems.
