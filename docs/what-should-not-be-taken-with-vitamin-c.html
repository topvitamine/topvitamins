What Should Not Be Taken With Vitamin C?

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a common, water-soluble nutrient valued for immune support and antioxidant activity. While generally safe at recommended intakes, it can interact with certain medications and supplements. For a focused overview of interactions and contraindications, see this article on what should not be taken with vitamin C.

Medicine interactions to watch

Some pharmaceuticals can be affected by vitamin C. Anticoagulants such as warfarin may have altered effects when users take high-dose vitamin C, potentially changing clotting risk. Evidence is mixed, but clinicians often recommend monitoring clotting times if supplements are introduced.

Chemotherapy is another area of concern: antioxidants can theoretically reduce oxidative mechanisms used by some cytotoxic drugs. Oncologists frequently advise against high-dose antioxidant supplementation during active chemotherapy unless coordinated within the treatment plan.

Vitamin C can influence urinary pH and renal excretion, which may change the clearance of certain antibiotics and other drugs that are eliminated via the kidneys. In diabetes management, very large doses may interfere with some glucose monitoring assays, producing inaccurate readings.

When supplementation is contraindicated

Specific medical conditions increase risk from excess vitamin C. People with hemochromatosis should avoid supplemental vitamin C because it enhances iron absorption and can exacerbate iron overload. Those with a history of calcium oxalate kidney stones may also be advised to limit supplemental vitamin C, since ascorbate is metabolized to oxalate.

Rare conditions such as glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency require caution: high doses of oxidant compounds can, in some cases, precipitate hemolysis. Individuals with severe renal impairment or on dialysis should consult nephrology before taking extra vitamin C because clearance and metabolite handling are altered.

Supplement and herb interactions

Vitamin C is frequently combined with multivitamins and other antioxidants. While low-to-moderate combinations are typically safe, overlapping high doses can create imbalances and competitive absorption issues. Herbal products that affect blood thinning (for example, ginkgo) or liver metabolism may change drug and nutrient kinetics when used concurrently.

Before adding vitamin C to a complex supplement routine, review product labels and consider spacing doses away from certain medications. For broader reading on combining supplements and daily multivitamin use, consult resources on taking multivitamins every day and an external perspective at Is it Good to Take Multivitamins Every Day?

Practical safety guidance

Follow established dietary guidelines: the RDA for most adults is 75–90 mg/day with a tolerable upper intake level of 2,000 mg/day. Start at the lowest effective dose, report supplement use to your healthcare provider, and have drug levels or clinical markers monitored when initiating or stopping supplements that might interact.
