Introduction

Vitamin D is essential for bone health, immune function, and calcium balance. While sunlight triggers vitamin D synthesis in skin, many people need dietary sources year-round—especially in higher latitudes or when time outdoors is limited. Below is a concise, evidence-focused overview of 10 foods that reliably contribute vitamin D to the diet, plus practical notes on bioavailability and fortification.

Top 10 Foods Rich in Vitamin D

  • Salmon (wild and farmed): Wild-caught salmon typically provides the highest amounts (several hundred IU per 100 g), with farmed salmon delivering lower but still meaningful levels. Fatty salmon also supplies omega-3s that support cardiovascular and bone health.
  • Mackerel: A fatty fish high in vitamin D and EPA/DHA. Regular consumption of mackerel contributes substantially to weekly vitamin D intake.
  • Sardines (canned): Small oily fish that are convenient, shelf-stable, and rich in vitamin D and calcium—helpful for maintaining bone density.
  • Tuna (canned light): A practical, long-lasting option offering moderate vitamin D per serving; larger predatory tuna should be consumed in moderation due to mercury concerns.
  • Cod liver oil: One of the most concentrated sources of vitamin D available in supplement form; also supplies vitamin A and omega-3s. Dose according to product labeling and clinical guidance.
  • Egg yolks: Contain vitamin D3, with amounts influenced by hens’ diet and sun exposure. Pasture-raised or vitamin-D–enriched eggs yield higher levels.
  • Shrimp and shellfish: Lower than fatty fish but still useful contributors; oysters provide vitamin D plus zinc and selenium for overall nutrient balance.
  • UV-exposed mushrooms: The only reliable plant-based natural source; when mushrooms are treated with UV light, ergosterol converts to vitamin D2. Select products labeled as UV-exposed for meaningful amounts.
  • Fortified dairy and plant milks: Many milks (cow and fortified plant alternatives) supply added vitamin D per cup, often paired with calcium—an effective combination for bone health.
  • Fortified cereals and orange juice: These staples are often enriched with vitamin D to help close dietary gaps, though individual product levels vary and should be checked on labels.

Practical considerations

Vitamin D exists as D2 (ergocalciferol) and D3 (cholecalciferol); D3 from animal sources is generally more effective at raising serum 25(OH)D, while D2 from UV-treated mushrooms remains useful for plant-based diets. For many people, combining natural sources, fortified foods, and measured supplementation achieves stable status. Professional guidance and blood testing help tailor intake, especially for older adults, people with darker skin, or those with limited sun exposure.
