Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential water-soluble nutrient that supports collagen synthesis, iron absorption and antioxidant defense. Because humans cannot synthesize vitamin C, dietary sources matter. Many fruits supply substantial amounts of vitamin C alongside complementary phytochemicals, and understanding relative concentrations helps guide dietary choices for immune and skin health.

Which fruit contains the most vitamin C?

Among commonly cited fruits, acerola cherries (Malpighia emarginata) are consistently reported as the richest natural source of vitamin C, with values often ranging from roughly 1,500 to 2,000 mg per 100 g in fresh fruit. In the context of available literature and nutrient databases, other exceptionally high sources include camu camu and concentrated extracts. High-content fruits to consider

Aside from acerola and camu camu, several fruits offer notable vitamin C per 100 g: guava (~228 mg), blackcurrants (~180 mg), kiwi (~92 mg), strawberries (~59 mg) and oranges (~50–53 mg). Portion size matters: a medium orange or a cup of strawberries can provide a large fraction of the typical adult requirement (about 75–90 mg per day for many adults). Concentrated powders and extracts are commonly used when fresh sources are unavailable, but whole fruits also deliver fiber and additional antioxidants.

Antioxidant synergies and practical considerations

Vitamin C often works synergistically with polyphenols, carotenoids and anthocyanins found in berries, pomegranate and other fruits. These compounds help modulate oxidative stress and inflammation. Cooking and prolonged storage degrade vitamin C, so fresh or minimally processed fruit preserves the most activity. When fresh high-C fruits are impractical, properly formulated extracts can maintain potency, but label literacy is important for assessing ingredient quality—learn more about supplement labeling to evaluate such products.

Assessing personal needs

Individual vitamin C needs vary with age, smoking status, pregnancy and health conditions. If you suspect deficiency or have symptoms such as fatigue or easy bruising, guidance on how to identify vitamin deficiencies can be helpful. Summary

In summary, acerola cherries are among the richest natural sources of vitamin C, with several other fruits—camu camu, guava, blackcurrant, kiwi and strawberries—also providing high amounts per 100 g. Eating a diverse range of fresh fruits maximizes intake of vitamin C and complementary antioxidants, while attention to storage, preparation and credible product information supports effective and evidence-based nutrition choices.
