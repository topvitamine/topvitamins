What are the signs of vitamin C deficiency?

Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is essential for collagen synthesis, antioxidant defenses, immune function, and iron absorption. Deficiency is uncommon in well-nourished populations but can develop gradually due to poor diet, malabsorption, tobacco use, or increased physiological demands. Early recognition of signs allows for corrective dietary changes and, when appropriate, targeted supplementation.

Common early signs

Mild or subclinical vitamin C deficiency often presents with nonspecific symptoms that can be misattributed to stress or aging. Typical early indicators include fatigue, irritability, and general weakness. Dermatologic and wound-healing signs are common: easy bruising, slow wound healing, dry or rough skin, and brittle hair. These symptoms reflect impaired collagen formation and reduced antioxidant protection.

Oral, musculoskeletal, and systemic manifestations

As levels decline further, more specific findings appear. Bleeding or swollen gums, gum recession, and loose teeth are characteristic because collagen supports oral connective tissues and capillaries. Musculoskeletal complaints such as joint pain, swelling, and tenderness may arise from weakened connective tissues. Systemically, deficiency can contribute to iron-deficiency anemia by reducing non-heme iron absorption, producing symptoms like pallor and lightheadedness.

Severe deficiency and scurvy

Prolonged, severe deficiency results in scurvy, a clinical syndrome historically linked to sailors without access to fresh produce. Scurvy features widespread capillary fragility (petechiae and large ecchymoses), significant gum disease, delayed wound healing, anemia, and characteristic corkscrew-shaped body hairs. Left untreated, scurvy can lead to secondary infections and other complications, but signs typically improve after restoring adequate vitamin C intake.

Why deficiency occurs and who is at risk

Inadequate intake of fruits and vegetables is the primary driver, but malabsorption disorders (for example, inflammatory bowel disease), restrictive eating patterns, heavy smoking, and high physiological stress can increase requirements or reduce availability. Older adults with limited food access and individuals with substance use or certain chronic illnesses may also be at elevated risk. Assessment and management

Assessment typically begins with dietary review and clinical examination; blood testing can measure plasma ascorbic acid in uncertain cases. Mild deficiency often responds to dietary correction—more citrus fruits, berries, peppers, and leafy greens—and to supplemental ascorbic acid when needed. Conclusion

Vitamin C deficiency ranges from subtle fatigue and skin changes to overt scurvy with bleeding gums and impaired healing. Recognizing early signs and addressing dietary gaps or absorption issues can prevent progression.
