Who Should Not Be Taking Magnesium?

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of enzymatic reactions, including muscle contraction, nerve signaling, and energy metabolism. While dietary magnesium is generally safe, supplemental magnesium can pose risks for specific populations. This article outlines who should avoid or use caution with magnesium supplements, common adverse effects, and practical precautions to reduce harm.

People with impaired renal function are at highest risk from supplemental magnesium. The kidneys excrete excess magnesium; when renal clearance is reduced, serum magnesium can accumulate and cause hypermagnesemia. Symptoms range from nausea and flushing to hypotension, diminished reflexes, respiratory depression, and potentially life-threatening cardiac abnormalities. Anyone with chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, or advanced age with suspected reduced glomerular filtration should not self-prescribe magnesium supplements without clinical monitoring.

Certain neuromuscular and cardiovascular conditions also merit caution. Individuals with heart block or conduction abnormalities can experience exaggerated bradycardia when magnesium levels rise. Those with neuromuscular disorders such as myasthenia gravis may notice worsened muscle weakness, since magnesium influences neuromuscular transmission. For these groups, supplementation should only occur under specialist guidance.

Gastrointestinal disorders that affect absorption or increase losses can complicate magnesium status. Conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, chronic diarrhea, short bowel syndrome, or recent gastrointestinal surgery may alter magnesium handling, leading either to deficiency or to unpredictable systemic exposure when using oral supplements. In these cases a clinician can recommend appropriate testing and dosing.

Magnesium interacts with several medication classes. Oral magnesium can bind to certain antibiotics (notably tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones) and reduce their absorption, so clinicians typically advise separating doses by several hours. Diuretics alter electrolyte balance and may either deplete or increase magnesium levels depending on the type used. Magnesium can also lower absorption of bisphosphonates and levothyroxine. Because of these interactions, review of all medications with a healthcare professional is important before starting supplements.

Pregnant and breastfeeding people should consult their obstetric provider prior to supplementation. While magnesium plays a role in fetal development and may be used therapeutically in some obstetric contexts, routine supplementation should be individualized to maternal and fetal needs and current evidence.

Common adverse effects of supplemental magnesium are primarily gastrointestinal: abdominal cramping, nausea, and osmotic diarrhea. These tend to be product- and dose-dependent; certain forms (for example, magnesium oxide or citrate) are more likely to cause laxative effects. More serious toxicity is uncommon in individuals with normal renal function but can occur with excessive dosing or with compounded intake from multiple sources.

Practical precautions include starting at a low supplemental dose, avoiding simultaneous use of multiple magnesium-containing products (including some antacids and laxatives), and arranging periodic laboratory monitoring if risk factors are present. Careful dosing and medical oversight minimize risks and support safe use.
