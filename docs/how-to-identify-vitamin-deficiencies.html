How do I know which vitamins I am lacking?

Maintaining adequate vitamin levels is essential for energy, immunity, cognitive function and long-term health. Symptoms of deficiency are often nonspecific — fatigue, skin or hair changes, recurrent infections, cognitive fog, and digestive complaints can all signal a nutrient gap. This article summarizes common signs, practical diagnostic steps, and evidence-based ways to determine which vitamins you may be lacking.

Common signs that suggest specific deficiencies

Some symptoms correlate with particular vitamins, although overlap is frequent and testing is required for confirmation:

  • Persistent fatigue and weakness: often associated with low vitamin B12, iron, or vitamin D.
  • Skin, hair and nail changes: biotin (B7), vitamin A, zinc and essential fatty acids play roles in skin integrity and hair growth.
  • Frequent infections or slow wound healing: vitamin C, vitamin D and zinc influence immune function and tissue repair.
  • Cognitive changes and mood disturbances: deficiencies in B12, folate (B9) and B6 can affect memory and mood.
  • Bone and muscle weakness: vitamin D and vitamin K are critical for calcium metabolism and bone health.
  • Oral symptoms: bleeding gums or an inflamed tongue may reflect low vitamin C or K status.
  • Night vision issues: classic for vitamin A insufficiency in more severe cases.

How to determine which vitamins you lack

Self-diagnosis based on symptoms alone is unreliable. Laboratory testing is the most objective method: common assessments include serum 25(OH)D for vitamin D, serum B12 and methylmalonic acid for B12 status, folate, ferritin for iron stores, and relevant mineral panels. If you have GI symptoms or a history of malabsorption, tests for intrinsic factor antibodies, celiac serology, or a broader nutrient panel may be indicated.

Keeping a short food and symptom diary before clinical evaluation helps clinicians interpret results in context. For targeted guidance on vitamin testing and interpretation, see this practical guide on how to identify vitamin deficiencies.

If testing confirms a deficiency, interventions usually combine diet optimization and, when appropriate, targeted supplementation at clinically recommended doses. Addressing underlying causes (for example, malabsorption or medication interactions) is essential to restore and maintain levels.

Prevention and monitoring

A varied diet rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, lean proteins and fortified foods reduces risk for most deficiencies. Regular follow-up testing is advisable when supplements are started, particularly for fat-soluble vitamins and minerals, to avoid under- or over-replacement.
