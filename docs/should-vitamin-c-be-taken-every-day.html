Should vitamin C be taken every day?

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble nutrient with well-documented roles in collagen synthesis, antioxidant defense, and immune function. Because the body cannot store vitamin C, regular intake is necessary to maintain adequate plasma levels. Whether that intake should come from food, supplements, or a combination depends on diet, lifestyle, and individual health factors.

When daily supplementation is reasonable

People who eat a variety of fruits and vegetables typically meet recommended intakes, but certain groups may benefit from daily supplementation. Smokers, older adults, people with restricted diets, and individuals under high physical or environmental stress often have higher needs. Consistent, moderate supplementation can help maintain steady levels that support wound healing, skin health, and immune cell function.

Immune and antioxidant effects

Vitamin C contributes to innate and adaptive immunity and acts as an antioxidant by neutralizing free radicals produced during normal metabolism and from environmental exposures. Clinical evidence indicates that regular vitamin C supplementation (commonly around 200 mg/day in trials) can modestly reduce the duration and severity of common cold symptoms for some people, particularly those exposed to extreme physical stress.

Practical dosing and timing

Authoritative recommendations vary by region: for adults, typical Population Reference Intakes are about 80–90 mg/day, with increased amounts advised for pregnant or lactating people. Smokers are often advised to add roughly 35 mg/day. Because intestinal absorption of ascorbic acid is limited in a single dose, spreading intake into smaller amounts (for example, up to ~200 mg per dose) can improve absorption and minimize urinary loss. Liposomal or time-release formulations are alternatives that may extend circulating levels.

Safety considerations

Vitamin C is generally safe, but high intakes can cause gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhea or cramps. The commonly cited Tolerable Upper Intake Level is 2000 mg/day; exceeding this increases the risk of adverse effects and may contribute to kidney stone formation in susceptible individuals. People with certain medical conditions or who take specific medications should consult a clinician before starting high-dose supplementation.

Combining nutrients and broader context

A balanced approach favors obtaining vitamin C from food first—citrus fruits, berries, kiwi, bell peppers, broccoli, and leafy greens are rich sources—supplementing when dietary intake is insufficient. Because nutrient needs interact, considering other micronutrients can be useful: for example, vitamin C enhances non-heme iron absorption. Conclusion

Daily vitamin C intake is appropriate for many people, particularly when dietary intake is limited or needs are elevated. Moderate, consistent dosing aligned with established guidelines and individualized to health status is a pragmatic approach. Discussing any supplementation plan with a healthcare professional can help align dose and formulation with personal risk factors and goals.
