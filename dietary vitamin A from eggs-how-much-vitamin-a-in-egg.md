## Introduction

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble micronutrient essential for vision, immune function, skin integrity and reproduction. Animal-derived foods supply preformed vitamin A (retinol), which is more bioavailable than plant carotenoids. Eggs—especially the yolk—are a convenient source of dietary retinol and can contribute meaningfully to daily intake without relying solely on supplements. For more detail on this topic, see the full discussion of <a href='https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/how-much-vitamin-a-in-egg'>vitamin A in eggs</a>.

## How much vitamin A is in an egg?

Vitamin A is concentrated in the yolk. A medium chicken egg (~50 g) typically provides about 245 IU (≈75 µg RAE); a large egg often contains roughly 270 IU (≈81 µg RAE). The albumen (egg white) contributes virtually no vitamin A. Compared with high-concentration sources—such as beef liver or orange vegetables—eggs are moderate but reliably bioavailable sources of retinol.

Several factors affect yolk vitamin A content: the hen’s feed (fortified or carotenoid-rich feeds increase retinol and carotenoid levels), production system (free-range or pasture access can raise micronutrient content), and seasonality. Deeper orange yolks usually indicate higher carotenoid content, though retinol concentrations depend primarily on dietary retinol supplied to the bird.

## Nutritional planning and contribution to daily needs

European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) adequate intake values are approximately 750 µg RAE/day for adult males and 650 µg RAE/day for adult females. A single large egg (≈81 µg RAE) supplies about 10–12% of an adult’s requirement. Practical examples:

- One egg per day supplies ~10–12% of daily RAE; weekly this totals over 560 µg.
- Two eggs per day yield ~150–160 µg RAE (≈20–25% of daily needs).
- Three eggs per week contribute ~240 µg RAE (≈30–35% of a single day’s requirement when averaged across the week).

Eggs fit well into a food-first approach: combine whole eggs with carotenoid-rich vegetables and a source of dietary fat (olive oil, avocado) to support absorption of fat-soluble vitamins. Note that consuming whites only eliminates this retinol contribution.

## Safety and cooking

Typical cooking methods produce minimal losses of vitamin A; retinol in yolks remains fairly stable under common culinary temperatures. The primary safety concern with vitamin A arises from chronic excessive intake of preformed retinol from supplements or organ meats, not from regular egg consumption. Pregnant people and others with higher requirements should consult guidance on total dietary retinol and avoid high-dose retinol supplements unless advised by a clinician.

## Conclusion

Egg yolks are a practical source of preformed vitamin A (retinol). While they do not match liver or certain vegetables in absolute content, eggs provide a stable, bioavailable contribution to dietary vitamin A that supports vision and immune health when included as part of a varied diet and considered alongside other nutrient sources.
