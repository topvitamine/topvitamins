Vitamin A is a fat‑soluble nutrient essential for vision, epithelial integrity, and immune function. Deficiency can present with a range of early and progressive signs; recognizing them promptly helps prevent irreversible damage.

Common early symptoms include difficulty seeing in low light (night blindness), dry eyes or conjunctival irritation, and progressively flaky or rough skin. Night blindness (nyctalopia) typically manifests as trouble adapting to dim environments and is one of the most specific clinical indicators of inadequate vitamin A availability for rhodopsin synthesis in retinal rod cells. For an overview of clinical features and management, see [What are the symptoms of vitamin A deficiency?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-are-the-symptoms-of-vitamin-a-deficiency).

More advanced deficiency affects mucosal surfaces and immunity. Patients may experience frequent respiratory and gastrointestinal infections, delayed wound healing, and increased susceptibility to common pathogens because vitamin A supports epithelial barriers and lymphocyte function. Dermatological signs can include follicular hyperkeratosis—small, rough bumps around hair follicles—and generalized skin dryness due to impaired keratinocyte differentiation.

Ocular progression beyond night blindness can lead to xerophthalmia, corneal ulceration, and keratomalacia, conditions that may cause permanent visual impairment or blindness if not treated. Symptoms that suggest structural eye involvement include persistent blurred vision, photophobia (light sensitivity), and the sensation of a gritty or foreign body in the eye.

Risk factors and populations at higher risk of deficiency include young children, pregnant and lactating women, people with fat‑malabsorption disorders (such as celiac disease or Crohn’s disease), and individuals on restrictive diets. Because vitamin A is fat‑soluble, inadequate dietary fat or impaired fat absorption reduces uptake of preformed vitamin A and provitamin A carotenoids.

Assessment and management
Clinical assessment should consider dietary history, signs on eye and skin exam, and, when indicated, biochemical testing of serum retinol. Management often begins with dietary optimization (liver, dairy, eggs, and orange or dark leafy vegetables rich in provitamin A carotenoids) and addressing underlying malabsorption. In many cases, supervised supplementation corrects deficiency and reduces infection risk. However, excessive intake of preformed vitamin A carries toxicity risks (eg, hepatic injury, teratogenicity) and requires medical supervision.

Key points
- Early: night blindness, dry skin, conjunctival dryness.
- Intermediate: frequent infections, delayed healing, follicular hyperkeratosis.
- Advanced: xerophthalmia, corneal damage, potential irreversible vision loss.
- Evaluation should include dietary review and medical assessment; supplementation can be effective but requires attention to safety.
