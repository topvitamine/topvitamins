## Quick summary

Your body requires 13 essential vitamins daily to support metabolism, immunity, bone health, vision, and cellular function. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and water-soluble vitamins (the B-complex and C) each have distinct roles. Daily needs vary by age, sex, health status, and lifestyle; targeted testing and professional guidance help determine personal requirements.

## Daily essentials and their roles

- Vitamin A: supports vision, epithelial integrity, and immune response. Sources include liver, dairy, and orange vegetables.
- B-complex (B1, B2, B3, B5, B6, B7, B9, B12): required for energy metabolism, red blood cell formation, and nervous system function. Particular attention is needed for B12 in people following plant-based diets.
- Vitamin C: an antioxidant that supports collagen synthesis and immune defense; abundant in citrus and many vegetables.
- Vitamin D: important for calcium homeostasis and bone health; produced in skin after sun exposure and present in fatty fish and fortified foods.
- Vitamin E: an antioxidant protecting cell membranes; found in nuts and seeds.
- Vitamin K: necessary for blood clotting and bone metabolism; present in leafy greens and fermented foods.

## Recommended intake and variability

Recommended intakes (RDAs or NRVs) are population-level guides, not one-size-fits-all prescriptions. Typical adult reference values include Vitamin A (≈700–900 µg), Vitamin D (≈15–20 µg), Vitamin C (≈80–100 mg), and Vitamin B12 (≈2.4 µg), among others. Life stages such as pregnancy, infancy, and older age often require adjusted amounts. Absorption factors (e.g., fat for fat-soluble vitamins, interactions like calcium and iron) and genetic variants (such as MTHFR) can alter effective needs.

Clinical tests—serum 25(OH)D for vitamin D, methylmalonic acid or B12 for cobalamin status—are useful tools for targeted supplementation decisions.

## Minerals and synergy

Minerals (magnesium, calcium, zinc, iron, selenium, iodine) are integral partners for vitamin function. Magnesium is involved in hundreds of enzymatic reactions; calcium and vitamin D interact directly for bone health. Imbalances or high-dose single-nutrient strategies can interfere with absorption or produce adverse effects, so measured approaches are recommended.

## Who may need supplements

Certain groups are at higher risk of deficiency: vegans/vegetarians (B12, iron, zinc), those with limited sun exposure (vitamin D), pregnant people (folate, iron), older adults (B12, D), and individuals with malabsorption conditions. Blood testing and clinical assessment help identify specific gaps. ## Practical considerations

Aim for a nutrient-dense diet as the primary source of vitamins and minerals. Use supplements to address verified gaps or increased needs, avoiding chronic megadoses, particularly of fat-soluble vitamins. Consider product formulation, forms of nutrients (e.g., methylated folate, methylcobalamin), and third-party quality verification when selecting supplements. Additional general resources are available at <a href='https://www.topvitamine.com'>Topvitamine</a>.

## Conclusion

Daily vitamin needs are structured but individualized. Combine dietary planning, lab assessment when indicated, and evidence-based supplementation to maintain optimal levels while minimizing the risk of deficiency or excess.
