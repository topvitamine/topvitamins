## Introduction

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of enzymatic reactions that support energy production, nerve transmission, muscle function, and bone health. While dietary magnesium from foods is safe for most people, supplemental magnesium—at therapeutic doses—can pose risks for certain individuals. This article summarizes who should avoid magnesium supplements, common safety concerns, and how to recognize adverse effects.

## When magnesium supplements are contraindicated

Some medical conditions and physiological states make magnesium supplementation inappropriate or potentially dangerous. Key contraindications include:

- Renal impairment: The kidneys regulate magnesium excretion. People with chronic kidney disease (especially stages 3–5), acute renal failure, or those on dialysis are at increased risk of magnesium accumulation (hypermagnesemia) and should avoid supplements unless closely supervised by a nephrologist.
- Cardiac conduction disorders: Magnesium influences cardiac electrical activity. Individuals with heart block or pronounced bradycardia should not take magnesium without medical oversight because it can worsen conduction abnormalities.
- Neuromuscular disorders: Conditions such as myasthenia gravis can be exacerbated by magnesium’s neuromuscular blocking effects, increasing weakness and respiratory risk.
- Severe dehydration or electrolyte disturbances: Uncorrected low calcium or potassium, or significant dehydration, may be worsened by magnesium and can lead to cardiac or neuromuscular complications.
- Acute gastrointestinal obstruction or severe bowel disease: Oral forms that act as laxatives (e.g., magnesium citrate, magnesium sulfate) can aggravate obstructions or perforation risk.

## Safety concerns and interactions

Supplemental magnesium can interact with medications (for example, reducing absorption of certain antibiotics like tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones or potentiating hypotension with some blood pressure agents). Diuretics and some antacids can also alter magnesium balance. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) suggests an upper limit of 250 mg/day from supplemental magnesium, excluding dietary sources, but clinical needs vary and monitoring is important.

People who are elderly, pregnant, or have chronic illnesses should consult a clinician before starting supplements. ## Allergies and adverse reactions

True allergies to elemental magnesium are extremely rare. Reported reactions usually arise from additives, fillers, or contaminants in a product rather than magnesium itself. Symptoms can include skin rash, hives, or gastrointestinal upset; severe allergic reactions require immediate medical attention.

## Recognizing magnesium overdose

Hypermagnesemia symptoms can progress from nausea, flushing, and weakness to hypotension, slowed respiration, muscle paralysis, and cardiac arrhythmias. Children and older adults are particularly vulnerable. Any signs of severe toxicity warrant emergency care.

## Practical guidance

Avoid self-prescribing magnesium if you have renal disease, cardiac conduction abnormalities, severe GI disorders, or significant electrolyte imbalances. Discuss supplementation with a healthcare professional who can assess risks, review medications, and order appropriate blood tests.
