Quick answer summary

- Most dried fruits do not naturally contain vitamin B12.
- Vitamin B12 is primarily found in animal-derived foods and is produced by microorganisms.
- Some dried fruits on the market are fortified with B12 (e.g., fortified apricots, prunes, raisins).
- Vegans and vegetarians typically rely on fortified foods or supplements to meet B12 needs.
- There are no known dried berries that naturally contain active B12.
- Read product labels to confirm fortification and identify the B12 form (cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin).
- Algae like spirulina often contain inactive B12 analogs and are unreliable as sources.
- Fortified dried fruits can be a convenient supplement to a plant-based diet but are not a universal solution.
- For consistent absorption guidance, consult resources on B12 absorption and supplement strategies.

Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for nerve function, DNA synthesis, and red blood cell formation. Because only certain bacteria synthesize B12, the vitamin is concentrated in animal tissues; plant-derived foods, including dried fruits, generally lack biologically active B12 unless manufacturers add it. This presents a challenge for those following strict plant-based diets, making fortified foods and supplements important tools to prevent deficiency.

Natural occurrence vs. fortification

Scientific analyses consistently show negligible or non-detectable levels of active B12 in typical dried fruits such as dates, raisins, figs, and cranberries. Any measurable B12 in plant foods is usually due to surface contamination by microbes or laboratory artifacts, and therefore unreliable as a dietary source. To address this gap, some producers fortify dried fruits during processing by adding stable synthetic forms of B12—most commonly cyanocobalamin, and less often methylcobalamin. Fortified servings can supply 1–3 micrograms of B12, making these products useful complements to a B12-conscious eating plan.

Practical advice for plant-based eaters

If you prefer to obtain B12 from fortified foods rather than supplements, look for explicit labeling such as 'fortified with vitamin B12' or ingredient listings naming cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin. Fortified dried apricots, prunes, raisins, and fruit bars are examples available in some markets. Keep in mind that portion sizes matter: check the nutrition facts to understand how much a serving contributes toward the recommended daily intake (roughly 2.4 µg for most adults).

Limitations of plant sources and algae

Claims that seaweeds, spirulina, or fermented plant products provide reliable B12 are not supported by robust evidence. Much of the B12-like activity measured in these foods comes from inactive analogs that humans cannot use and may even interfere with absorption.
