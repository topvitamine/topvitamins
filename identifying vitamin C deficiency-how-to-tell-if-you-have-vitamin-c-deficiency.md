Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is required for collagen synthesis, antioxidant defence, iron absorption and normal immune function. Because humans cannot synthesise or store large amounts of vitamin C, inadequate intake or increased losses can produce a recognizable clinical picture. This article summarises common signs, diagnostic approaches and practical considerations for identifying deficiency.

Quick identification

Early, non-specific signs include fatigue, irritability, poor concentration and slower healing of minor cuts or bruises. As levels decline further, classic features related to impaired collagen production and weakened capillary integrity appear: bleeding or swollen gums, easy bruising, corkscrew hair deformities and prolonged wound healing. Severe, prolonged deficiency can lead to scurvy, which presents with anemia, gingival hypertrophy, joint pain and subcutaneous hemorrhages.

Who is at higher risk?

People with limited fruit and vegetable intake, those living in food-insecure areas, smokers, chronic alcohol users, and individuals with malabsorption (for example, after some gastrointestinal surgeries or with inflammatory bowel disease) are at elevated risk. Older adults who eat poorly and patients under major physiological stress (infection, burn, surgery) also have greater vitamin C requirements.

Assessing intake and symptoms

A practical first step is a dietary review: frequent consumption of citrus, berries, kiwi, bell peppers, broccoli, kale and similar produce generally supplies adequate vitamin C. If dietary intake is limited and you notice the signs described above, consider clinical assessment. Keeping a short food and symptom diary over one to two weeks can help clinicians identify patterns and possible deficiencies. Laboratory diagnosis

Definitive diagnosis is via measurement of plasma ascorbic acid. Concentrations below about 0.2 mg/dL (11 µmol/L) are widely accepted as deficient, although clinical symptoms can appear at higher concentrations in some individuals. Routine blood tests (e.g., complete blood count) may show anemia that accompanies deficiency because impaired ascorbate availability reduces iron absorption and affects erythropoiesis.

Differential considerations

Symptoms such as fatigue, bruising and gum disease overlap with other conditions (iron or B12 deficiency, periodontal disease, bleeding disorders). Clinicians will therefore interpret vitamin C measurements alongside other laboratory data and clinical findings. Management considerations

Correction of deficiency combines improving dietary intake and, where appropriate, short-term supplementation to restore plasma levels. Choice of formulation and dose should be individualised to tolerance and comorbidities. When to seek care

Persisting bleeding gums, unexplained bruising, significant fatigue or poor wound healing warrant medical evaluation. Laboratory testing can confirm deficiency and help rule out other causes.

Key points: consider recent diet, look for characteristic mucocutaneous signs, confirm with plasma ascorbic acid levels, and manage with dietary correction and targeted supplementation under clinical guidance.
