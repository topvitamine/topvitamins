## Understanding disease-related causes of vitamin B12 deficiency

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell production, neurological function, and DNA synthesis. Deficiency can cause megaloblastic anemia, neuropathy, cognitive changes, and, if prolonged, potentially irreversible neurological damage. While low dietary intake is a common cause, a range of diseases and structural issues can impair absorption or utilization of B12 even when intake seems adequate.

### Major disease categories that reduce B12 status

- Pernicious anemia and autoimmune gastritis: Pernicious anemia arises when autoantibodies target intrinsic factor or gastric parietal cells, preventing the intrinsic factor–mediated uptake of B12 in the terminal ileum. Autoimmune gastritis similarly reduces acid and intrinsic factor production, leading to progressive malabsorption and lifelong need for monitoring and replacement.

- Malabsorption syndromes: Conditions such as celiac disease, tropical sprue, chronic pancreatitis, and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) disrupt the coordinated steps required for B12 absorption. Damage to the ileal mucosa, abnormal bacterial competition, or insufficient pancreatic enzymes can all reduce the amount of B12 available for uptake.

- Gastrointestinal diseases and surgery: Crohn’s disease commonly affects the terminal ileum and directly impairs the B12 absorption site. Surgical resections (including gastrectomy and ileal resection) and bariatric procedures can remove or bypass the anatomical structures needed for B12 processing, producing rapid depletion of stores.

- Systemic autoimmune disorders: Autoimmune conditions such as type 1 diabetes, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and systemic lupus erythematosus are associated with higher rates of autoimmune gastritis and pernicious anemia. These comorbidities can compound the risk of clinically significant deficiency.

- Dietary insufficiency: Strict vegetarian and vegan diets, restricted eating disorders, and limited access to animal-source foods increase the risk of deficiency because reliable natural food sources of B12 are animal-derived.

### Clinical implications and testing

Symptoms can be subtle at first (fatigue, pallor, paresthesia, mild cognitive changes) and progress to gait disturbance, neuropathy, or cognitive decline if untreated. Laboratory assessment typically includes serum B12, methylmalonic acid, and homocysteine when diagnosis is uncertain; antibody testing can detect pernicious anemia. Identifying the underlying disease is critical because it determines the route and frequency of replacement therapy.

### Management considerations

When malabsorption or autoimmune mechanisms are present, parenteral (intramuscular) B12 or very high-dose oral/sublingual regimens are often required to restore and maintain tissue levels. In dietary deficiency, fortified foods and oral supplements usually suffice. Close follow-up and biochemical monitoring guide ongoing therapy.
