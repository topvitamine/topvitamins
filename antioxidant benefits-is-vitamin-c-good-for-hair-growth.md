<p><strong>Introduction</strong></p>

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is widely recognized for its antioxidant properties and role in collagen synthesis. Its involvement in processes relevant to hair health—iron absorption, protection from oxidative stress, and support for collagen production—makes it a nutrient of interest for people seeking to maintain or improve hair strength and growth. This article summarizes the evidence and practical considerations for vitamin C in hair care.</p>

<h2>Vitamin C’s biological roles relevant to hair</h2>

<p>Vitamin C contributes to hair health through several mechanisms. It enhances absorption of non-heme iron, a mineral required for oxygen delivery to hair follicles. It also acts as a cofactor in collagen synthesis, supporting the extracellular matrix surrounding follicles and helping maintain hair shaft strength. Finally, as a potent antioxidant, vitamin C neutralizes free radicals that can damage follicular cells and accelerate follicle aging.</p>

<h2>Evidence from nutrition and dermatology</h2>

<p>Clinical and observational studies link poor antioxidant status and low ascorbic acid levels with increased diffuse hair thinning. Systematic reviews in trichology emphasize the role of oxidative stress in follicle miniaturization and hair aging. While direct randomized trials isolating vitamin C's effect on hair length are limited, mechanistic data (iron absorption, collagen formation, antioxidant protection) provide plausible pathways by which adequate vitamin C status supports hair resilience and reduces breakage.</p>

<p>Dietary sources such as citrus fruits, berries, bell peppers, and leafy greens provide vitamin C alongside fiber and phytonutrients. Supplementation can be useful for people with low dietary intake, absorption issues, or higher requirements due to illness, but it should be considered within the context of an overall nutrient strategy.</p>

<h2>Topical vs oral administration</h2>

<p>Topical vitamin C formulations (stabilized ascorbic acid serums or scalp masks) target local oxidative stress and may support scalp barrier repair. However, systemic effects—iron absorption and collagen synthesis—depend on oral intake and body stores. Combining dietary improvements, targeted supplementation if needed, and appropriate topical care can create a supportive environment for hair follicles without relying on any single intervention.</p>

<h2>Practical considerations</h2>

<ul>
<li>Recommended dietary allowances (RDA) vary by sex and age; adults generally require around 75–90 mg/day, with a tolerable upper intake level at 2000 mg/day to avoid gastrointestinal side effects.</li>
<li>Vitamin C works synergistically with nutrients like iron, zinc, biotin, and amino acids used for keratin and collagen production.</li>
<li>Addressing lifestyle contributors—smoking, UV exposure, poor diet, and chronic stress—reduces oxidative load on the scalp and complements nutritional measures.</li>
</ul>
