**Introduction**

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential water-soluble nutrient involved in multiple physiological processes. The human body cannot synthesize it, so regular intake through diet or supplements is required to maintain adequate levels. This article summarizes the major uses of vitamin C based on current evidence, including its antioxidant action, immune support, role in collagen synthesis, effects on skin health, and contributions to disease prevention. For a detailed overview, see this guide: [What is vitamin C and its uses](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-is-vitamin-c-and-its-uses).

Antioxidant properties

Vitamin C is a primary aqueous-phase antioxidant that neutralizes reactive oxygen species and regenerates other antioxidants such as vitamin E. By reducing oxidative stress, vitamin C helps protect cellular components from damage linked to aging and chronic conditions. Clinical trials have shown reductions in oxidative markers with supplementation in populations exposed to environmental stressors or intense physical activity. Research suggests synergistic benefit when vitamin C is combined with other nutrients that support oxidative defense.

Immune system support

Vitamin C contributes to immune competence through several mechanisms: enhancing neutrophil function, supporting lymphocyte proliferation, strengthening epithelial barriers, and modulating cytokine signaling. Meta-analyses indicate that regular supplementation can shorten the duration and lessen the severity of common respiratory infections, particularly in individuals under physical stress. While not a guaranteed preventative for every infection, vitamin C supports both innate and adaptive immune responses and can be considered as part of a broader nutritional strategy.

Collagen synthesis and wound healing

A key biochemical role of vitamin C is as a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases, enzymes required for proper collagen maturation. Adequate vitamin C is necessary for stable connective tissue, effective wound repair, and maintenance of vascular integrity. Historically, deficiency results in scurvy, which demonstrates the centrality of vitamin C to collagen-dependent structures.

Skin health

Vitamin C supports dermal health by promoting collagen formation, improving elasticity, and protecting skin cells from photo-oxidative damage. Both topical and oral applications have evidence of benefit: topical formulations deliver targeted concentrations to the epidermis, while oral intake supports deeper dermal layers. Combining systemic and topical approaches tends to provide the most comprehensive skin support.

Disease prevention and cardiovascular effects

Through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways, vitamin C has been associated with favorable markers of cardiovascular function, such as improved endothelial responsiveness and reduced arterial stiffness. Epidemiological studies often report correlations between higher vitamin C status and lower risk of certain chronic diseases, though causality varies by condition. High-dose therapeutic uses (for example, intravenous vitamin C in clinical oncology) are under investigation and should be managed by clinicians.

Safety and dosing

Recommended daily allowances vary by age and sex; common guidance for adults ranges from 75–90 mg/day, with many supplementation strategies using 200–1000 mg/day for specific needs. Intakes above the established upper limit (generally 2000 mg/day) can cause gastrointestinal symptoms. Individual requirements depend on diet, health status, and exposures.

Conclusion

Vitamin C remains a foundational nutrient with broad biological functions. Appropriate intake through diet and, when needed, supplementation supports antioxidant defense, immune resilience, connective tissue health, skin integrity, and aspects of chronic disease risk mitigation. Discuss supplementation with a healthcare professional when tailoring doses to specific health needs.
