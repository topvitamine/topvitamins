Quick answer

Magnesium can help reduce heartburn for some people by neutralizing stomach acid and supporting smooth muscle function in the gastrointestinal tract. Certain magnesium salts (for example, magnesium hydroxide and magnesium carbonate) are used as antacids, while more bioavailable forms (like magnesium citrate and glycinate) support systemic magnesium status and muscle relaxation. Evidence is promising but mixed; supplementation should be individualized and monitored.

Introduction

Heartburn — the burning sensation that rises from the stomach to the chest — is commonly caused by acid reflux and dysfunction of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES). Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of biochemical reactions, including muscle contraction and relaxation, which can influence LES behavior and gut motility. How magnesium may relieve heartburn

Two primary mechanisms may explain how magnesium influences heartburn: chemical neutralization of gastric acid and neuromuscular effects on the LES. Magnesium hydroxide and magnesium carbonate are alkaline compounds that react with stomach acid to produce temporary buffering, producing rapid symptom relief similar to conventional antacids. Separately, magnesium supports smooth muscle relaxation and neuromuscular transmission; adequate magnesium status may help the LES maintain appropriate tone and reduce reflux episodes.

Which forms matter

Different magnesium salts vary in solubility, absorption and gastrointestinal effects. Magnesium citrate and magnesium glycinate are relatively well absorbed and better tolerated for long-term supplementation. Magnesium carbonate and hydroxide have more direct antacid activity and show faster symptomatic relief. Magnesium oxide tends to be less bioavailable and can cause gastrointestinal upset in some users. Selection should match the therapeutic goal: short-term acid neutralization versus long-term correction of deficiency and muscle function.

Safety and dosing considerations

Moderate magnesium supplementation is generally safe for people with normal kidney function. Excessive doses — especially of more osmotic forms like magnesium citrate — can produce diarrhea, cramping, and electrolyte imbalance. Those with kidney impairment, cardiac conditions, or who use certain medications (e.g., some antibiotics, bisphosphonates, and proton pump inhibitors) should consult a clinician before starting magnesium. Clinical studies suggest benefit in specific cohorts, but large randomized trials on routine use for GERD are limited.

Conclusion

Magnesium may provide meaningful heartburn relief for some individuals through direct antacid effects and by supporting LES function and gut motility. Choice of magnesium form, appropriate dosing and attention to safety are important.
