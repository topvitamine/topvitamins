**Introduction**

Magnesium is a key mineral involved in more than 300 enzymatic reactions, affecting muscle and nerve function, energy production, and bone health. At around age 50, physiological changes—altered gut function, medication use, and hormonal shifts—can reduce magnesium absorption and increase requirements. Choosing an appropriate form and dose can help support bone density, cardiovascular function, sleep, and metabolic health.

Magnesium benefits and common concerns

After 50, magnesium supports bone mineralization (alongside vitamin D and K), vascular tone, and neuromuscular function. Clinically relevant benefits include reduced muscle cramps, improved sleep quality, and potential improvements in blood pressure and insulin sensitivity. Symptoms suggesting low magnesium include nocturnal cramps, persistent fatigue, sleep disturbances, mood changes, and gastrointestinal complaints.

Dosage and safety considerations

Recommended intakes vary by sex and guideline source; many authorities advise approximately 300–420 mg/day for adults over 50, combining dietary sources and supplements. Foods high in magnesium include leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes, and whole grains. Supplemental magnesium should be titrated to tolerance; excessive supplemental magnesium can cause diarrhea and GI upset. When using supplements, many practitioners recommend splitting doses to enhance absorption and reduce side effects.

Best forms for middle-aged adults

- Magnesium glycinate: a chelated form notable for good absorption and minimal laxative effect; often chosen for sleep and anxiety-related concerns.
- Magnesium citrate: well absorbed and useful for constipation and muscle cramps, though it can have mild laxative effects at higher doses.
- Magnesium malate: associated with energy metabolism and may suit those with fatigue-related complaints.
- Magnesium chloride: available for oral or topical use and generally well tolerated.
- Magnesium oxide: contains more elemental magnesium per tablet but is less bioavailable and more likely to cause GI effects.

Optimizing absorption at midlife

Absorption can be improved by maintaining vitamin D sufficiency, avoiding excessive concurrent calcium intake, taking smaller divided doses, and supporting gut health through fiber and probiotics. Alcohol and high caffeine intake increase urinary magnesium losses, so moderation can help maintain status.

When to test and consult

Serum magnesium tests may not reflect total body stores; clinical assessment and review of medications (e.g., diuretics, proton-pump inhibitors) are important. Discuss persistent symptoms or planned high-dose supplementation with a healthcare professional.

Conclusion

Magnesium remains an important, evidence-supported nutrient for people around and after age 50. Selection of form and dose should be individualized to symptoms, comorbidities, and tolerance.
