Vitamin A is an essential fat‑soluble nutrient with a well‑documented role in visual function and ocular health. At a biochemical level, vitamin A is converted into retinal, which combines with opsin proteins to form visual pigments such as rhodopsin in rods and photopsins in cones. These pigments are critical for phototransduction—the process that converts light into neural signals—so adequate vitamin A availability is fundamental for dark adaptation and contrast sensitivity.

Deficiency presents earliest as impaired night vision (nyctalopia) and can progress to xerophthalmia, corneal damage, and increased susceptibility to infection if untreated. Clinically, restoring vitamin A through diet or supplementation improves dark adaptation in people with marginal status, typically within days to weeks, because retinol restores the photopigment regeneration cycle. At the same time, hypervitaminosis A from excessive preformed vitamin A intake carries risks (liver toxicity, headaches, and other systemic effects), so adherence to established upper intake levels is important.

Beyond the visual cycle, vitamin A maintains the integrity of epithelial tissues on the ocular surface. It supports mucin secretion by conjunctival goblet cells and helps stabilize the tear film, reducing dryness and surface damage. These epithelial and mucosal effects also underpin vitamin A’s role in immune defense within the eye, helping to reduce the risk of conjunctival infection and promoting wound healing after injury.

Emerging observational evidence links consistent, adequate vitamin A status with reduced markers of retinal oxidative stress and slower degeneration of retinal tissues. While regulatory bodies do not endorse vitamin A as a treatment for specific degenerative diseases, a balanced intake—often achieved through a combination of diet and, when appropriate, supplementation—supports retinal resilience. Dietary sources include preformed vitamin A from animal foods (liver, eggs, dairy) and provitamin A carotenoids (beta‑carotene) from colorful fruits and vegetables. The conversion of beta‑carotene to retinol is regulated by the body, making carotenoids a safer source with lower toxicity risk. In practice, maintaining eye health means balancing dietary intake, monitoring for signs of deficiency (night vision problems, chronic dry eye, frequent ocular infections), and discussing supplementation with a healthcare provider when risks for malabsorption or increased requirement exist. Special populations—infants, pregnant women, people with malabsorption syndromes, and older adults—may need tailored advice to avoid both deficiency and excess.
