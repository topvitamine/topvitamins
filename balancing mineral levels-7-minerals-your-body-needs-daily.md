Introduction

Minerals are inorganic elements required for fundamental physiological processes, including bone structure, nerve conduction, fluid balance, and enzymatic reactions. Modern diets, soil depletion, and lifestyle factors can make it difficult to meet daily mineral needs through food alone. This article summarizes seven essential minerals—calcium, magnesium, potassium, zinc, iron, selenium, and iodine—covering their roles, common sources, deficiency signals, and practical balancing tips.

The seven minerals and why they matter

- Calcium: Central to bone strength, muscle contraction, and cell signaling. Common dietary sources include dairy, leafy greens, almonds, and fortified plant milks. Adults typically need about 1,000–1,200 mg/day.

- Magnesium: A cofactor in hundreds of enzymatic reactions affecting energy, nerve function, and muscle relaxation. Found in spinach, nuts, seeds, whole grains, and dark chocolate. Recommended intakes are roughly 310–420 mg/day depending on age and sex.

- Potassium: Essential for maintaining cellular electrical gradients, blood pressure regulation, and muscle function. Rich sources are bananas, potatoes (with skin), legumes, avocados, and citrus. Typical targets are ~3,500–4,700 mg/day.

- Zinc: Important for immune function, wound healing, and DNA synthesis. Sources include oysters, beef, legumes, and whole grains. Recommended intakes are about 8–11 mg/day.

- Iron: Required for oxygen transport and energy metabolism. Heme iron from meat is better absorbed than non-heme iron from plants; combining plant sources with vitamin C improves uptake. Adult men need ~8 mg/day; menstruating women often require ~18 mg/day.

- Selenium: A component of antioxidant enzymes and thyroid hormone metabolism. High sources include Brazil nuts, tuna, eggs, and sunflower seeds. Typical intake is about 55 µg/day.

- Iodine: Necessary for thyroid hormone production and metabolic regulation. Seaweed, seafood, dairy, and iodized salt are primary sources. The general recommendation is ~150 µg/day.

Deficiency signals and assessment

Deficiencies can present as fatigue, muscle cramps, cognitive fog, hair loss, immune changes, or thyroid enlargement. Because symptoms overlap with many conditions, clinical testing (blood, urine, or specific functional assays) is the best way to confirm a shortfall before routine supplementation.

Balancing interactions and safety

Minerals interact: for example, excessive zinc can impair copper status; high calcium intakes may affect magnesium availability; iron absorption is reduced by concurrent calcium. Spacing supplements appropriately and favoring food-first approaches reduces the risk of antagonistic interactions. Be aware of upper intake limits—chronic intakes above recommended levels can lead to toxicity or metabolic disturbances.

Practical approach

Aim for a varied, minimally processed diet that includes leafy greens, nuts, legumes, seafood, dairy (or fortified alternatives), whole grains, and seeds. When dietary gaps are likely (e.g., restrictive diets, pregnancy, older age, or certain medical conditions), targeted supplementation based on testing and professional guidance can be useful.
