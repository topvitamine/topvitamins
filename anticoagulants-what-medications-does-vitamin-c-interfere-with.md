Quick answer

Vitamin C can interact with several medications and supplements. Common concerns include potential effects on anticoagulants, chemotherapy drugs, statins, and iron therapy. Dose, timing, route of administration (oral versus intravenous), and individual medical conditions determine clinical relevance. For a more detailed discussion see [what medications does vitamin C interfere with?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-medications-does-vitamin-c-interfere-with).

Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a widely used antioxidant and micronutrient. While generally safe at dietary levels, supplemental vitamin C can alter absorption, metabolism, or the pharmacodynamics of some drugs. Below are evidence-based summaries of the most clinically relevant interactions and practical considerations for safe use.

Anticoagulants and blood thinners

Warfarin and other anticoagulants require careful monitoring because small changes in metabolism or vitamin K balance can affect clotting. Case reports and limited studies suggest high-dose vitamin C (>1,000 mg/day) could affect INR in warfarin users, possibly through altered liver metabolism or indirect effects on vitamin K pathways. Patients on anticoagulants should avoid megadoses and maintain consistent vitamin C intake while having regular clotting tests.

Chemotherapy

Many chemotherapeutic agents exert part of their effect via generation of reactive oxygen species. High-dose antioxidants like vitamin C may theoretically blunt this mechanism. Clinical data are mixed: some studies show potential protection of healthy tissue, others suggest reduced tumoricidal activity when antioxidants are given concurrently. Because of these risks, oncology guidelines recommend discussing any supplement use with the treating oncologist; intravenous high-dose vitamin C is a separate clinical scenario with distinct pharmacology compared with oral dosing.

Statins

Statins reduce cholesterol and exert pleiotropic effects that involve oxidative pathways. Some trials of combined antioxidant therapy (including vitamin C) report modest improvements in biomarkers, but results are inconsistent. There is limited evidence that ordinary supplemental doses of vitamin C interfere with statin efficacy; however, very high doses could theoretically modulate liver enzymes involved in statin metabolism. Discuss supplementation with your clinician if you experience side effects or take multiple medications.

Iron supplements

Vitamin C enhances non-heme iron absorption by reducing ferric to ferrous iron. Co-administration of modest vitamin C (about 250–500 mg) with oral iron improves bioavailability and therapy effectiveness for iron-deficiency anemia. Conversely, people with iron overload disorders (e.g., hemochromatosis) should avoid vitamin C megadoses because they can exacerbate iron accumulation.

Other medications and timing

Oral contraceptives may modestly affect vitamin C status, and chronic NSAID use can reduce vitamin C levels. Certain antibiotics and medications with pH-dependent absorption may be best spaced at least two hours from high-dose vitamin C to avoid interaction.

Practical recommendations

- Keep supplemental vitamin C within recommended ranges (commonly 75–90 mg/day from diet; supplements usually 100–500 mg/day) unless supervised by a provider.  
- Avoid megadoses (>1,000 mg/day) when on anticoagulants, during chemotherapy, or with iron-overload conditions.  
- Maintain consistent intake and inform your prescriber about all supplements.
