## Introduction

Omega-3 fatty acids—mainly EPA and DHA—are essential nutrients with well-established roles in cardiovascular, cognitive, and inflammatory regulation. Emerging evidence also supports their relevance to female sexual health, where vascular function, hormonal balance, neural signaling, and mucosal integrity intersect to influence desire, arousal, lubrication, and comfort.

## Biological mechanisms linking omega-3s to sexual function

Omega-3s are incorporated into cell membranes and influence membrane fluidity, eicosanoid production, and inflammatory signaling. These effects have several downstream implications for sexual health:

- Vascular and endothelial function: EPA/DHA support endothelial health and microcirculation, which can improve genital blood flow necessary for arousal and natural lubrication.
- Neurotransmission and mood: DHA is important for neuronal membrane structure; EPA appears to modulate mood and reduce anxiety through anti-inflammatory and neurochemical effects. Improved mood and reduced stress facilitate sexual desire and responsiveness.
- Hormone regulation: Omega-3s contribute to pathways that influence sex hormone production and metabolism, including modulation of prostaglandins and sex hormone–binding globulin (SHBG), which can affect free hormone availability.
- Tissue integrity and mucosal health: Anti-inflammatory effects and support for cellular repair help maintain vaginal mucosa elasticity and hydration, especially relevant during perimenopause and menopause.

## Evidence and clinical observations

Observational studies and smaller clinical trials suggest associations between higher omega-3 intake and improved aspects of sexual function in women. Reported benefits include reduced vaginal dryness, improved lubrication, reduced sexual distress in menopausal populations, and better mood regulation that supports libido. Improvements are often modest and variable between individuals, and more large-scale randomized trials are needed for definitive recommendations.

## Practical considerations

Dietary sources (fatty fish, algae) supply EPA and DHA, but conversion from plant-based ALA is limited. Typical therapeutic intakes cited in the literature range from a few hundred milligrams to about 1 gram of combined EPA/DHA per day, depending on the clinical context and goals. As with any supplement strategy, dosing should be individualized and discussed with a healthcare provider, particularly when on anticoagulant therapy or managing chronic conditions.

Evidence-based integrative strategies may pair omega-3 intake with nutrient and lifestyle approaches that support sexual wellness (sleep optimization, stress management, and addressing micronutrient sufficiencies).

## Conclusion

Omega-3 fatty acids have plausible and evidence-supported pathways to support several domains of female sexual health, including vascular function, mood regulation, hormone modulation, and mucosal integrity. While not a universal remedy, omega-3s can be considered one component of a multifaceted approach to improving sexual comfort and satisfaction. Clinical decisions should be personalized and guided by current evidence and professional consultation.
