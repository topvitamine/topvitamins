Introduction

Vitamin D, often called the “sunshine vitamin,” is essential for bone health, immune regulation, muscle function, and aspects of mood. Deficiency is common in many populations and can present with a wide range of subtle to serious signs. Early recognition supports timely testing and corrective measures grounded in clinical evidence.

Common symptoms and clinical clues

- Persistent fatigue and low energy. One of the most frequently reported complaints among people with low vitamin D levels is unexplained tiredness that does not improve with rest. Vitamin D influences cellular energy metabolism, and insufficiency can reduce mitochondrial efficiency, contributing to lethargy.

- Bone pain and muscle weakness. Vitamin D facilitates intestinal calcium absorption and supports bone mineralization. Deficiency may cause dull bone aches (often in the lower back, pelvis, and legs), increased fracture risk, and proximal muscle weakness that affects mobility and balance.

- Mood changes and depressive symptoms. Vitamin D receptors are present in brain regions involved in mood regulation. Low levels have been associated with depressive symptoms and seasonal affective disorder in observational studies; supplementation has shown benefit in some trials, particularly when baseline levels are low.

- Increased susceptibility to infections. Vitamin D plays a role in innate and adaptive immunity, including the induction of antimicrobial peptides. People with low vitamin D may experience more frequent respiratory infections and delayed recovery from illnesses.

- Hair loss and skin problems. Hair thinning and certain inflammatory skin conditions have been linked to vitamin D insufficiency, possibly through effects on follicle cycling and epidermal repair.

- Bone softening and fractures. Severe or prolonged deficiency can cause osteomalacia in adults (and rickets in children), characterized by defective bone mineralization and a higher risk of fractures.

- Delayed wound healing and chronic musculoskeletal pain. Vitamin D contributes to tissue repair and modulates inflammation; low levels can slow wound closure and perpetuate muscle pain.

- Laboratory findings such as secondary hyperparathyroidism. Low serum 25(OH)D often accompanies elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) and altered calcium/phosphate balance, which may worsen bone resorption over time.

Assessment and evidence-based response

Measurement of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25[OH]D) is the standard test to assess status. Clinical context—age, skin pigmentation, sun exposure, diet, comorbidities, and medications—guides interpretation and management. Conclusion

Vitamin D deficiency can manifest in non-specific ways that affect energy, mood, musculoskeletal health, skin, and immune function. Recognition relies on clinical suspicion and confirmation with laboratory testing. Management should be individualized and based on current clinical guidelines and patient-specific risk factors.
