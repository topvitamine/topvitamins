Understanding Omega-3s and When You Might Need More

Omega-3 fatty acids are essential nutrients with well-established roles in cellular membranes, inflammation regulation, and brain and eye structure. Humans cannot synthesise sufficient amounts of EPA and DHA, so dietary intake or supplementation is necessary to maintain adequate stores. Determining whether you need more omega-3 depends on symptoms, dietary patterns, medical history, and, when available, objective testing.

Common signs that suggest low omega-3 intake include persistent dry or flaky skin, brittle hair or nails, joint stiffness, poor concentration or memory lapses, mood changes such as increased irritability or low mood, and dry eyes or visual fatigue. These symptoms reflect omega-3s’ roles in skin barrier function, neuronal membrane fluidity, and anti-inflammatory pathways.

Risk factors that increase the likelihood of insufficiency include low or no consumption of oily fish, long-term vegetarian or vegan diets without algal DHA supplementation, gastrointestinal conditions that impair fat absorption (for example, celiac disease or Crohn’s), aging-related declines in conversion efficiency, and increased physiological demand during pregnancy and lactation.

Testing and objective assessment

For a definitive assessment, the Omega-3 Index (a blood test measuring EPA and DHA in red blood cell membranes) is the most informative measure. An index of 8–12% is generally considered desirable by many researchers, while values <4% indicate low status and potentially higher risk for cardiometabolic issues. If testing is not accessible, a careful review of diet and symptoms with a healthcare provider or registered dietitian can guide decisions.

Dietary sources and practical adjustments

Fatty fish such as salmon, sardines, mackerel, and herring are the most direct dietary sources of EPA and DHA. For those following plant-based diets, ALA-rich foods (flaxseed, chia, walnuts, hemp seeds) provide a precursor that the body can convert to EPA and DHA inefficiently; algal DHA can be used to supply preformed DHA when needed.

Practical steps include evaluating weekly fish intake (two servings of oily fish is a common recommendation for many adults), adding ALA sources regularly if you avoid fish, and checking fortified foods. If symptoms persist despite dietary adjustments, discussing blood testing or supplementation with a clinician can be appropriate.

Balancing evidence and safety

Evidence supports roles for omega-3s in maintaining normal heart function, blood lipid levels, brain health, and eye health at specific intake levels. Safety considerations include avoiding very high supplemental intakes (generally >3 g/day of EPA+DHA) without medical supervision due to bleeding risk or interactions with medications.

When to consult a professional

If you experience multiple symptoms consistent with low omega-3 status, have malabsorption, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are taking anticoagulant medications, seek personalised advice from a healthcare professional. Objective testing can clarify needs and help tailor safe, evidence-based dietary or supplemental strategies.
