**Introduction**

A resilient immune system depends on multiple lifestyle and nutritional factors. Vitamins are central to immune function because they support cell-mediated responses, antioxidant defenses, and barrier integrity. This article summarizes key vitamins and related nutrients with evidence for supporting immune health, offers intake context, and points to resources for label transparency and supplement composition.

For a focused overview of immune-supporting vitamins and practical guidance, see [Which vitamin boosts the immune system?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/which-vitamin-boosts-the-immune-system)

Vitamin C

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) supports leukocyte function, enhances epithelial barrier integrity, and acts as an antioxidant during infection. Regular supplementation has been associated with modest reductions in duration and severity of upper respiratory tract infections in some randomized trials; it is not a cure. Food sources include citrus, berries, peppers, and leafy greens. EFSA recommends about 80 mg/day for adults, with higher short-term needs during stress or infection.

Vitamin D

Vitamin D functions like a hormone and modulates both innate and adaptive immunity. Meta-analyses suggest supplementation reduces the risk of acute respiratory infections, especially in individuals with low baseline 25(OH)D concentrations. Major sources are UVB-driven skin synthesis, oily fish, and fortified foods. EFSA guidance commonly references 15 µg (600 IU)/day for adults, but individual requirements can vary; blood testing informs personalized dosing.

Vitamin A and Beta‑carotene

Vitamin A is essential for maintenance of mucosal surfaces and for T‑cell differentiation. Preformed vitamin A is found in liver and dairy; provitamin A carotenoids (e.g., beta‑carotene) are abundant in orange and dark‑green vegetables. Both deficiency and excess carry risks—deficiency impairs barrier function and host defense, while chronic high doses of preformed vitamin A can be toxic.

B‑Complex Vitamins

Vitamins B6, B12, and folate support immune cell proliferation, antibody production, and DNA synthesis during immune responses. Deficiencies can manifest as anemia, fatigue, and impaired immunity; certain populations (older adults, strict vegetarians, people with malabsorption) are at higher risk for B12 deficiency. Dietary sources include meat, dairy, legumes, and fortified foods.

Trace Elements and Probiotics

Zinc and selenium are trace elements required for enzyme function and antioxidant defense; zinc lozenges and short courses of zinc have shown benefit in some cold studies. The gut microbiome heavily influences mucosal immunity; probiotic strains can support gut‑associated lymphoid tissues and modulate inflammatory responses. Food sources and balanced diets remain foundational.

Practical considerations

Prioritize a varied, nutrient‑dense diet to meet most needs; use supplements to address identified gaps, low sun exposure, or life stages with increased requirements. Choose bioavailable forms (e.g., vitamin D3, methylcobalamin for B12) and avoid unnecessary megadoses of fat‑soluble vitamins without clinical indication. Summary

No single vitamin is a universal solution; immune health reflects a network of nutrients working together. Vitamin C, D, A, B vitamins, zinc, selenium, and a healthy microbiome each play defined roles. Use dietary strategies first, reserve supplementation for evidence‑based gaps, and consult testing or a healthcare professional when tailoring intake.
