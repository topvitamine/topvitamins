Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is essential for collagen synthesis, antioxidant defense, iron absorption, and immune function. Deficiency can range from mild, nonspecific symptoms to scurvy in severe, prolonged cases. If you are wondering whether you need more Vitamin C, watch for key clinical clues and consider objective testing when risk factors are present. For an accessible overview, see [How to Know if You Need Vitamin C?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/how-to-know-if-you-need-vitamin-c).

Early signs of low Vitamin C are often subtle. Common complaints include persistent fatigue, easy bruising, slow wound healing, and dry or rough skin. Psychological symptoms such as irritability, low mood, or reduced motivation may accompany physical findings because Vitamin C participates in neurotransmitter synthesis. Recurrent infections or longer-than-expected respiratory illnesses can also reflect impaired immune defenses associated with low ascorbate levels.

More specific findings suggestive of advancing deficiency include bleeding or swollen gums, petechiae (small red spots from capillary leakage), and joint pain with swelling. In severe deficiency, scurvy can develop, characterized by pronounced gum disease, loosening teeth, extensive bruising, anemia, and profound weakness. Scurvy remains rare in high-income settings but can occur in people with extremely limited diets, malabsorption disorders, or severe psychiatric illness.

Risk factors that increase the likelihood of deficiency include low intake of fresh fruits and vegetables, smoking, heavy alcohol use, older age with limited diets, and gastrointestinal conditions (e.g., Crohn’s disease, celiac disease, or prior gastric surgery) that impair absorption. Physiological states such as pregnancy and lactation increase requirements. Lifestyle and socioeconomic factors — for example, reliance on processed foods or food insecurity — also contribute to lower intakes.

Assessment and management

Clinical assessment starts with a careful dietary history and attention to the signs described above. Laboratory measurement of plasma ascorbic acid provides objective evidence of status; however, levels can fluctuate with recent intake and illness. If deficiency is suspected, short-term supplementation or dietary correction is effective: symptoms often begin to improve within days and more markedly over weeks. Recommended intakes vary by authority, but typical adult targets are around 80 mg/day, with higher therapeutic doses used under medical supervision for deficiency. Avoid unsupervised megadoses due to potential gastrointestinal effects.

Prevention emphasizes regular consumption of Vitamin C–rich foods such as citrus fruits, berries, bell peppers, cruciferous vegetables, and kiwifruit. When dietary sources are insufficient or absorption is impaired, supplemental forms (including buffered or liposomal preparations) can be considered in consultation with a clinician. Objective testing and a combination of dietary optimization and appropriately dosed supplements are the standard approaches to correction.
