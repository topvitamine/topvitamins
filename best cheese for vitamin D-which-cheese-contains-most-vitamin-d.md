# Which cheese contains the most vitamin D?

## Introduction

Vitamin D, the “sunshine vitamin,” supports calcium absorption, bone health, immune function, and cellular processes. While sunlight and fatty fish are primary sources, dairy products — including cheese — contribute to total dietary vitamin D intake. Not all cheeses contain the same amounts, so understanding varieties and fortification practices helps consumers make informed choices.

For a focused overview of cheese varieties and vitamin D levels, see [Which cheese contains the most vitamin D?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/which-cheese-contains-most-vitamin-d).

## How vitamin D appears in cheese

Vitamin D in cheese originates largely from the milk used during production. Milk delivers vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) when cows receive adequate sunlight or when milk is fortified. During cheesemaking, some of that fat-soluble vitamin remains in the curd and thus in the finished cheese. Fat content, processing, and aging influence retention: harder, higher-fat cheeses retain more vitamin D per gram than soft, high-moisture varieties. Some manufacturers also add vitamin D directly to processed cheeses.

## Which cheeses are relatively higher in vitamin D?

Data from food composition tables show modest differences across cheese types. Among natural cheeses, Swiss tends to be among the higher contributors (roughly 5–7 IU per 100 g), with Gouda and Cheddar providing similar, slightly lower amounts. Soft cheeses such as Brie and Camembert typically contain less vitamin D per 100 g due to higher moisture and lower fat concentration. Processed or fortified cheeses can provide substantially more vitamin D when manufacturers add the vitamin deliberately; these products can range widely depending on fortification levels.

When interpreting these numbers, remember that cheese is not a primary dietary source of vitamin D compared with fatty fish or fortified milk. However, cheese contributes complementary fat that can aid absorption of vitamin D from food or supplements.

## Nutritional considerations

Cheese is also a source of protein, calcium, vitamin B12, and other micronutrients that work synergistically with vitamin D for bone and metabolic health. However, some cheeses are high in saturated fat and sodium, so portion control and variety are important. Fortified dairy products (e.g., vitamin D–fortified milk or yogurt) often deliver more consistent vitamin D per serving than natural cheeses.

## Conclusion

Among natural cheeses, Swiss, Gouda, and Cheddar are relatively higher in vitamin D, with Swiss often cited near the top of natural options. Fortified cheeses can provide more vitamin D per serving, but levels vary by product. Cheese should be viewed as a complementary dietary source within a diversified approach that includes fortified foods, natural sources like fish, sensible sun exposure, and evidence-based supplementation when appropriate.
