# Introduction

Vitamins play an indispensable role in maintaining human health, from supporting immune function and cellular repair to enabling cognitive processes. This guide, [Which vitamins are for what table?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/which-vitamins-are-for-what-table), explains how vitamins are organized across different “tables” — supplement labels, nutrition charts, source lists, and deficiency guides — and how to interpret those formats for evidence-based decisions.

## Vitamins in Supplements and Labels

Supplement tables usually list active ingredients, amounts per serving, and %DV (Percentage Daily Value). Common entries include Vitamin C (immune support, antioxidant), Vitamin D (calcium balance, bone health), Vitamin K (blood clotting, bone metabolism), and B-complex vitamins (energy metabolism and neurological function). Minerals such as magnesium often appear alongside vitamins in multiformulas.

Important annotation: water-soluble vitamins (B-complex, C) are excreted when in excess, whereas fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) accumulate and can be toxic in high chronic doses. Reading labels for serving size, units (mg, mcg, IU), and %DV helps avoid inadvertent stacking when combining foods and supplements.

## Nutrition Charts: What to Look For

Nutrition charts on food or supplement packaging show serving size, calories, %DV, and micronutrient content. They are essential for tracking intake and preventing duplication (for example, a fortified beverage plus a multivitamin). Learning to compare labels lets you manage total daily intake and maintain safety margins for fat-soluble vitamins.

## Vitamin Functions at a Glance

- Vitamin A: Vision (especially night vision), epithelial health, immune support. Sources include liver, carrots, and leafy greens.
- B Vitamins: Group of eight supporting energy metabolism, red blood cell production, and nervous system health; B12 is critical for those on plant-based diets.
- Vitamin C: Antioxidant, collagen synthesis, enhances iron absorption; rich in citrus, peppers, and berries.
- Vitamin D: Regulates calcium and bone health, modulates immune responses; obtained from sun exposure and oily fish.
- Vitamin E: Antioxidant that protects cell membranes; found in nuts and seeds.
- Vitamin K: Essential for clotting and bone mineralization; abundant in green vegetables.

If symptoms such as unexplained fatigue, poor wound healing, or easy bruising occur, targeted testing can identify deficiencies needing intervention.

## Choosing the Right Supplement Approach

Multivitamins provide broad coverage but may not address specific clinical needs. Targeted supplements (e.g., Vitamin D or B12) are appropriate when lab results or clinical context warrant them. Best practices include taking fat-soluble vitamins with meals containing fat, considering split doses for some water-soluble nutrients, and avoiding duplicate dosing across products.

## Conclusion

Different “tables” (supplement labels, nutrition charts, and source lists) serve distinct educational purposes: safety, dosing, and practical sourcing. Use whole foods as a primary source of vitamins, supplement only to correct documented gaps or risk factors, and consult testing or a healthcare professional when uncertain. Balance, transparency on labels, and an evidence-based approach ensure safe and effective nutrient management.
