Vitamin A is essential for vision, immune function, and cellular health. It occurs in diets as preformed vitamin A (retinol) from animal sources and as provitamin A carotenoids (notably beta-carotene) from plant foods. Fruits that concentrate beta-carotene can therefore be important contributors to adequate vitamin A status, particularly in plant-based diets.

Among commonly consumed fruits, several stand out for their provitamin A content. Mangoes are often cited as a leading source: their deep orange flesh contains substantial beta-carotene, and one cup of sliced mango supplies a significant proportion of the recommended daily intake. Cantaloupe and papaya also provide high amounts of provitamin A alongside vitamin C and other carotenoids, while apricots—fresh or dried—offer a convenient, concentrated source of beta-carotene.

Quantitatively, published food composition data indicate wide variation across fruits and serving sizes. For example, cantaloupe can deliver several thousand International Units (IU) of vitamin A per cup-equivalent, and mango flesh often provides over a thousand IU per 100 grams. These values depend on ripeness, variety, and preparation method: cooking and pureeing can increase carotenoid bioavailability by breaking down plant matrices, and consuming these fruits with a small amount of dietary fat enhances absorption of fat-soluble provitamins.

From a safety perspective, provitamin A carotenoids are converted to active vitamin A in proportion to the body’s needs, reducing the risk of toxicity that can occur with excessive preformed retinol. Nevertheless, individuals with specific health conditions or those taking high-dose supplements should consult professional guidance.

Nutritionally, selecting fruits for vitamin A should be part of a broader strategy. Many vitamin A–rich fruits also supply fiber, vitamin C, potassium, and antioxidant phytochemicals, producing complementary benefits for skin, mucosal immunity, and metabolic health. In practice, calling a single fruit the absolute “king” of vitamin A depends on the metric used (IU per 100 g, IU per typical serving, or bioavailability). Mangoes, cantaloupe, papaya, and apricots reliably rank highly and are practical sources of provitamin A when consumed regularly and prepared to enhance absorption.
