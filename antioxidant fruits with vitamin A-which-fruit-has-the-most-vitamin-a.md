Introduction

Vitamin A is essential for vision, immune function, reproduction, and cellular health. While preformed vitamin A (retinol) is found in animal foods, many people obtain provitamin A carotenoids—especially beta‑carotene—from plant sources. Fruits supply carotenoids alongside antioxidants, fiber, and micronutrients that support vitamin A absorption and function.

Mangoes: the top fruit source

Among common fruits, mango consistently ranks highest for provitamin A content. A cup (≈165 g) of raw sliced mango provides roughly 89 micrograms of retinol activity equivalents (RAE), which is a meaningful contribution toward daily needs. Mango flesh is rich in beta‑carotene as well as lutein and zeaxanthin; these carotenoids are converted enzymatically to retinol in the intestine based on physiological demand, which reduces the risk of toxicity compared with preformed vitamin A.

Other carotenoid-rich fruits

Papaya, cantaloupe, and apricots are notable beta‑carotene sources. For example, 100 g of cantaloupe yields appreciable RAE and papaya can supply nearly half of daily vitamin A requirements per 100 g in some samples. Dried apricots concentrate carotenoids further and are useful as nutrient‑dense snacks. Botanically speaking, some commonly referenced vegetables—carrots and pumpkins—are also fruiting structures and are exceptionally rich in beta‑carotene.

Berries and antioxidant synergy

Berries (strawberries, raspberries, blackberries) contain modest amounts of vitamin A precursors but are important because their high polyphenol and vitamin C content can preserve vitamin A function by limiting oxidative degradation. Including berries alongside carotenoid‑rich fruits helps form a nutrient matrix that supports absorption and cellular efficacy.

Practical absorption tips

Because carotenoids are fat‑soluble, consuming fruit with a small amount of dietary fat (olive oil, avocado, nuts, yogurt) improves bioavailability. Antioxidant co‑nutrients (vitamin C, vitamin E, polyphenols) further protect carotenoids during digestion and transport. For dietary planning, combine tropical fruits or melon with healthy fats in salads, smoothies, or breakfast bowls.

Supplement considerations

When supplements are used, fruit extracts or freeze‑dried powders (mango, apricot, carrot) provide carotenoids that the body converts as needed. This regulated conversion generally offers a safety advantage over high doses of preformed vitamin A; however, high supplemental beta‑carotene is not recommended for smokers. Summary

Mango is the leading fruit source of vitamin A precursors among common fruits, with papaya, cantaloupe, and apricot also providing significant beta‑carotene. Eating a variety of carotenoid‑rich fruits with small amounts of dietary fat and other antioxidants optimizes conversion and utility.
