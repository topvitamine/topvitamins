## Overview

Ensuring adequate daily vitamin intake supports energy production, immune function, bone health, and cognitive performance. Dietary sources remain the preferred route, but modern diets, limited sun exposure, and life-stage changes mean targeted supplementation can be helpful for many people. For a concise primer, see [Which vitamins should I take every day?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/which-vitamins-should-i-take-every-day).

## Core vitamins to consider

- Vitamin D: Critical for calcium absorption, bone health, and immune modulation. Many adults—especially those in low-sunlight regions or with limited outdoor time—have suboptimal vitamin D status. Typical maintenance doses range from 10–20 µg (400–800 IU), with higher therapeutic doses used under medical supervision.

- Vitamin C: A water-soluble antioxidant involved in collagen synthesis and immune resilience. The EU Nutrient Reference Value is 80 mg; some people supplement at higher doses for short periods when needed.

- B‑complex (including B12 and folate): B vitamins support energy metabolism, red blood cell formation, and nervous system function. B12 is particularly important for older adults and people following vegetarian or vegan diets.

- Vitamin K: Important for bone metabolism and normal blood clotting. It works synergistically with vitamin D for skeletal health.

- Magnesium (mineral, not a vitamin): Plays roles in energy production, muscle function, and hundreds of enzymatic reactions. Many diets are borderline low in magnesium.

- Omega‑3s (EPA/DHA): Not vitamins, but essential fatty acids that support brain and cardiovascular health.

## Individual needs and safety

Daily requirements vary with age, sex, pregnancy, medical conditions, and medication use. Reference values such as EU NRVs or national RDAs provide useful baselines but may not reflect individual needs. Excessive intake—particularly of fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)—can cause harm, so avoid high doses without clinical indication.

Simple clinical cues (fatigue, brittle nails, slow wound healing, mood changes) may suggest suboptimal status, but objective assessment through blood testing is the most reliable approach. Work with a healthcare professional to interpret results and set safe dosages.

## Practical guidance for supplementation

- Follow established reference values and upper tolerable limits; use higher therapeutic doses only when supervised.  
- Take fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) with a meal containing healthy fats to improve absorption.  
- Distribute water‑soluble vitamins (B‑complex, C) across the day if large doses are taken.  
- Consider a baseline daily regimen that addresses common shortfalls (vitamin D, B12 if at risk, vitamin C and magnesium), then personalize further based on testing and lifestyle.

## Conclusion

A small set of nutrients—vitamin D, vitamin C, B‑complex, vitamin K, magnesium, and omega‑3s—covers common gaps for many adults, but personalization is key. Use reference values as a starting point, prioritize testing when uncertain, and coordinate supplementation with a healthcare professional to ensure safety and efficacy.
