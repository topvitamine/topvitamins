## What does vitamin A do to your body?

Vitamin A is a fat‑soluble micronutrient with diverse roles across vision, immunity, skin health, cellular differentiation, and antioxidant defense. It exists as preformed retinoids (retinol and retinyl esters) in animal foods and as provitamin A carotenoids (notably beta‑carotene) in plant foods. Because vitamin A is fat‑soluble, dietary fat and intestinal absorption influence how effectively it is taken up and stored for later use.

Vision depends on vitamin A for the formation of rhodopsin, a retinal protein required for low‑light sight. Inadequate vitamin A impairs the visual cycle and can lead to night blindness and, in severe deficiency, xerophthalmia and corneal damage. Public health supplementation programs have demonstrated clear reductions in childhood blindness where deficiency is endemic, and regulatory bodies provide intake guidelines to balance benefit and safety. Vitamin A contributes to innate and adaptive immune function by maintaining epithelial and mucosal barriers and modulating immune cell differentiation and cytokine responses. Adequate vitamin A supports mucosal integrity in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts, reducing susceptibility to infection and aiding recovery. Meta‑analyses have shown benefits of targeted supplementation in populations with documented deficiency, though excess preformed vitamin A can impair immune balance and cause toxicity.

Dermatologically, retinoids—active derivatives of vitamin A—regulate keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation, enhance collagen synthesis, and support wound healing. Both topical and systemic retinoids are used therapeutically for acne, psoriasis, and photoaging; however, they must be used within recommended parameters because overuse may cause irritation, dryness, or heightened photosensitivity.

On a cellular level, vitamin A binds nuclear receptors that control gene expression related to growth and morphogenesis. It is essential during fetal development for organogenesis and in infancy for normal growth. Because very high intakes of preformed vitamin A are teratogenic, pregnancy recommendations often favor provitamin A sources or carefully dosed supplements under clinical guidance.

As an antioxidant, beta‑carotene and other carotenoids help neutralize reactive oxygen species, supporting cellular redox balance. Diets rich in carotenoid‑containing fruits and vegetables are associated with lower rates of certain chronic diseases, although high‑dose isolated beta‑carotene supplementation has shown adverse outcomes in specific subgroups (for example, smokers), underscoring the importance of context and combined nutrient strategies.

Selecting a nutritional approach requires attention to form (retinol vs beta‑carotene), dose, life stage, and health status. Those with malabsorption conditions, restrictive diets, or increased physiological need may benefit from carefully chosen supplements; clinical supervision reduces the risk of toxicity.
