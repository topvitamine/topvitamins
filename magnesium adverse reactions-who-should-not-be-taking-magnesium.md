Introduction

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of enzymatic processes, including nerve transmission, muscle contraction, and energy metabolism. While dietary magnesium is generally safe, supplemental magnesium can pose risks for certain groups. This article summarizes evidence-based contraindications, common adverse effects, drug interactions, and monitoring considerations to help clinicians and informed readers weigh benefits and potential harms.

Who should avoid or limit magnesium supplements?

- People with impaired renal function: The kidneys excrete most absorbed magnesium. Individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD) or acute renal failure cannot reliably clear excess magnesium and are at risk of hypermagnesemia, which can present with nausea, hypotension, muscle weakness, diminished reflexes, respiratory depression, and cardiac arrhythmias. Any supplementation in CKD should be supervised and monitored with serum magnesium testing.

- Individuals with neuromuscular conditions: Conditions such as myasthenia gravis or advanced neuromuscular junction disorders may be exacerbated by magnesium’s muscle-relaxant effects. Clinicians should evaluate neuromuscular status before recommending supplements.

- Patients on interacting medications: Magnesium reduces absorption of certain oral antibiotics (tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones), bisphosphonates, and levothyroxine if taken concurrently. Diuretics (loop, thiazide, potassium-sparing) alter magnesium balance and can either increase loss or retention depending on type; combined use requires monitoring of electrolytes.

- People with gastrointestinal disorders causing unpredictable absorption: Severe inflammatory bowel disease, chronic diarrhea, or malabsorption syndromes can lead to unpredictable magnesium levels and may warrant individualized assessment.

- Older adults and polypharmacy patients: Aging is associated with reduced renal reserve and increased medication use. The elderly commonly take drugs that interact with magnesium and therefore benefit from careful dosing and periodic lab checks.

Safety considerations and common adverse reactions

Oral magnesium commonly causes dose-dependent gastrointestinal effects (bloating, cramps, osmotic diarrhea), especially with magnesium oxide and citrate. Rare but serious toxicity (hypermagnesemia) occurs with very high doses, impaired excretion, or combined use of multiple magnesium-containing products (e.g., antacids, laxatives, supplements). Reported severe effects include hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, and cardiac arrest.

Guidance for safer use

- Assess renal function and medication list before initiating supplements; monitor serum magnesium in at-risk individuals.
- Space magnesium and interacting oral medications by 2–4 hours to reduce absorption interference.
- Prefer dietary sources (leafy greens, nuts, seeds) where possible; supplementation should be tailored to deficiency evidence.
- Start with low supplemental doses and reassess benefits and adverse effects rather than assuming higher doses are better.

Conclusion

Magnesium supplementation can be beneficial but is not universally safe. Individuals with renal impairment, certain neuromuscular disorders, or those taking interacting medications require individualized evaluation and monitoring. Evidence-based, conservative dosing and clinician oversight reduce the likelihood of adverse outcomes.
