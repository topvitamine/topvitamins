Vitamin A is a fat-soluble micronutrient with well-established roles in vision, immune function, skin health, cellular growth, and antioxidant defense. It exists as preformed retinoids (retinol, retinal, retinoic acid) in animal foods and as provitamin A carotenoids (notably beta-carotene) in plant sources. Dietary adequacy supports a variety of physiological processes, while both deficiency and excess carry health risks.

For a concise overview of its benefits and uses, see [What is vitamin A good for?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-is-vitamin-a-good-for).

Vision: Vitamin A derivatives are central to the visual cycle. In the retina, 11-cis-retinal combines with opsins to form light-sensitive pigments such as rhodopsin, which enable scotopic (low-light) vision. Insufficient vitamin A first manifests as night blindness and, with prolonged deficiency, can lead to xerophthalmia and corneal damage. Public health supplementation in deficient populations reduces blindness risk and related morbidity.

Immune support: Vitamin A maintains the integrity of epithelial barriers in the skin and mucosa, serving as a frontline defense against pathogens. Its active metabolite, retinoic acid, modulates gene expression in immune cells—affecting T- and B-cell differentiation, mucosal homing, and cytokine production. Observational and interventional evidence links deficiency to higher rates of infectious disease, particularly in children.

Skin health and dermatology: Retinoids are widely used topically and systemically to regulate keratinization, promote collagen synthesis, and accelerate epithelial repair. Topical retinoic acid and related compounds can improve acne, photoaging, and dyspigmentation. Oral retinoids are effective for specific dermatologic conditions but require medical supervision because of systemic effects and teratogenicity.

Cell growth and development: Retinoic acid functions as a signaling molecule during embryogenesis and in tissue differentiation. Adequate vitamin A is essential for fetal organogenesis, childhood growth, and ongoing cellular turnover in adults. However, pregnancy demands careful management of intake due to teratogenic risk at high doses.

Antioxidant properties: Beta-carotene and other carotenoids provide antioxidant benefits by quenching reactive oxygen species and helping protect lipids, proteins, and DNA from oxidative damage. These compounds act synergistically with other antioxidants—vitamin C, vitamin E—and with omega-3 fatty acids to support cellular resilience. High-dose beta-carotene supplementation has shown mixed outcomes in specific populations (e.g., smokers), so risk stratification is important.

Safety and dosing: Recommended intakes vary by age, sex, and regulatory body; for adults, guidance typically centers around 700–900 µg retinol activity equivalents per day, with established upper limits to prevent hypervitaminosis A. Chronic excess can cause nausea, liver dysfunction, and increased intracranial pressure. Because vitamin A is fat-soluble and stored in the liver, clinicians balance therapeutic benefit against toxicity when advising supplements.
