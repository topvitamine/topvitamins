Fatigue is a common and often multifactorial complaint characterized by persistent physical or mental tiredness that interferes with daily life. While lifestyle factors—sleep, diet, exercise, and stress—are primary contributors, micronutrient insufficiencies can also play a significant role. This article summarizes evidence-based vitamins and nutrients that are commonly implicated in low energy and offers practical guidance for addressing them.

Core vitamins and minerals

- Vitamin B12: Essential for red blood cell formation, neurological function, and methylation. Deficiency frequently causes fatigue, cognitive slowing, and neuropathic symptoms. Populations at higher risk include older adults, people with malabsorption, and those following strict vegan diets.

- Iron: Although technically a mineral, iron is crucial for oxygen transport via hemoglobin. Iron-deficiency anemia is a leading global cause of physical fatigue. Laboratory testing (ferritin, hemoglobin) is recommended before initiating supplementation.

- Vitamin D: Observational studies associate low vitamin D levels with increased fatigue and depressive symptoms. Supplementation can normalize levels and may improve energy in deficient individuals.

- Magnesium: Participates in ATP synthesis and neuromuscular function. Low magnesium is linked to muscle weakness, cramps, sleep disturbances, and reduced energy.

- Vitamin C and Folate (B9): Vitamin C supports immune function and iron absorption; folate works with B12 in cell synthesis. Deficiencies can contribute to lethargy and impaired recovery.

Adjunctive supplements with supportive evidence

- Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10): An antioxidant integral to mitochondrial ATP production. Trials in specific populations (e.g., statin-associated fatigue, chronic conditions) show modest benefits.

- Adaptogens (Rhodiola, Ashwagandha): Plant-derived compounds that may improve resilience to stress and reduce subjective fatigue in short-term trials.

- Omega-3 fatty acids (DHA/EPA): May support cognitive energy and reduce inflammation-related tiredness; algae-derived formulations provide a plant-based option.

Diagnosis and testing

Because causes of fatigue are heterogeneous, targeted laboratory tests can clarify whether supplementation is appropriate. Common tests include serum B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA) if B12 deficiency is suspected, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, ferritin and complete blood count, and basic metabolic panels. Self-prescribing megadoses without testing increases risk of side effects or masking other conditions.

Practical considerations

- Bioavailability matters: choose absorbable forms (e.g., methylcobalamin for B12, heme or well-absorbed iron forms, magnesium glycinate) and products with third-party verification where possible.

- Combine with lifestyle changes: adequate sleep, balanced macronutrients, regular activity, and stress management amplify nutritional interventions.

- Professional guidance: consult a clinician or registered dietitian for persistent or unexplained fatigue to rule out medical causes and design a safe supplementation plan.
