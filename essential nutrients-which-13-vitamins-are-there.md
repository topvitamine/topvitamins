Introduction

Vitamins are organic micronutrients required in small amounts but essential for normal physiological function. The human diet supplies 13 vitamins that cannot be synthesized in sufficient quantities endogenously; they support metabolism, immune function, bone health, blood clotting, and neurological processes. This article summarizes those 13 vitamins, their main roles, common food sources, and signs of deficiency.

Classification and why it matters

Vitamins are grouped as water-soluble (vitamin C and the B-complex: B1, B2, B3, B5, B6, B7, B9, B12) and fat-soluble (A, D, E, K). Water-soluble vitamins are not stored in significant amounts and require regular intake; fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed with dietary fats and can accumulate in tissues. Understanding this classification helps guide dietary planning and informs supplementation decisions for populations at risk of deficiency.

The 13 essential vitamins (brief overview)

- Vitamin A (retinol, provitamin A): Supports vision, epithelial health, and immune responses. Sources include liver, dairy, and orange or dark green vegetables (beta‑carotene).
- Vitamin B1 (thiamine): Important for carbohydrate metabolism and nerve function; found in whole grains, legumes, and pork.
- Vitamin B2 (riboflavin): Involved in redox reactions and energy production; sources include dairy, eggs, and leafy greens.
- Vitamin B3 (niacin): Precursor for NAD/NADP cofactors; abundant in meat, fish, and fortified grains.
- Vitamin B5 (pantothenic acid): Required for coenzyme A synthesis and steroid production; present in many foods (chicken, potatoes, whole grains).
- Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine): Cofactor in amino acid metabolism and neurotransmitter synthesis; found in meat, legumes, and bananas.
- Vitamin B7 (biotin): Supports carboxylase enzymes in fatty acid and gluconeogenesis pathways; eggs, nuts, and vegetables provide biotin.
- Vitamin B9 (folate/folic acid): Central to nucleotide synthesis and cell division; high in leafy greens, beans, and fortified grains.
- Vitamin B12 (cobalamin): Needed for myelin maintenance and red blood cell formation; primarily in animal products and fortified foods.
- Vitamin C (ascorbic acid): Antioxidant and collagen cofactor; abundant in citrus, bell peppers, and broccoli.
- Vitamin D (cholecalciferol/ergocalciferol): Regulates calcium and phosphorus absorption for bone health; synthesized in skin with UVB exposure and present in fatty fish and fortified foods.
- Vitamin E (tocopherols/tocotrienols): Lipid‑soluble antioxidant protecting cell membranes; sources include vegetable oils, nuts, and seeds.
- Vitamin K (phylloquinone and menaquinones): Required for gamma‑carboxylation of clotting factors and bone proteins; found in green leafy vegetables and fermented foods.

Deficiency and public health considerations

Deficiency presentations vary: scurvy for vitamin C, rickets/osteomalacia for vitamin D, megaloblastic anemia for folate or B12, and night blindness for vitamin A. Populations at risk include those with restrictive diets, malabsorption syndromes, older adults, and individuals with limited sun exposure. Dietary assessment and targeted laboratory testing guide correction strategies.

Summary

Maintaining adequate vitamin intake through diverse foods is the primary strategy for preventing deficiency. When diet alone is insufficient, evidence-based supplementation—guided by healthcare professionals and informed by laboratory testing—can address specific needs while avoiding risks associated with excessive intake, particularly of fat‑soluble vitamins.
