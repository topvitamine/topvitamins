Dizziness is a common complaint with many potential causes. Beyond cardiovascular, vestibular, and psychological contributors, nutritional factors — notably vitamin D status — have been investigated for their potential role in balance, gait, and vertigo. This article summarizes current evidence on how low vitamin D might relate to dizziness and practical considerations for clinicians and patients.

Vitamin D and balance: basic biology

Vitamin D supports calcium metabolism, muscle function, and neurological health. Vitamin D receptors are expressed in skeletal muscle, inner ear tissue, and brain regions involved in sensory integration. Deficiency can impair muscle strength and proprioception, reduce neuromuscular coordination, and alter inner ear calcium handling — all systems that contribute to postural control and spatial orientation. Mechanisms linking low vitamin D to dizziness

1) Muscle weakness: Inadequate vitamin D reduces calcium flux in muscle, impairing contraction and endurance. Leg and trunk weakness can produce unsteadiness that patients describe as lightheadedness or imbalance.

2) Inner ear (vestibular) effects: Otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) in the vestibular apparatus are important for detecting head position. Vitamin D helps regulate calcium homeostasis, and deficiency has been associated with structural changes that may predispose to benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV).

3) Neurological modulation: Vitamin D influences neurotrophic factors, neurotransmitter synthesis, and neuroimmune balance. Low levels have been correlated with cognitive complaints, mood changes, and increased susceptibility to dizziness in observational studies.

Clinical evidence

Observational studies have found lower mean serum 25(OH)D levels in patients with recurrent BPPV compared with controls. Some randomized, controlled trials and cohort studies suggest that vitamin D repletion may reduce recurrence of BPPV and improve measures of balance in older adults, though effect sizes vary and not all trials show benefit. Meta-analyses indicate a modest reduction in fall risk with vitamin D supplementation in certain populations, supporting a plausible role in balance preservation.

Practical considerations

- Assessment: When dizziness, falls, or recurrent BPPV are unexplained by routine ENT or neurological evaluation, measure serum 25(OH)D as part of a broader workup. Interpretation should follow local laboratory reference ranges and clinical context.

- Management: Correcting deficiency with vitamin D3 under medical supervision can be considered alongside vestibular rehabilitation, strength and balance training, and management of comorbidities. Co-nutrients such as magnesium and vitamin K may support calcium balance but should be individualized.

- Limitations: Dizziness is multifactorial. Vitamin D may be one contributing factor among many. High-quality randomized trials are still needed to define which patients benefit most from supplementation specifically for vestibular symptoms.

Summary

Low vitamin D is biologically plausible as a contributory factor in dizziness, particularly where muscle weakness, impaired proprioception, or recurrent BPPV exist. Clinical evaluation should be comprehensive, and vitamin D testing and repletion should be considered as part of an integrated, evidence-based approach overseen by a healthcare provider.
