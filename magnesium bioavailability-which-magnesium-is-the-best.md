## Quick summary

Magnesium is essential for hundreds of enzymatic reactions, affecting muscle, nerve, bone, heart rhythm and energy metabolism. Bioavailability and intended use determine which form is most appropriate: magnesium citrate and glycinate are generally best absorbed, oxide is inexpensive but poorly absorbed, malate supports energy, threonate targets the brain, and chloride or sulfate are often used topically. For a full reference guide see [Which magnesium is the best?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/which-magnesium-is-the-best).

## Understanding forms and absorption

Not all magnesium salts are absorbed equally. Magnesium citrate and magnesium glycinate show high bioavailability and are suitable for correcting deficiency or supporting sleep and mood. Magnesium oxide contains more elemental magnesium per dose but has low absorption and a higher chance of GI side effects. Magnesium malate pairs magnesium with malic acid, which is involved in cellular ATP production and may help fatigue. Emerging evidence suggests magnesium threonate can cross the blood–brain barrier and could support cognition, though studies are still evolving. Topical forms such as magnesium chloride and Epsom salt (magnesium sulfate) are used for local muscle relaxation; oral absorption of sulfate is limited.

## Dosage considerations

General daily intakes vary by age and sex; typical adult ranges are about 300–400 mg/day from all sources. Supplemental guidance often distinguishes elemental magnesium and the salt it’s bound to; higher elemental amounts do not automatically mean more is absorbed. Some regulatory bodies note a supplemental tolerable upper intake for magnesium salts (often cited around 250 mg/day for supplemental magnesium) because higher doses, especially from poorly absorbed salts, increase risk of diarrhea. Splitting doses and taking magnesium with food can improve tolerability.

## Recognizing deficiency

Symptoms of low magnesium can be nonspecific: muscle cramps, twitches, fatigue, irregular heartbeat, mood changes, migraines, and poor sleep. Certain groups are at higher risk — older adults, people with gastrointestinal disorders, athletes with high sweat losses, those on some diuretics or proton pump inhibitors, and people with poorly varied diets.

## Dietary sources and practical tips

Prioritize magnesium-rich foods: dark leafy greens (spinach, chard), nuts and seeds (almonds, pumpkin seeds), legumes, whole grains, and fatty fish. Light cooking preserves mineral content. Vitamin D and B6 support magnesium utilization, while very high supplemental calcium may compete with absorption.

## Choosing a form for your goals

- Sleep/anxiety: magnesium glycinate (well tolerated, minimal laxative effect)
- Constipation/general deficiency: magnesium citrate (high absorption, mild laxative action)
- Energy/fatigue: magnesium malate (supports mitochondrial function)
- Cognitive support: magnesium threonate (brain penetration in early research)
- Budget option: magnesium oxide (low absorption; may suit short-term needs)
