## Introduction

Multivitamins are commonly used to fill dietary gaps and support routine health. Deciding whether to take a multivitamin every day depends on your diet, life stage, medical history, and the formulation and dose of the product. This article summarizes evidence-based considerations on daily use, absorption, dosage, benefits, and safety.

## Why some people take a daily multivitamin

A multivitamin typically combines essential vitamins and minerals that people might not get consistently from food. Water-soluble nutrients (B-complex vitamins and vitamin C) are not stored long-term and may require daily replenishment. Older adults, pregnant people, vegans, and those with restricted diets or malabsorption conditions are groups that frequently benefit from consistent supplementation.

## Potential benefits

When used appropriately, multivitamins can help:

- Fill nutritional gaps and support baseline nutrient status.
- Support immune function via vitamins A, C, D and minerals such as zinc and selenium.
- Support energy metabolism through B vitamins involved in ATP production.
- Provide targeted support for life stages (e.g., folate for pregnancy, vitamin D and calcium for bone health in older adults).

These outcomes are generally modest and most pronounced in people who start out nutrient-deficient rather than in those with already adequate diets.

## Dosage and safety considerations

Effective use requires attention to Recommended Daily Allowances (RDAs) and Tolerable Upper Intake Levels (ULs). Multivitamins that provide near-100% of RDAs are common and usually safe for most adults. Be cautious with products that deliver supraphysiologic doses, particularly of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) which can accumulate, or minerals like iron if not indicated.

Medication interactions are important: for example, vitamin K can interfere with warfarin, and high-dose supplements may alter the absorption of other minerals (zinc and copper, iron and calcium). People with chronic conditions or those taking multiple medications should consult a clinician before starting daily supplementation.

## Absorption tips

Nutrient bioavailability depends on formulation and administration: take fat-soluble vitamins with meals containing fat, consider methylated B vitamins if methylation issues are relevant, and recognize that gastrointestinal health affects uptake. Avoid taking competing minerals simultaneously when possible (e.g., separate high-dose iron and calcium).

## Evidence and limitations

Randomized trials and observational studies show mixed results: some demonstrate modest improvements in nutrient status and certain biomarkers, while others find limited impact on long-term disease outcomes in well-nourished populations. ## Practical summary

If you have dietary gaps, a verified nutrient deficiency, or increased requirements, a daily multivitamin that provides balanced dosages can be a reasonable, low-risk strategy to maintain nutrient sufficiency. It is not a substitute for a varied, nutrient-rich diet. Check labels, avoid excessive doses, consider product quality, and discuss ongoing use with a healthcare provider.
