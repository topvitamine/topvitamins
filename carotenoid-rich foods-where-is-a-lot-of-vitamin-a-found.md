## Where Is a Lot of Vitamin A Found?

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble nutrient essential for vision, immune function, reproduction, and cellular communication. It occurs as preformed vitamin A (retinol and retinyl esters) in animal foods and as provitamin A carotenoids (notably beta-carotene) in plants. Both forms support health, but they differ in bioavailability and metabolic handling.

Animal sources provide retinol, the most bioavailable form. Liver (beef, chicken, fish) contains particularly high concentrations; for example, beef liver can exceed daily requirements by manyfold per 100 g. Full-fat dairy and egg yolks are additional retinol sources that pair naturally with dietary fat, aiding absorption. Because preformed vitamin A accumulates in tissues, frequent consumption of high-liver portions or high-dose retinol supplements can risk toxicity, so intake should be moderated.

Plant foods supply carotenoids—pigments that the body converts to active vitamin A as needed. Orange and yellow vegetables such as carrots, sweet potatoes, pumpkins, and butternut squash are consistently rich in beta-carotene. Dark leafy greens (spinach, kale, collards) also contain carotenoids; chlorophyll masks their orange pigment but not their provitamin A content. The enzymatic conversion of carotenoids to retinol varies by individual factors (genetics, health), and carotenoid absorption is improved when consumed with dietary fat (olive oil, nuts, avocado) and by cooking or pureeing to break down plant cell walls.

Globally, vitamin A deficiency remains a major public health concern, especially affecting young children and pregnant women in low-resource settings. Deficiency manifests as night blindness, increased infection risk, and impaired growth. High-dose supplementation programs and food fortification have reduced deficiency burdens in many regions. In clinical contexts, people with malabsorption syndromes (e.g., celiac disease, Crohn’s), very-low-fat diets, or restrictive vegan patterns may also be at risk and benefit from monitoring and tailored interventions.

For balanced intake, combine food-first strategies with appropriate supplementation when needed. Omnivores can rotate liver, full-fat dairy, eggs, and a spectrum of colorful vegetables. Vegetarians and vegans can rely on carotenoid-rich plant foods plus cooking techniques and dietary fats to enhance conversion and uptake; vegan-friendly beta-carotene supplements are available when dietary sources are insufficient.
