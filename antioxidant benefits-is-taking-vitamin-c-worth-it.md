Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is one of the most-studied micronutrients, credited with roles in antioxidant defense, collagen synthesis, and immune function. This article summarizes current evidence so you can decide whether routine supplementation is appropriate for your needs.

Why Vitamin C matters

As a water-soluble vitamin, Vitamin C must be consumed daily via diet or supplements. It participates in collagen formation, supports immune cell function, and neutralizes free radicals in the body’s aqueous compartments. Typical dietary sources include citrus, kiwi, strawberries, bell peppers, and cruciferous vegetables. For many people, a balanced diet supplies sufficient amounts; for others—especially smokers, those under physiological stress, or with restrictive diets—supplementation acts as nutritional insurance.

Evidence for immune support and cold outcomes

Clinical research indicates that regular Vitamin C intake can modestly reduce the duration and severity of upper respiratory infections. Meta-analyses find reductions in cold length of around 8% in adults and up to 14% in children with routine supplementation (commonly ≥200 mg/day). However, in the general population, routine Vitamin C does not appear to prevent the initial occurrence of colds, and beginning high-dose supplementation only after symptoms start shows inconsistent benefits.

Antioxidant and cardiovascular considerations

Vitamin C complements the body’s antioxidant network by scavenging reactive oxygen species and regenerating oxidized Vitamin E. Observational studies associate higher plasma Vitamin C with lower markers of oxidative stress and improved endothelial function. Targeted supplementation may benefit individuals exposed to elevated oxidative load (e.g., smokers, people with chronic inflammatory conditions), but indiscriminate high-dose antioxidant use can blunt adaptive responses to stressors such as exercise.

Skin health and collagen

Because Vitamin C is a cofactor for enzymes involved in collagen biosynthesis, adequate intake supports skin integrity, wound healing, and elasticity. Clinical trials report improvements in skin texture and reduced fine lines with oral Vitamin C supplementation, particularly when combined with other nutrients. Topical Vitamin C formulations also deliver localized antioxidant protection and can reduce hyperpigmentation and photodamage.

Safety and dosing

Recommended intakes vary by authority, but a typical range is around 95–110 mg/day for adults, with many studies using 200–1,000 mg/day for clinical effects. The tolerable upper intake level is commonly cited near 2,000 mg/day; higher intakes raise the risk of gastrointestinal symptoms like diarrhea and cramps. Interactions with medications are uncommon but possible; consult a healthcare professional when in doubt.

Practical guidance

Decide based on diet, health status, and lifestyle. If your diet includes a variety of fruits and vegetables, supplements may offer limited incremental benefit. If intake is inconsistent, or you face increased oxidative or immune stress, moderate daily supplementation (within established safe limits) is reasonable.
