Introduction

Vitamin A is critical for vision, immune function, and epithelial health. Deficiency can develop from inadequate intake, malabsorption, or increased needs, and its early signs are often subtle. This article summarizes common clinical indicators and practical considerations for recognition and management, with an emphasis on evidence-based associations.

Key ocular signs

Eye involvement is among the most specific manifestations. Early complaints include dry eyes, increased light sensitivity, and difficulty adapting to low-light conditions. Classic findings with prolonged deficiency include Bitot’s spots (foamy conjunctival patches) and, in severe cases, corneal ulcers and keratomalacia that can threaten sight. Night blindness (nyctalopia) is a characteristic early symptom, reflecting impaired rhodopsin regeneration in rod photoreceptors.

Night vision and function

Patients with low vitamin A commonly report trouble driving or moving safely in dim environments, and a longer delay when adapting from bright to dark rooms. Clinical studies indicate that appropriate vitamin A repletion can restore night vision within days to weeks in deficient individuals, highlighting the nutrient’s direct role in phototransduction.

Immune system implications

Vitamin A contributes to mucosal barrier integrity and the function of innate and adaptive immune cells. Deficiency is associated with increased frequency and severity of respiratory and gastrointestinal infections, delayed wound healing, and poorer outcomes from some infectious diseases. Public health interventions in at-risk populations have shown that targeted vitamin A supplementation reduces severity and mortality from certain infections, particularly in young children.

Skin and hair manifestations

Because vitamin A regulates epithelial cell growth and differentiation, deficiency often presents with dermatological signs: dry, scaly skin; follicular hyperkeratosis (rough bumps on extensor surfaces); flaky scalp; and, in some cases, hair thinning. These signs may improve with correction of deficiency, although assessment should also consider other nutritional and dermatological causes.

Early visual changes and monitoring

Beyond night blindness, early visual complaints can include blurred vision, difficulty focusing, and increased visual fatigue. Routine eye examinations and dietary assessment help identify progressive deficits before irreversible damage occurs. In clinical practice, addressing potential malabsorption or liver disease is important when low serum retinol is suspected.

Conclusion

Vitamin A deficiency produces a recognizable cluster of signs—especially night blindness, dry or irritated eyes, recurrent infections, and characteristic skin changes. Early recognition and appropriate clinical evaluation of dietary intake and absorption are essential. Where deficiency is confirmed, evidence-based repletion under professional guidance improves outcomes and reduces the risk of irreversible ocular damage.
