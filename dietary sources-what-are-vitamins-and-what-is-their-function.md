Vitamins are organic micronutrients required in small amounts for a wide range of physiological functions. Unlike macronutrients that provide calories, vitamins act primarily as regulators and facilitators: they support enzymatic reactions, maintain tissue integrity, aid immune responses, and contribute to cellular growth and repair. Because most vitamins are not synthesized in sufficient amounts by the human body, they must be obtained through diet or supplementation.

Vitamins are broadly classified as water-soluble (the B-complex group and vitamin C) and fat-soluble (vitamins A, D, E, and K). Water-soluble vitamins generally require regular intake because they are not stored extensively, whereas fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed with dietary fat and can be stored in the liver and adipose tissue. This distinction influences both dosing strategies and the risk profile for insufficiency or excess.

As co-factors and coenzymes, many vitamins enable metabolic processes. B vitamins, for example, are central to carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism and support mitochondrial energy production. Vitamin D facilitates calcium absorption and bone mineralization, while vitamin C participates in collagen synthesis and acts as an antioxidant. Deficiencies can present as fatigue, impaired immune function, dermatological changes, or more specific clinical syndromes depending on the nutrient involved.

Absorption and bioavailability vary by nutrient and individual factors. Age, gastrointestinal health, medication use, and dietary composition can all affect how well vitamins are taken up and utilized. For instance, plant-based iron is better absorbed when consumed with vitamin C, while fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed more effectively with meals containing fat. Vitamins also play key roles in immune competence. Vitamin C supports leukocyte function and antioxidant defense, vitamin D modulates innate and adaptive immunity, and vitamin A maintains mucosal barriers. B vitamins contribute to the formation and function of immune cells. Dietary sources remain the preferred route for most vitamins: fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, dairy, nuts, and seeds supply a spectrum of micronutrients. However, modern food processing, limited sun exposure, and individual dietary choices can create gaps.
