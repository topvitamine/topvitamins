Introduction

Reaching age 60 often changes how the body absorbs and uses nutrients. Reduced skin synthesis of vitamin D, lower gastric acid and intrinsic factor affecting B12 absorption, changes in renal clearance, and the common use of multiple medications all influence which vitamins deserve attention. A food-first approach combined with test-guided, targeted supplementation usually offers the safest and most effective strategy.

Key vitamins and why they matter

Vitamin D and calcium are primary considerations for many adults over 60 because they support bone mineral density and muscle function. When sunlight exposure or dietary intake is limited, supplemental vitamin D can help restore serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D to recommended ranges. Vitamin B12 is another frequent priority: age-related absorption decline and some drugs (e.g., metformin, proton pump inhibitors) can lead to deficiency, which affects nervous system function and energy levels.

Antioxidant nutrients such as vitamin C and vitamin E contribute to cellular protection from oxidative stress, and B vitamins (B6, folate, B12) support energy-yielding metabolism and nervous system health. Magnesium and vitamin K (notably K2 forms) play supporting roles for bone and cardiovascular health, while omega-3 long-chain fatty acids (DHA/EPA) are linked to normal brain and heart function. Assessment and personalization

Individual needs vary. Baseline testing (for example, serum 25(OH)D for vitamin D status; serum B12 and methylmalonic acid for B12) and a review of diet, lifestyle, comorbidities, and medications should guide decisions. People following plant-based diets often require B12 and may need attention to calcium and long-chain omega-3s. Those with limited sun exposure, darker skin, or living at higher latitudes commonly benefit from vitamin D assessment and, if needed, supplementation.

Safety and interactions

Safety considerations are essential. Fat-soluble vitamins can accumulate—clinicians are cautious with long-term high-dose vitamin A or D. Vitamin K interacts with vitamin K antagonists; high-dose vitamin E may affect bleeding risk. Minerals like magnesium and calcium require consideration of renal function and drug interactions. A pharmacist or clinician review of all medicines and supplements helps identify potential conflicts and appropriate timing.

Practical tips

1. Prioritize a varied diet: fatty fish, dairy or fortified alternatives, leafy greens, legumes, nuts, whole grains, and a range of fruits and vegetables.  
2. Use targeted supplements when testing or clinical indicators show deficiency rather than routine mega-dosing.  
3. Monitor: periodic lab tests and clinical follow-up help ensure adequacy without excess.

Conclusion

No single vitamin fits everyone at age 60. Vitamin D and B12 are commonly prioritized due to age-related changes, but assessment and individualized planning provide the most reliable path to safe, evidence-based supplementation.
