Vitamin D supports bone health, immune function, and many metabolic processes, yet deficiency is common worldwide. Multiple factors — what some call “vitamin D thieves” — can reduce synthesis, absorption, storage, or activation of this nutrient, often without obvious symptoms.

Sunlight is the primary natural source, but endogenous production is influenced by geography, season, time of day, skin pigmentation and age. People living above ~37° latitude, those who spend most time indoors, or those who routinely use high-SPF sunscreen will synthesise less vitamin D. Darker skin contains more melanin, which reduces UVB penetration and may require substantially longer sun exposure to produce equivalent vitamin D compared with lighter skin.

Dietary and digestive factors also matter. Vitamin D is fat-soluble and requires dietary fat and intact lipid absorption for optimal uptake. Conditions that impair fat absorption — celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, cystic fibrosis, or long-term use of some acid-suppressing medications — can reduce serum 25(OH)D. Older adults often have reduced bile production and digestive efficiency, further limiting absorption.

Body composition and storage play a role: vitamin D is sequestered in adipose tissue. Obesity can lower circulating 25(OH)D concentrations because more of the vitamin is partitioned into fat stores and is less bioavailable. Genetic factors — including polymorphisms in the vitamin D receptor (VDR) or in enzymes involved in vitamin D metabolism — can alter transport, activation and cellular responses, meaning two people with similar intakes can have different functional status.

Medications and metabolic clearance are additional thieves. Certain anticonvulsants, glucocorticoids and rifampin induce hepatic enzymes that accelerate vitamin D catabolism. Chronic liver disease impairs 25-hydroxylation, and chronic kidney disease can limit conversion to the active 1,25-dihydroxy form. Inflammation and hormonal imbalances (for example, altered parathyroid hormone levels) also perturb activation pathways.

Hidden environmental and lifestyle contributors include indoor lighting (which does not provide UVB), food processing losses, and interactions with other nutrients. Magnesium and vitamin K are cofactors in vitamin D metabolism and bone health; low levels can blunt vitamin D’s effectiveness even when blood levels appear adequate.

Diagnosis relies on measuring serum 25(OH)D. Symptoms can be nonspecific — fatigue, musculoskeletal pain, increased infections or mood changes — so testing is the most reliable method to detect deficiency and guide management. Management strategies should address both intake and the underlying thieves: moderate, safe sun exposure tailored by skin type and latitude; choosing fat-containing meals with oral vitamin D; addressing malabsorption or drug interactions with clinicians; and monitoring levels in chronic liver or kidney disease.
