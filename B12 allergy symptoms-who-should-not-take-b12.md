Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for nerve function, red blood cell production, and DNA synthesis. While supplementation can correct deficiency in many people, it is not universally appropriate. This article describes key contraindications, interactions, allergic concerns, and scenarios where B12 may be ineffective or harmful.

Who should be cautious

Certain medical conditions and genetic disorders make B12 supplementation risky or ineffective. People with Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) are advised to avoid some forms of B12, particularly cyanocobalamin, because it may worsen mitochondrial dysfunction in optic nerve cells. Individuals with known hypersensitivity to cobalt or components of injectable or oral formulations can develop allergic reactions ranging from rash to anaphylaxis.

Other groups requiring medical oversight include those with polycythemia vera (where additional stimulation of red cell production could be problematic), advanced chronic kidney disease (altered B12 handling has been associated with adverse outcomes in some studies), and patients undergoing chemotherapy or immunosuppression, where nutrient effects on cell replication warrant clinician guidance.

Allergy and intolerance

True B12 allergy is uncommon but possible. Signs include urticaria, swelling of the face or lips (angioedema), breathing difficulty, and, rarely, anaphylaxis. Reactions can be due to the cobalamin molecule, the cobalt center, or inactive ingredients such as preservatives and fillers used in injections or tablets. Non-allergic intolerances—such as gastrointestinal upset, bloating, or headaches—are often linked to additives rather than B12 itself.

Drug interactions to consider

Several medication classes interact with B12 absorption or metabolism. Proton pump inhibitors and other acid-suppressing drugs can reduce absorption of B12 from food; alternative routes (sublingual or intramuscular) or monitored supplementation may be needed. Long-term metformin use is associated with lower B12 levels and periodic monitoring is recommended for people on this medication. Some antibiotics and combinations used with levodopa may also affect B12 function or drug efficacy. Always inform clinicians about supplements when medications are prescribed.

When supplementation won’t help

Absorption or utilization defects mean oral B12 may be ineffective. Pernicious anemia and autoimmune gastritis reduce intrinsic factor required for absorption; injections are often the indicated approach. Genetic variants (for example, certain MTHFR-related issues) can alter which B12 form is most appropriate—methylcobalamin or adenosylcobalamin may be preferable to cyanocobalamin in some cases. Misleading lab results that measure total but not active B12 can result in inappropriate supplementation while underlying causes (iron, folate, or other disorders) remain untreated.

Over-supplementation concerns

B12 is water-soluble and overdoses are uncommon, but high-dose supplementation has been linked in some reports to acneiform eruptions, gastrointestinal upset, and rare neurologic complaints. Long-term, very high serum levels should be evaluated by a clinician to rule out underlying pathology or interactions with other nutrients.

Practical approach

Screening with appropriate blood tests, medication review, and medical history is the best way to decide whether B12 supplementation is appropriate. Summary

B12 supplementation is helpful for many but not all. People with LHON, cobalt hypersensitivity, specific blood disorders, certain kidney or autoimmune conditions, or absorption defects should consult a clinician before starting B12. Laboratory assessment and individualized guidance reduce risk and improve outcomes.
