# Which Magnesium Do Doctors Recommend?

Magnesium is a fundamental mineral involved in hundreds of enzymatic reactions, including nerve conduction, muscle contraction, energy production, and bone health. Clinicians typically choose a magnesium form based on absorption, tolerability, targeted benefits, and patient-specific factors such as existing conditions and medications.

**Quick summary:** Magnesium glycinate is often preferred for sleep and anxiety due to its gentle profile and bioavailability; magnesium citrate is commonly used for constipation and rapid repletion; magnesium threonate is favored when cognitive support is the focus; magnesium malate is selected for energy and muscle complaints; magnesium oxide is inexpensive but less absorbed and mainly used short-term for digestive issues. ## How doctors decide
Healthcare providers weigh several factors when recommending a form of magnesium: bioavailability, gastrointestinal tolerance, the primary symptom being treated (e.g., insomnia, cramps, constipation, cognitive concerns), interactions with medications, and renal function. Blood magnesium tests and a clinical history help guide individualized dosing and duration.

## Common forms and typical clinical uses
- Magnesium glycinate: A chelated form bound to the amino acid glycine. It is well absorbed and tends to cause fewer GI side effects, making it suitable for sleep support, anxiety management, and long-term repletion.

- Magnesium citrate: A highly soluble form used for constipation and for relatively rapid correction of deficiency. Its osmotic effect can produce loose stools, so clinicians often recommend cautious dosing.

- Magnesium threonate: Designed to cross the blood–brain barrier more readily; emerging evidence suggests potential cognitive benefits (memory and synaptic function), though larger clinical trials are needed.

- Magnesium malate: Bound to malic acid, this form may support cellular energy production and is sometimes used in fatigue syndromes and fibromyalgia management.

- Magnesium oxide: Contains a high percentage of elemental magnesium but has lower absorption. It is commonly used as an antacid or laxative for short-term needs.

## Evidence and safety considerations
Systematic reviews and clinical trials indicate differences in absorption and tolerability among formulations. Chelated forms (e.g., glycinate) and organic salts (e.g., citrate, malate) generally show better bioavailability than inorganic forms like oxide. Adverse effects are mainly gastrointestinal (diarrhea, cramping) and are dose-dependent. Patients with significant renal impairment require specialist guidance because magnesium is renally excreted.
