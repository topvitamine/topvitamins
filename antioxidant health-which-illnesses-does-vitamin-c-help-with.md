**Introduction**

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble micronutrient essential for collagen synthesis, neurotransmitter production, iron absorption, and antioxidant defense. Because humans cannot synthesize or store large amounts of vitamin C, regular dietary intake from fruits, vegetables, or supplements is necessary to maintain physiological function. This article summarizes current evidence on which illnesses and conditions vitamin C can help with, focusing on immune support, respiratory infections, antioxidant protection, and tissue repair.

**Immune support and infection recovery**

Vitamin C contributes to multiple immune processes: it supports the function of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and phagocytes, enhances chemotaxis, and protects immune cells from oxidative damage. Deficiency impairs host defense and increases susceptibility to infections, while acute infections rapidly deplete body stores due to increased metabolic demand. Randomized trials and reviews suggest that regular supplementation (often ≥200 mg/day in study settings) can reduce severity and shorten duration of some infections, especially in people with low baseline intake or those under physical stress. **Common cold and respiratory illnesses**

Large meta-analyses indicate that routine vitamin C supplementation does not markedly lower cold incidence in the general population but does modestly reduce duration and symptom severity. In specific high-stress groups (e.g., endurance athletes, military personnel in cold environments) regular supplementation has been associated with a substantial reduction in cold incidence. For acute colds, divided doses throughout the day or formulations with sustained release can help maintain plasma levels; however, evidence for therapeutic mega-dosing after symptom onset is limited.

**Antioxidant protection and chronic disease risk modulation**

As a potent antioxidant, vitamin C scavenges reactive oxygen species and regenerates other antioxidants such as vitamin E and glutathione. Observational data link higher plasma vitamin C with lower all-cause mortality and reduced markers of oxidative stress. While supplementation can help restore antioxidant capacity—particularly in smokers, those exposed to pollution, or people with inadequate diets—evidence that vitamin C supplementation alone prevents chronic diseases (cardiovascular disease, cancer, neurodegeneration) is mixed and often inconsistent in randomized trials.

**Skin health and wound healing**

Vitamin C is essential for collagen formation, which underpins wound healing and skin integrity. Deficiency leads to impaired collagen synthesis and poor wound repair (scurvy being the extreme manifestation). Topical and systemic vitamin C can support skin recovery and may improve outcomes in deficient individuals; however, clinical benefit in otherwise well-nourished populations varies with dose and formulation.

**Considerations, dosing, and safety**

Recommended intakes differ by region; many trials showing benefit used daily doses in the 200–1000 mg range. The tolerable upper intake level is commonly cited around 2,000 mg/day for adults to avoid gastrointestinal side effects. Individuals with kidney stone risk or certain metabolic conditions should consult a clinician before high-dose supplementation. Vitamin C works synergistically with other nutrients (e.g., vitamin E, iron, zinc), and combined approaches often feature in research protocols.

Summary: Vitamin C helps most clearly with supporting immune cell function, shortening cold duration and severity in some groups, supporting antioxidant defenses, and enabling collagen-dependent tissue repair. Benefits are greatest when supplementation corrects low dietary intake or meets increased demand during illness or stress.
