# Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is an essential nutrient involved in red blood cell formation, neurological function, DNA synthesis, and energy metabolism. Because humans do not synthesize B12 endogenously, intake must come from dietary sources or supplements. For many people—particularly those with dietary restrictions or absorption issues—daily supplementation can be a practical strategy to maintain adequate levels. # How B12 Is Absorbed

B12 absorption is a multi-step process that requires stomach acid to release B12 from food proteins and a stomach-produced glycoprotein called intrinsic factor to transport it to receptors in the ileum. Age-related declines in stomach acid, gastrointestinal disorders, and certain medications (e.g., proton pump inhibitors, metformin) can reduce absorption. Different supplement forms—cyanocobalamin, methylcobalamin, sublingual tablets, or injections—circumvent absorption limitations to varying degrees. # Benefits of Consistent Daily Intake

Regular B12 intake supports energy production through methylation cycles, protects neurological health by maintaining myelin, and prevents megaloblastic anemia by supporting healthy red blood cell formation. Suboptimal B12 levels have been associated with fatigue, cognitive impairment, neuropathy, and mood changes. For some people—particularly older adults, vegans, and those with malabsorption—daily supplementation offers a steady supply that reduces the risk of slow depletion and associated symptoms.

# Dosage and Safety

Recommended intakes vary: many authorities set the adult Recommended Dietary Allowance near 4 mcg/day, but supplements commonly provide 100–1,000 mcg per dose. B12 is water-soluble and excess is usually excreted, so toxicity is rare; nonetheless, individualized dosing is advised. People with confirmed deficiency or malabsorption may require higher oral doses or intramuscular injections under clinical supervision. Monitor serum B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA), and homocysteine as appropriate to guide therapy. Mild adverse effects (e.g., gastrointestinal upset or skin reactions) are uncommon; those with specific conditions (such as Leber’s disease) should consult a clinician before high-dose B12.

# Practical Considerations

Daily supplementation can be a low-risk, effective strategy for maintaining B12 status in at-risk groups: older adults, people following plant-based diets, those on certain medications, and individuals with GI disorders. Choosing an appropriate form (e.g., methylcobalamin for neurological concerns) and confirming need with simple blood tests helps tailor a safe plan. Taking B12 every day is reasonable and often beneficial for people with limited dietary intake or impaired absorption. The decision should be individualized, informed by clinical testing and professional advice, and implemented using an appropriate form and dose to meet personal needs without unnecessary excess.
