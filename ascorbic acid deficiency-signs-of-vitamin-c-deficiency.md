## Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is essential for antioxidant protection, collagen synthesis, immune function, and iron absorption. Deficiency is uncommon in well-nourished populations but can still occur and cause a spectrum of symptoms—from subtle functional declines to the severe disease scurvy. This short guide summarizes the common signs to help with early recognition and informed dietary choices.

## Common early signs

Mild or developing deficiency often begins with non-specific symptoms: persistent fatigue, irritability, and general malaise. As ascorbic acid stores decline, tissue-level effects appear. Look for:

- Easy bruising and small subcutaneous hemorrhages due to fragile capillaries.
- Slow wound healing and recurrent infections, reflecting impaired collagen formation and immune dysfunction.
- Dry, rough skin and brittle hair; in clinical cases hair may take on a corkscrew shape.
- Bleeding or swollen gums and dental issues when oral tissues lose structural support.
- Joint pain or stiffness related to weakened connective tissue and cartilage.

These signs frequently overlap with other conditions, so pattern recognition (multiple related symptoms) is important for suspicion of deficiency.

## Severe deficiency and scurvy

Prolonged, untreated deficiency can progress to scurvy, historically seen in populations lacking fresh produce. Scurvy presents with pronounced gum disease, loose teeth, extensive bruising, anemia, severe joint pain, and impaired wound healing. Fortunately, scurvy is reversible with restored vitamin C intake, but it illustrates the vitamin's role in maintaining connective tissues and vascular integrity.

## Physiological basis

Ascorbic acid acts as a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase enzymes necessary for stable collagen formation and also supports neutrophil function and antioxidant defense. Low vitamin C therefore impairs tissue repair, weakens blood vessel walls, and compromises certain immune responses. It also enhances absorption of non-heme iron; deficiency can indirectly worsen iron-deficiency anemia.

## Who is at risk

Groups at higher risk include smokers, older adults with limited diets, people with gastrointestinal malabsorption (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease), those with eating disorders or food insecurity, and individuals under chronic physical or psychological stress. Consideration of underlying risk factors helps guide assessment and intervention.

## Assessment and management

Clinical suspicion may be supported by dietary review and laboratory testing when appropriate. Increasing intake of vitamin C–rich foods (citrus, berries, peppers, brassicas) is the primary approach. In some cases, targeted supplementation is used to restore normal levels under clinical guidance. ## Conclusion

Recognizing early, often subtle signs of vitamin C deficiency—fatigue, slow wound healing, bruising, skin and gum changes—allows timely dietary or supplemental correction and prevents progression to scurvy. Consider risk factors and professional evaluation when multiple symptoms cluster or when dietary intake is uncertain.
