Magnesium is essential for hundreds of enzymatic reactions, from energy production to muscle and nerve function. Despite its benefits, magnesium supplements are not appropriate for everyone. Certain medical conditions, medications, and sensitivities increase the risk of adverse effects or toxicity. This article summarizes the main groups who should avoid or use magnesium only under close medical supervision.

People to avoid unmonitored magnesium

- Severe kidney disease or renal failure: The kidneys excrete excess magnesium. Impaired renal function can lead to accumulation and hypermagnesemia, which may cause muscle weakness, low blood pressure, slowed breathing, or cardiac conduction problems. Patients on dialysis require individualized mineral management.

- Certain cardiac conditions: Individuals with advanced heart block, symptomatic bradycardia, or specific arrhythmias should not take magnesium supplements without cardiology input. Magnesium alters cardiac electrophysiology and can worsen conduction abnormalities in susceptible people.

- Drug interactions: Magnesium chelates or reduces absorption of some antibiotics (tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones), bisphosphonates, and oral thyroid hormones like levothyroxine. It may also interact with antihypertensives, diuretics, and some antiarrhythmics. Timing and dose adjustments are often necessary to avoid reduced therapeutic effect or increased adverse events.

- Pregnancy and breastfeeding (unsupervised): Although magnesium has clinical uses in obstetrics, supplementation should be supervised to avoid electrolyte shifts, dehydration from diarrhea, or interactions with prenatal medications.

- Known allergy or sensitivity to excipients: True allergy to magnesium ion is rare; most reactions relate to salts, binders, or additives (e.g., magnesium stearate, artificial dyes). Those with multiple allergies or atopic disease should choose hypoallergenic formulations and introduce products slowly.

- Chronic gastrointestinal or absorption disorders: Conditions such as Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, or significant malabsorption may alter magnesium handling, necessitating medical oversight and monitoring of serum levels.

Common adverse effects and overdose signals

Gastrointestinal upset—especially diarrhea, cramping, and nausea—is the most common side effect and is more frequent with poorly absorbed salts like magnesium oxide or sulfate. Persistent diarrhea can cause secondary electrolyte losses (potassium, sodium) and dehydration. Signs of significant magnesium excess include progressive muscle weakness, diminished reflexes, hypotension, respiratory depression, and in severe cases, coma. Any concerning neurological or cardiopulmonary symptoms after supplement use warrant urgent evaluation.

Risk mitigation and practical guidance

- Check kidney function before initiating regular magnesium supplementation and during long-term use in at-risk populations.
- Review all medications for potential interactions; separate magnesium from interacting drugs by several hours when recommended (for example, two hours before or four to six hours after certain antibiotics or bisphosphonates).
- Prefer well-absorbed, gentler forms (e.g., magnesium glycinate) for individuals prone to GI side effects, and start at lower doses while monitoring tolerance.
- Introduce new supplements gradually and stop if allergic-type reactions (hives, swelling, breathing difficulty) occur.
