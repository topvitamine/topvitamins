Introduction

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of biochemical reactions, from energy production and muscle function to nerve signaling and bone health. While dietary magnesium from food is unlikely to cause harm in healthy people, concentrated supplemental forms can produce adverse effects when intake exceeds recommended levels. This article summarizes common signs of excess magnesium, risk factors for toxicity, and practical monitoring considerations based on current guidance.

Recognizing early symptoms

Mild excess magnesium typically first affects the gastrointestinal tract. Symptoms often include nausea, abdominal cramping and watery diarrhea, especially with salt forms such as magnesium oxide or citrate. Neuromuscular effects may follow: muscle weakness, lethargy and diminished reflexes occur as magnesium interferes with neuromuscular transmission. Early recognition is important because many symptoms are reversible with dose adjustment or discontinuation.

Progression to serious toxicity

If magnesium levels continue to rise, cardiovascular and respiratory systems can be affected. Clinically relevant signs include low blood pressure (hypotension), slow heart rate (bradycardia), irregular heart rhythms and, in severe cases, respiratory depression due to impaired function of respiratory muscles. These outcomes are uncommon in people with normal kidney function but represent medical emergencies when they occur.

Who is at higher risk?

People with impaired renal function have markedly increased risk because the kidneys excrete excess magnesium. Older adults, infants, and individuals taking multiple magnesium-containing products (for example antacids or laxatives) are also more vulnerable. Certain chronic conditions—such as gastrointestinal disorders that alter absorption or endocrine disorders that affect electrolyte balance—can complicate regulation of magnesium and raise toxicity risk.

Diagnosis and treatment principles

Hypermagnesemia is diagnosed with serum magnesium measurement; normal reference ranges vary but typically fall around 0.75–0.95 mmol/L. Management begins by stopping magnesium intake. Supportive care includes intravenous calcium gluconate to antagonize cardiac and neuromuscular effects, IV fluids and diuretics to enhance renal elimination, and dialysis in severe or refractory cases—particularly when kidney function is compromised.

Safe supplementation practices

Regulatory authorities generally set the Tolerable Upper Intake Level for supplemental magnesium at 350 mg/day for adults (this excludes dietary magnesium from foods). To minimize risk: review all sources of magnesium (prescription and over-the-counter medications), choose well-tolerated forms and doses, and consult a clinician when you have kidney disease or take interacting medications. Periodic blood testing is advisable in long-term, high-dose use.

Conclusion

Magnesium is vital, but balance matters. Awareness of symptoms, knowledge of risk factors and sensible dosing help prevent adverse effects. When in doubt—especially with renal impairment or polypharmacy—seek medical advice and consider serum monitoring to ensure safety.
