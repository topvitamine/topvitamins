Introduction

Vitamin A is a critical nutrient for eye structure and function, with well‑established roles in night vision, retinal health, epithelial maintenance, and antioxidant defense. Adequate intake supports the biochemical cycles that regenerate light‑sensitive pigments, stabilizes the tear film, and helps protect ocular tissues from oxidative stress. How vitamin A supports vision

At the molecular level, vitamin A is required to form 11‑cis‑retinal, the chromophore component of rhodopsin in rod cells and of visual pigments in cones. This molecule enables photoreceptors to convert light into neural signals; without sufficient vitamin A the visual cycle is impaired, producing symptoms such as delayed dark adaptation and reduced low‑light acuity. Vitamin A also contributes to the maintenance of conjunctival and corneal epithelial cells, preserving lubrication and barrier function against infection.

Deficiency, risk groups and prevention

Vitamin A deficiency can present as night blindness, dry eyes, and in severe cases xerophthalmia and corneal damage. Groups at higher risk include people with malabsorption disorders, restrictive diets, young children in resource‑limited settings, and older adults with reduced nutrient absorption. Prevention strategies range from dietary diversification (liver, dairy, orange vegetables, leafy greens) to targeted supplementation when dietary intake or absorption is insufficient. When considering supplements, consult a healthcare professional: vitamin A is fat‑soluble and excessive intake can cause toxicity.

Retinal protection and antioxidant effects

Beyond the visual cycle, provitamin A carotenoids such as beta‑carotene act as antioxidants that help neutralize free radicals generated by light exposure and metabolic processes. Antioxidant nutrients may slow degenerative processes in the macula and photoreceptors when combined with other protective nutrients. Research indicates that multi‑nutrient approaches—incorporating vitamin A precursors, lutein, zeaxanthin, vitamins C and E, zinc, and omega‑3s—provide broader retinal protection than single nutrients alone.

Clinical considerations and supplementation

Supplementation can correct marginal deficiencies and improve measures of dark adaptation and ocular surface health in affected individuals. Dosage should follow recommended guidelines to avoid hypervitaminosis A; carotenoid forms (e.g., beta‑carotene) are converted as needed and carry a lower risk of toxicity than high doses of preformed retinol. Practical summary

Vitamin A is integral to photopigment regeneration, epithelial integrity, tear production, and antioxidant defense—functions that together support night vision, retinal resilience, and ocular surface health. Balanced diets typically supply sufficient provitamin A for many people, but supplementation can be appropriate for at‑risk populations under medical guidance.
