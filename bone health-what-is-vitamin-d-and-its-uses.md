Vitamin D is a fat-soluble micronutrient that functions more like a hormone than a conventional vitamin. It exists mainly as vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) and D2 (ergocalciferol), and it plays central roles in calcium and phosphorus homeostasis, bone mineralization, immune regulation, and cellular signaling. Endogenous synthesis in the skin following exposure to ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation is the primary natural source, while limited dietary sources and supplements provide alternative intake.

Understanding how vitamin D works is important for appreciating its clinical relevance. In the liver and kidneys, vitamin D is converted into 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) and the hormonally active form, calcitriol, respectively. Active vitamin D increases intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphate, supporting bone formation and neuromuscular function. Immune cells also express vitamin D receptors and enzymes that locally activate vitamin D, which influences innate antimicrobial defenses and modulates inflammatory responses.

Population groups at increased risk of low vitamin D status include older adults (reduced cutaneous synthesis), people with darker skin (higher melanin reduces UVB penetration), those who have limited sun exposure, and individuals with fat malabsorption syndromes. Common clinical manifestations of deficiency can be nonspecific—fatigue, muscle weakness, bone pain—or more definitive in children (rickets) and adults (osteomalacia). Serum 25(OH)D concentration is the accepted biomarker for assessing status.

Sensible sun exposure can generate sufficient vitamin D for many people, but geographic latitude, season, time of day, sunscreen use, clothing, and air pollution all affect cutaneous synthesis. When endogenous production and dietary sources are insufficient, supplements can be used to maintain target serum concentrations under clinical guidance. Bone health is the most established outcome related to vitamin D. By facilitating calcium absorption and supporting bone remodeling, adequate vitamin D status helps maintain bone mineral density and reduce the risk of fractures—particularly among older adults. Clinical guidelines commonly recommend combined attention to dietary calcium and vitamin D for skeletal protection.

Emerging evidence supports roles for vitamin D in immune competence. Observational studies associate low 25(OH)D with higher incidence of respiratory infections, and some randomized trials show modest protective effects of supplementation in deficient populations. Vitamin D’s immunomodulatory properties include induction of antimicrobial peptides and downregulation of excessive inflammatory cytokines, which may contribute to more balanced immune responses.

Because vitamin D influences mineral metabolism, coordination with other nutrients matters: magnesium is required for enzymatic activation of vitamin D metabolites, and vitamin K2 has been discussed as a cofactor in directing calcium to bone rather than soft tissues. Clinical decisions about supplementation should consider baseline serum levels, concurrent medications, and potential risks of hypercalcemia when using high doses.
