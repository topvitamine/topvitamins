# What does vitamin E do?

Vitamin E is a fat-soluble vitamin made up of tocopherols and tocotrienols that functions primarily as an antioxidant and a regulator of cell function. Its most active form, alpha-tocopherol, is widely studied for roles that range from protecting cell membranes to supporting immune competence. Understanding these mechanisms helps clarify why vitamin E is commonly included both in diets and in supplements.

## Antioxidant protection and cellular stability

One of vitamin E’s central roles is neutralizing free radicals—reactive molecules that cause oxidative stress and damage lipids, proteins, and DNA. By interrupting lipid peroxidation in cell membranes, vitamin E helps preserve membrane integrity and prevent cascade effects that amplify cellular injury. These antioxidant actions are relevant across many tissues and may help lower the long-term risk of conditions associated with cumulative oxidative damage, although vitamin E is not a standalone treatment for chronic disease.

## Skin health and aging

Topical and dietary vitamin E contribute to skin barrier function by integrating with skin lipids and reducing trans-epidermal water loss. Its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects can support wound healing, reduce irritation, and mitigate photo-induced damage that accelerates visible aging. When used alongside other antioxidants (for example vitamin C) and good photoprotection, vitamin E may help maintain skin texture and resilience over time.

## Cardiovascular considerations

Vitamin E can protect low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol from oxidative modification, a step implicated in atherogenesis. By limiting LDL oxidation and supporting endothelial cell function, vitamin E may contribute to vascular health. Clinical trial results have been mixed, however, and benefits appear most consistent when vitamin E is part of an overall nutrient-rich dietary pattern rather than taken in isolation.

## Immune function

Vitamin E influences immune cell signaling and proliferation, notably affecting T-cell responses important for pathogen defense. Supplementation has shown measurable immune benefits in certain populations, such as older adults, where immunosenescence reduces responsiveness. Vitamin E’s anti-inflammatory effects also help modulate chronic inflammatory states that can impair immune efficiency.

## Practical intake and sources

Dietary sources include nuts, seeds, and vegetable oils, which supply various tocopherols and tocotrienols. When dietary intake is insufficient or when absorption is impaired, supplements can help meet recommended intake levels. ## Summary

Vitamin E supports cellular defense through antioxidant activity, contributes to skin health and repair, and plays modulatory roles in cardiovascular and immune systems. Its most meaningful benefits occur within a balanced dietary and lifestyle context, often alongside complementary nutrients.
