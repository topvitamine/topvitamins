Introduction

Vitamin D is essential for bone health, immune function, and overall wellbeing. While human skin produces vitamin D3 when exposed to UVB sunlight, dietary sources become important when sun exposure is limited. Animal foods (fatty fish, egg yolks) are typically richest in vitamin D, but this poses a challenge for vegetarians and vegans. This article summarizes the evidence on plant-based sources and practical ways to maintain adequate vitamin D status.

Vegetable sources: the role of mushrooms

Most green vegetables—kale, spinach, broccoli—contain negligible vitamin D naturally. The notable exception is fungi: mushrooms can synthesize ergosterol, which converts to vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) when exposed to ultraviolet light. UV-exposed varieties such as maitake, portobello, shiitake, and treated white button mushrooms can provide substantial amounts of vitamin D2 per 100 g when properly processed. Vitamin D2 vs D3: efficacy and implications

Nutrition science distinguishes vitamin D2 (plant/fungal) from D3 (animal-derived). D3 (cholecalciferol) is generally more effective at raising and maintaining serum 25(OH)D concentrations, while D2 can still increase levels and is a valid option for plant-based diets. Clinical studies show that UV-treated mushrooms can raise circulating 25(OH)D, though D2 often has a shorter half-life. Fortified foods and dietary planning

Because natural plant sources are limited, food fortification is an important public-health tool. Fortified plant milks, fortified cereals, tofu, and some spreads often contain added vitamin D (D2 or D3). Some pre-packaged salad mixes or vegetable products are fortified in limited markets, but this is not widespread. Always read product labels for the form and amount of vitamin D provided. Practical recommendations

- Include UV-exposed mushrooms regularly (grilled or baked to preserve vitamin D content) and pair meals with healthy fats to support absorption.  
- Use fortified plant milks and cereals as reliable, consistent sources.  
- Monitor serum 25(OH)D if risk factors are present (limited sun, darker skin, older age), and discuss supplementation with a clinician.

Conclusion

Natural vegetable-derived vitamin D is essentially limited to UV-exposed mushrooms, while fortified foods fill many gaps in plant-based diets. D2 from mushrooms is effective but behaves differently from D3, so vegans and vegetarians should plan meals deliberately and consider testing or supplementation when needed.
