Vitamin D, often called the “sunshine vitamin,” is essential for bone health, immune function, muscle performance, and aspects of mental well-being. Deficiency is common worldwide and can develop gradually, producing symptoms that are easy to overlook. For an accessible overview, see [symptoms of too little vitamin D](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/symptoms-of-too-little-vitamin-d).

Common early complaints include persistent fatigue, diffuse muscle weakness, and bone discomfort. Fatigue from low vitamin D often feels chronic and less responsive to rest. Muscle weakness or aching, particularly in proximal muscles (thighs and shoulders), can reduce mobility and make routine activities harder. Bone pain may be vague—described as aching in the hips, lower back, ribs, or long bones—and in children can contribute to delayed growth or rickets.

Immune effects are also notable. Vitamin D modulates innate and adaptive immune responses, and low levels are associated with increased susceptibility to respiratory infections and longer recovery times. Mood alteration, including symptoms consistent with depression and reduced mental clarity or “brain fog,” has been observed in observational studies of people with low vitamin D, although causality is not fully established.

Less commonly recognized signs include hair thinning or hair loss, slow wound healing, and increased frequency of falls in older adults due to reduced muscle strength and impaired balance. Lab testing frequently reveals low serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentrations before severe symptoms appear, so mild or nonspecific complaints may prompt testing in at-risk individuals.

Several factors contribute to deficiency: limited sun exposure (including northern latitudes, heavy pollution, or extensive sunscreen use), darker skin pigmentation, aging skin, dietary insufficiency, malabsorption syndromes (for example, celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease), obesity (vitamin D sequestration in adipose tissue), and certain medications that alter vitamin D metabolism. Addressing cause helps guide treatment, and clinicians often recommend testing for people with persistent symptoms or risk factors.

Diagnosis relies on the serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D test. Most guidelines categorize levels below about 20 ng/mL as deficient, 21–29 ng/mL as insufficient, and 30 ng/mL or higher as adequate for many people, though target ranges can vary by clinical context. If deficiency is confirmed, clinicians tailor recommendations based on severity, comorbid conditions, and individual risk factors, with follow-up testing after initiation of any intervention.

Practical measures to improve vitamin D status include sensible sun exposure, inclusion of dietary sources (fatty fish, fortified foods, and egg yolks), and targeted supplementation when needed.
