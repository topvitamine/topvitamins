## Introduction

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of enzymatic reactions, supporting muscle and nerve function, cardiovascular health, and bone integrity. Many people can raise magnesium intake effectively through whole foods, and vegetables are a convenient, low-calorie source of this nutrient. This article summarizes evidence-based information about the vegetables highest in magnesium, factors that affect absorption, and practical ways to increase dietary magnesium.

## Highest-magnesium vegetables

Leafy greens lead the list of magnesium-rich vegetables. Cooked spinach is one of the most concentrated sources, with approximately 157 mg of magnesium per cooked cup. Swiss chard and kale also provide substantial amounts, while collard greens contribute meaningful levels depending on preparation. Beyond leafy greens, vegetables such as artichokes (about 77 mg per medium boiled artichoke), green peas (roughly 62 mg per cooked cup), and broccoli (around 33 mg per cooked cup) are useful contributors to daily intake.

## Bioavailability and preparation

Not all consumed magnesium is equally absorbable. Plant foods can contain compounds such as oxalates and phytates that bind minerals and reduce bioavailability. Leafy greens high in oxalates (for example, raw spinach) may have reduced magnesium absorption compared with cooked forms. Gentle cooking methods such as steaming or light sautéing can reduce oxalate levels and improve mineral availability while preserving other nutrients.

Soil quality and growing conditions also influence magnesium content in vegetables: produce grown in magnesium-rich soils tends to contain higher levels, and post-harvest storage and prolonged boiling can leach minerals into cooking water.

## Practical ways to increase intake

- Emphasize a variety of leafy greens: rotate spinach, Swiss chard, kale, and collards across meals.
- Combine vegetables with seeds and legumes—pumpkin seeds, for example, are among the richest plant sources of magnesium and pair well with vegetable dishes.
- Prefer steaming or quick stir-frying to long boiling, and use cooking water (when appropriate) in soups to retain dissolved minerals.
- Include magnesium-containing vegetables across the day rather than concentrating intake at one meal to support steady absorption.

## Health relevance

Dietary magnesium supports bone health by interacting with calcium and vitamin D, aids neuromuscular function, and contributes to cardiovascular regulation through effects on vascular tone. These benefits are typically observed in the context of overall dietary patterns rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and adequate hydration.

## Practical summary

Vegetables—especially cooked leafy greens like spinach and Swiss chard—are among the best dietary sources of magnesium in a plant-based diet. Attention to preparation, variety, and pairing with other magnesium-containing foods (such as seeds and legumes) enhances intake and bioavailability. When dietary constraints or clinical conditions limit magnesium status, healthcare professionals can advise on whether additional strategies are needed.
