## Introduction

Vitamin D is essential for bone health, immune regulation, muscle function, and mood. Because the body synthesizes vitamin D in response to ultraviolet B (UVB) sunlight, modern lifestyles and geographic factors can make deficiency common. Recognizing the signs and understanding how laboratories interpret results helps guide appropriate corrective steps.

## Common signs and symptoms

Symptoms of low vitamin D are often subtle and overlap with other conditions. Frequent indicators include persistent fatigue, diffuse muscle weakness or aches (especially proximal muscles), and bone pain that is difficult to attribute to a specific injury. Mood changes such as depressive symptoms and cognitive fog have been associated with low serum vitamin D in observational studies. Increased susceptibility to respiratory infections and slower wound healing are other reported associations.

Physical findings may include hair thinning, delayed wound closure, recurrent dental issues, and a higher incidence of fractures or signs of low bone density over time. In children, severe deficiency can present as rickets, characterized by bone deformities and delayed growth.

## Who is at increased risk?

Risk factors that reduce cutaneous vitamin D synthesis or impair absorption include darker skin pigmentation, older age (reduced cutaneous production), limited sun exposure (indoor occupations, high latitudes, or cultural clothing practices), obesity (sequestration in adipose tissue), and disorders that affect fat absorption such as celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease. Chronic kidney or liver disease can also impair conversion to active forms.

## Testing and interpretation

Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) is the accepted marker for assessing vitamin D status. Common interpretive thresholds are: deficiency <20 ng/mL (<50 nmol/L), insufficiency 20–29 ng/mL, and adequate ≥30 ng/mL, with some experts suggesting an optimal range around 40–60 ng/mL for certain populations. Related laboratory values—serum calcium, phosphorus, and parathyroid hormone (PTH)—can provide context: elevated PTH with low 25(OH)D suggests secondary hyperparathyroidism due to inadequate vitamin D.

If you suspect deficiency, clinical evaluation and a 25(OH)D test ordered by a healthcare professional are appropriate first steps. ## Addressing low levels

Correction strategies depend on cause and severity. For many, moderate, supervised supplementation and safe sun exposure raise levels effectively; vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is more effective than D2 in increasing serum 25(OH)D. Dietary sources are limited and may be insufficient alone. Combining vitamin D with dietary fat, and ensuring adequate intake of cofactors such as magnesium and vitamin K, supports its physiological actions.

## Conclusion

Vitamin D deficiency can present with nonspecific symptoms and has systemic implications beyond bone health. Risk assessment, targeted testing, and individualized correction under professional guidance help restore adequate levels and reduce longer-term risks.
