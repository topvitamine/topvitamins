# Understanding Omega-3: Essential Fatty Acids and Their Roles

Omega-3 fatty acids are essential polyunsaturated fats that support multiple physiological systems. The primary forms relevant to human health are ALA (alpha-linolenic acid), EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid). Because humans cannot synthesise these fats in sufficient quantities, dietary sources or supplements are required — see detailed coverage at [What does omega-3 do for the body?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-does-omega-3-do-for-the-body).

Diets high in omega-6 and low in omega-3 are common in many countries and may contribute to a pro-inflammatory state. ALA is abundant in plant foods such as flaxseed and chia, but conversion to EPA and DHA is limited. Marine sources (fatty fish, krill) and algae provide preformed EPA and DHA, which are more directly incorporated into tissues.

## Heart health and triglyceride reduction

EPA and DHA exert several cardioprotective effects. They can reduce triglyceride synthesis in the liver and enhance triglyceride clearance via increased lipoprotein lipase activity. Clinical trials indicate that higher-dose EPA/DHA (commonly 2–4 g/day) can lower triglycerides substantially in people with elevated levels. Additional mechanisms include modest blood pressure reduction, improved arterial elasticity, and decreased platelet aggregation — all factors that influence cardiovascular risk.

## Anti-inflammatory benefits

EPA and DHA modify the production of eicosanoids and cytokines, shifting the balance toward less inflammatory mediators. They are precursors to resolvins and protectins, compounds involved in resolving inflammation. This biochemical activity underlies observed benefits in conditions with chronic inflammation, such as rheumatoid arthritis, and may reduce reliance on some symptomatic medications when used as an adjunct.

## Brain function and mental health support

DHA is a major structural component of neuronal membranes and the retina. Adequate DHA supports membrane fluidity, neurotransmission, and synaptic function. Evidence links omega-3 status to cognitive development in infancy and to mood regulation in adults; EPA in particular has been studied for depressive symptoms when used alongside standard care. Longitudinal research is ongoing regarding omega-3s and age-related cognitive decline.

## Immune modulation

Omega-3s are incorporated into immune cell membranes and influence the behaviour of macrophages, neutrophils and lymphocytes. By promoting resolution of inflammation and reducing excessive immune activation, EPA and DHA can support balanced immune responses. In older adults, omega-3 intake has been associated with improved vaccine responses and markers of immune function in some studies.

## Practical considerations

For most adults, a baseline intake of EPA+DHA around 250 mg/day supports heart function, while higher therapeutic doses are used under medical supervision for triglyceride reduction or significant inflammatory conditions. Supplements vary by source and concentration; algae-based products provide a vegan alternative to fish oil.

## Summary

EPA and DHA are biologically active fatty acids that support cardiovascular health, reduce chronic inflammation, contribute to brain structure and function, help manage triglyceride levels, and modulate immune responses. Individual dosing and clinical use should be guided by health status and professional advice.
