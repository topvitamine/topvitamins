Magnesium is a key micronutrient whose importance increases with age. It participates in hundreds of enzymatic reactions that influence cardiovascular function, bone health, neuromuscular activity, energy metabolism, and cognitive processes. Age-related physiological changes — reduced intestinal absorption, increased renal excretion, and polypharmacy — can all contribute to lower magnesium status in older adults, making monitoring and tailored nutrition increasingly relevant.

Older adults commonly consume less magnesium-rich food (leafy greens, nuts, seeds, whole grains) and may experience impaired uptake due to hypochlorhydria or gastrointestinal conditions. Several widely used medications, including diuretics and proton pump inhibitors, are associated with greater magnesium loss and can produce chronic, subclinical deficiency. Clinically, magnesium insufficiency is often subtle: symptoms such as cramps, fatigue, palpitations, irritability, or cognitive changes can overlap with other age-related disorders and therefore be overlooked.

Maintaining adequate magnesium supports multiple systems that typically decline with age. In the cardiovascular system, magnesium helps regulate vascular tone and myocardial excitability; low levels are associated with hypertension and a greater risk of arrhythmias. For skeletal health, magnesium is integral to bone mineralization and modulates the actions of vitamin D and calcium; many studies link higher magnesium intake with better bone mineral density and reduced fracture risk. Neurologically, magnesium contributes to neurotransmitter balance and synaptic function, and emerging research suggests benefits for sleep quality and mood regulation.

Given absorption challenges, some older adults may benefit from supplements, selecting forms with higher bioavailability and better tolerability. Commonly used forms include magnesium citrate (good absorption, may affect bowel motility), magnesium glycinate (gentle on digestion, often chosen for sleep and anxiety support), and magnesium malate (often used for energy metabolism). Typical supplemental ranges often discussed in clinical guidance are approximately 200–400 mg elemental magnesium per day, with adjustments for individual tolerance, renal function, and concomitant medications. Co-nutrients such as vitamin D and vitamin K play synergistic roles in bone and calcium metabolism and can support magnesium utilization.

Optimizing magnesium status requires an individualized approach: dietary assessment, review of medications, basic laboratory testing when indicated, and coordination with a clinician or dietitian. Simple lifestyle measures can also help: consuming magnesium-rich foods, maintaining hydration, supporting gut health (including consideration of probiotics where appropriate), and spacing medications that interfere with absorption may improve outcomes.
