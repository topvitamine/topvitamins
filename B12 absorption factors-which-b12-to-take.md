## Introduction

Vitamin B12 is essential for neurological function, red blood cell production, DNA synthesis and energy metabolism. Because the body cannot synthesize B12, dietary intake or supplementation is required. Deficiency is common among older adults, people with gastrointestinal disorders, those on certain medications, and those following strict plant-based diets. Left untreated, deficiency can cause fatigue, cognitive changes, mood disturbance and peripheral neuropathy.

## Forms of B12 and how they differ

Supplements typically contain one of four cobalamin forms: methylcobalamin, cyanocobalamin, hydroxocobalamin and adenosylcobalamin. These differ in bioavailability, metabolic pathway and clinical use:

- Methylcobalamin: a bioactive coenzyme form used directly in methylation reactions. It supports nervous-system function and homocysteine metabolism.
- Cyanocobalamin: a stable synthetic form commonly used in fortified foods and many supplements; it requires conversion in the body to active forms.
- Hydroxocobalamin: often administered by injection with good tissue retention; commonly used in clinical repletion.
- Adenosylcobalamin: a mitochondrial form involved in energy metabolism, less common in supplements.

Choice of form should reflect individual factors such as digestive health, genetic variants affecting methylation (for example MTHFR), cost considerations and dietary preferences.

## Absorption factors and delivery methods

Absorption of B12 is complex: dietary B12 must be released from protein in food (a process aided by stomach acid) and then bound to intrinsic factor for receptor-mediated uptake in the ileum. Conditions that reduce stomach acid, remove intrinsic factor, or affect the terminal ileum (e.g., atrophic gastritis, pernicious anemia, celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, bariatric surgery) can impair absorption.

Delivery methods include oral tablets, sublingual preparations, nasal sprays and intramuscular injections. Sublingual and liquid forms can bypass some digestive limitations, and injections are used when rapid repletion or reliable absorption is necessary.

## Dosage guidance

Recommended intakes for most adults are small (around 2.4 mcg/day), but supplements are commonly provided in much higher doses because passive absorption becomes relevant at high oral intakes. Maintenance regimens often range from 250–1,000 mcg daily or several times weekly; repletion strategies may use higher daily or weekly doses or intramuscular therapy for severe deficiency. Diagnostic testing (serum B12, methylmalonic acid, homocysteine) should guide therapeutic decisions.

## Practical considerations for vegans and those with malabsorption

Vegans should rely on fortified foods and supplements because plant foods generally do not provide active B12. Many vegan supplements use methylcobalamin or cyanocobalamin produced by bacterial fermentation. For people with malabsorption, parenteral therapy or high-dose oral/sublingual regimens may be required.

## Summary

Methylcobalamin is often preferred for neural support and for people with methylation concerns, while cyanocobalamin remains a stable, cost-effective option for many. Individual health status, absorption capability and diagnostic testing should determine the optimal form and regimen.
