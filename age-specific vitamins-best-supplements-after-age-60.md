## Introduction

As people move past age 60, nutrient needs and absorption change in ways that can affect bone strength, muscle mass, cognition, immune resilience, and energy. A nutrient-dense diet remains primary, but targeted supplementation — when guided by clinical assessment and used to fill verified gaps — can help maintain function and independence. This article summarizes evidence-aligned nutrients, practical selection tips, and safety considerations for older adults.

## Key nutrients for people over 60

- Vitamin D: Supports calcium absorption, bone and muscle function. Many older adults have suboptimal vitamin D due to less sun exposure and skin changes.
- Calcium and vitamin K: Work together to maintain bone integrity. Total daily calcium should include dietary sources plus supplements when needed.
- Vitamin B12: Important for nervous system function and red blood cell formation; absorption declines with age and some medications.
- Protein and amino acids: Help preserve muscle mass and functional capacity; distribute protein across meals.
- Omega-3 (EPA/DHA): Contribute to normal cardiac and brain function; choose products that list EPA/DHA amounts and show purity testing.
- Magnesium and vitamin C: Magnesium supports muscle and nervous system function and reduces tiredness and fatigue; vitamin C contributes to normal immune function.

## Practical selection and timing

Start with a dietary and medication review and consider blood tests (vitamin D, B12, basic metabolic profile) to identify deficits. Choose reputable brands with transparent labels and third-party testing. Formulation notes:

- Prefer vitamin D3 for improving serum 25(OH)D. Take with a meal containing fat.
- For B12, oral or sublingual cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin can be used if absorption is a concern.
- Calcium citrate is often better tolerated than calcium carbonate for those with lower stomach acid; split doses improve absorption.
- Take fat-soluble vitamins with food; magnesium is commonly taken in the evening if it aids sleep.

Avoid megadoses unless clinically indicated. Review supplements annually or with any medication change.

## Bone and cognitive considerations

Bone health after 60 benefits from combined strategies: adequate calcium and vitamin D, magnesium and vitamin K where appropriate, and regular weight-bearing/resistance exercise. Coordinate vitamin K supplements with clinicians if anticoagulants are used. For cognitive support, ensure adequate DHA and B-vitamins; lifestyle elements such as exercise, sleep, social engagement, and cognitive stimulation are essential complements.

## Conclusion

Supplements after 60 can be useful to correct specific deficiencies and support bone, muscle, cognitive, and immune health. Prioritize a food-first approach, use testing to guide targeted supplementation, choose transparent products, and consult a healthcare professional to avoid interactions and inappropriate dosing. When combined with exercise, sleep, and social engagement, a measured supplement strategy supports healthy aging.
