Introduction

Vitamins are essential micronutrients that support a wide range of physiological processes, from energy metabolism and immune function to bone health and cognitive maintenance. This concise guide summarizes key vitamins, their primary roles, common deficiency signs, and evidence-based considerations for supplementation. For a fuller treatment of these topics, see [What are different vitamins good for?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-are-different-vitamins-good-for).

Core vitamin categories

- Water-soluble vitamins: The B-complex (B1, B2, B3, B5, B6, B7, B9, B12) and Vitamin C dissolve in water and are not stored extensively, requiring regular intake.
- Fat-soluble vitamins: Vitamins A, D, E, and K are absorbed with dietary fat and stored in tissues; excess intake can accumulate and cause adverse effects.

Key functions and clinical relevance

- Vitamin A: Supports vision (especially low-light adaptation), epithelial integrity, and immune responses. Deficiency can present as night blindness and increased infection risk.
- B-complex vitamins: Central to energy production, red blood cell formation, and neurological function. B12 and folate are particularly important for DNA synthesis and neurological health; deficiency may cause anemia and cognitive symptoms.
- Vitamin C: An antioxidant involved in collagen synthesis and immune cell function. Severe deficiency leads to scurvy (fatigue, bleeding gums, impaired wound healing).
- Vitamin D: Regulates calcium and phosphate homeostasis, crucial for bone mineralization and immune modulation. Low levels are associated with rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults.
- Vitamin E: A lipid-soluble antioxidant that protects cell membranes from oxidative damage and supports skin and eye health.
- Vitamin K: Essential for coagulation and bone matrix protein modification; interacts with Vitamin D in calcium metabolism.

Who is at risk of deficiency

Populations with elevated risk include individuals on restrictive diets (e.g., vegans for B12), older adults with reduced absorption, people with malabsorptive gastrointestinal conditions, and pregnant or lactating women with increased nutrient demands. Regular clinical assessment and targeted laboratory testing can identify deficiencies that warrant correction.

Supplementation considerations

Evidence supports targeted supplementation when dietary intake is insufficient or clinical need is identified. Nutrient interactions matter: Vitamin C enhances non-heme iron absorption; Vitamins D and K act synergistically in bone and vascular health. Conversely, excessive intake of fat-soluble vitamins may cause toxicity. Multivitamins can be useful for some, but individualized strategies based on biomarkers, diet, and health status are preferable. Broader micronutrient context

Vitamins act alongside minerals and trace elements (e.g., magnesium, zinc, iron) to support enzyme systems, electrolyte balance, and tissue repair. Integrated approaches that consider both vitamins and minerals tend to yield the most robust nutritional outcomes.

Conclusion

Vitamins are indispensable for health, but optimal benefit depends on appropriate intake, monitoring, and recognition of interactions and risks. Clinical assessment and evidence-based supplementation help correct deficiencies and support long-term wellbeing without replacing a diverse, nutrient-dense diet.
