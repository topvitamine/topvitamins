Understanding the evidence around omega-3 fatty acids and arterial health requires separating marketing shorthand from physiological realities. Omega-3s—primarily EPA and DHA from marine sources—are well studied for effects on triglycerides, inflammation, and endothelial function, but they do not mechanically 'clean' arteries in the way a surgical intervention would.

Omega-3s influence several pathways relevant to atherosclerosis. They lower circulating triglycerides in dose-dependent fashion, modulate inflammatory signalling by serving as precursors to resolvins and protectins, and can improve endothelial responsiveness through effects on nitric oxide and oxidative stress. Clinical imaging studies and randomized trials have reported that high-dose EPA can reduce or stabilize coronary plaque volume and reduce markers of plaque inflammation, suggesting an effect on plaque composition and vulnerability rather than direct removal of calcified or fibrous material.

A careful reading of the literature shows heterogeneity: benefits are most consistent for triglyceride reduction and anti-inflammatory effects, and in certain high-risk populations for reduction of major cardiovascular events. For example, the REDUCE-IT trial demonstrated substantial event reduction with high-dose EPA in patients with elevated triglycerides on statin therapy, while other trials using mixed EPA/DHA formulations have shown more modest or mixed outcomes. This implies that EPA-specific effects and therapeutic dosing matter when interpreting results.

Practically, omega-3 supplementation is best viewed as an adjunctive, evidence-based strategy that can improve components of cardiovascular risk. It is not a replacement for proven interventions such as lipid-lowering therapies, blood pressure control, smoking cessation, and lifestyle modification. When integrated into a comprehensive approach, omega-3s can support arterial health by reducing inflammation, lowering triglycerides, and promoting plaque stability.

Quality and dose are important determinants of clinical effect. Trials showing the largest benefits typically used pharmaceutical-grade or highly concentrated EPA at therapeutic doses. For general cardiovascular support, lower daily intakes (often 1 g combined EPA/DHA) are common; for triglyceride lowering or specific clinical indications, higher supervised doses are used. Consumers and clinicians should prioritize products that disclose EPA/DHA content and have undergone third-party testing for purity.
