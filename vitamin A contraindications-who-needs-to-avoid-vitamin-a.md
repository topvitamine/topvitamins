Introduction

Vitamin A is essential for vision, immune function, reproduction and cellular communication. It exists as preformed vitamin A (retinol and retinyl esters) in animal foods and many supplements, and as provitamin A carotenoids (like beta‑carotene) in plant foods. While deficiency can cause night blindness and increased infection risk, excessive intake—particularly of preformed vitamin A—carries real hazards. Understanding who should avoid supplements and why helps prevent toxicity while preserving benefit.

Who should be cautious

Certain populations should avoid vitamin A supplements or use them only under medical supervision. People with chronic liver disease should not take preformed vitamin A because the liver stores and metabolizes this nutrient; impaired hepatic function increases the risk of accumulation and worsening liver injury. Individuals with a history of hypervitaminosis A should similarly avoid re‑supplementation, as their tolerance to additional vitamin A may be reduced.

Pregnant women are a key at‑risk group. High intakes of preformed vitamin A in early pregnancy are teratogenic and associated with congenital malformations. Medical guidance typically limits preformed vitamin A in pregnancy; dietary carotenoids from vegetables are generally safer because the body controls conversion to active retinol.

Children require age‑appropriate dosing; their smaller body mass makes them more vulnerable to overdose. Patients taking retinoid medications for acne or psoriasis (such as isotretinoin or acitretin) should avoid additional vitamin A sources because of additive effects that can precipitate toxicity. People on anticoagulants or certain anticonvulsants should also consult a clinician, as interactions and altered metabolism can change risk profiles.

Risks and signs of toxicity

Hypervitaminosis A can be acute (very large single doses) or chronic (repeated intake above the tolerable upper intake). Early signs resemble flu—nausea, headache, dizziness, vomiting and blurred vision. Chronic exposure may cause dry skin, hair loss, bone pain, increased intracranial pressure and liver abnormalities. In severe cases, prolonged toxicity may lead to liver fibrosis or neurological injury.

Prevention and monitoring

Preventive strategies focus on dosage awareness and source control. Preformed vitamin A appears under names such as retinyl palmitate or retinyl acetate on labels; fish liver oils and organ meats are concentrated dietary sources. Monitoring total daily intake from supplements, fortified foods and high‑vitamin foods reduces inadvertent excess. For people at risk, periodic blood tests (serum retinol, liver enzymes) and clinical follow‑up are prudent.

When supplementation is appropriate

Supplementation is necessary and beneficial in confirmed deficiency, particularly in populations with poor access to vitamin A–rich foods or in certain clinical malabsorption states. Those with fat‑absorption disorders, cystic fibrosis or post‑bariatric surgery may need tailored replacement under medical supervision to balance deficiency correction and toxicity avoidance.
