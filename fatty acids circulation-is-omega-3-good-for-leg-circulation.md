Poor circulation in the legs is common with aging, sedentary behavior, and vascular disease. Omega-3 fatty acids — particularly EPA and DHA from marine sources — have been studied for their effects on vascular function, and several mechanisms support a plausible benefit for lower-limb blood flow.

Omega-3s influence circulation through multiple, interrelated pathways. They reduce vascular inflammation, modulate blood lipid profiles (notably lowering triglycerides), and decrease platelet aggregation, which can reduce clot risk and improve microvascular perfusion. EPA and DHA also promote endothelial health by supporting nitric oxide availability and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity, facilitating vasodilation and lowering arterial stiffness. Improvements in red blood cell deformability have been reported as well, which helps erythrocytes traverse narrow capillaries in muscle and skin of the legs.

Clinical and mechanistic studies indicate that regular intake of omega-3s can modestly improve markers tied to circulation. For general cardiovascular maintenance, many guidelines cite combined EPA+DHA intakes in the range of about 250–500 mg per day; higher therapeutic doses are sometimes used under clinical supervision for specific lipid or inflammatory conditions. For peripheral circulation specifically, evidence supports benefits on endothelial function, arterial compliance, and reduced blood viscosity — all favorable for supplying oxygen and nutrients to lower-extremity tissues.

Dietary sources include fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), while ALA from flaxseed, chia, and walnuts provides a limited precursor that converts poorly to EPA/DHA. Supplements (fish oil, krill oil, or algae-derived omega-3 for vegetarians) offer a reliable way to reach target intakes when dietary intake is inadequate. When considering omega-3 for leg circulation, keep these practical points in mind:

- Mechanisms: anti-inflammatory effects, improved endothelial function, reduced platelet stickiness, and enhanced red blood cell flexibility.
- Potential outcomes: reduced arterial stiffness, improved microcirculatory flow, and support for tissue repair and reduced cramping in some individuals.
- Dosage and safety: modest daily intakes are generally well tolerated; bleeding risk and interactions with anticoagulant medications warrant consultation with a healthcare provider.

Nutrition is only one part of an integrated strategy to support leg circulation. Physical activity, weight management, smoking cessation, and control of blood pressure and blood sugar all influence peripheral blood flow.
