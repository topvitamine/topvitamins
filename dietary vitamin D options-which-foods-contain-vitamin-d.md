# Which foods contain vitamin D?

Vitamin D is essential for bone health, muscle function, and immune regulation. While sunlight drives endogenous synthesis, dietary sources matter when sun exposure is limited or seasonal. This article summarizes the most meaningful food-based contributors to vitamin D intake and highlights practical considerations for different dietary patterns.

## Major natural sources

Fatty fish are the top whole-food providers of vitamin D. Species such as salmon, mackerel, sardines, and tuna supply substantial amounts per serving and also provide omega-3 fatty acids. For example, a 100 g portion of cooked salmon may contain several hundred international units (IU), depending on wild or farmed origin. Fish liver oils—most notably cod liver oil—are very concentrated sources and have traditionally been used where high single doses are needed.

Egg yolks contain modest amounts of vitamin D; levels increase when hens have outdoor access or vitamin D–enriched feed. Mushrooms exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light can produce vitamin D2 and, when specially treated, may provide hundreds of IU per 100 g—making them important plant-based options for vegetarians and vegans.

## Fortified foods and plant-based alternatives

Because few plant foods contain vitamin D naturally, fortification is widely used to improve population intake. Milk (dairy and many plant milks), breakfast cereals, orange juice, and some meat substitutes are commonly fortified with vitamin D2 or D3. Fortified plant milks typically supply about 100–140 IU per cup, offering practical non-dairy alternatives. When choosing fortified items, check the label to confirm the form of vitamin D (D2 vs D3) and the level per serving.

## Bioavailability and absorption

Vitamin D is fat-soluble, so consuming sources with dietary fat improves absorption. This is one reason whole-fat dairy and oily fish are particularly effective. Between forms, vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is generally more bioavailable and longer acting in the bloodstream than D2 (ergocalciferol), though both can increase serum 25(OH)D when consumed consistently. ## Practical food list (approximate values)

- Cod liver oil: very high concentration per teaspoon
- Cooked salmon: several hundred IU per 100 g
- Canned sardines/mackerel: substantial per serving
- UV-exposed mushrooms: variable (200–1000 IU per 100 g when treated)
- Fortified milk and plant milks: ~100–140 IU per cup
- Egg yolks: ~35–40 IU per egg
- Fortified breakfast cereals: check labels (commonly 80–150 IU per serving)

## When diet may be insufficient

Individuals with limited sun exposure, those living at higher latitudes in winter, older adults, people with darker skin, and those with malabsorption disorders may not meet requirements through diet and sun alone. Routine assessment of serum 25(OH)D can guide individualized management. ## Summary

Dietary vitamin D sources include fatty fish, fish oils, egg yolks, UV-exposed mushrooms, and fortified foods. Combining food choices with appropriate assessment and, where necessary, supplementation helps maintain adequate status.
