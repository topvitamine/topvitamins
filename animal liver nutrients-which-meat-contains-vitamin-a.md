## Meat Containing Vitamin A: What to Know

Vitamin A is essential for vision, immune function, reproduction, and cellular communication. Animal-derived vitamin A appears primarily as retinol, the bioactive form the body can use immediately, which makes certain meats—especially liver—among the richest dietary sources. For a concise overview on this topic see [Which meat contains vitamin A?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/which-meat-contains-vitamin-a).

### Liver: The Most Concentrated Source

Liver from beef, chicken, pork, and lamb contains very high concentrations of preformed vitamin A (retinol). Typical values (per 100 g) vary by species and preparation, but common estimates include:

- Beef liver: roughly 9,000–25,000 IU (3,000–8,300 µg RAE)
- Chicken liver: ~13,000–16,000 IU
- Pork liver: ~15,000 IU
- Lamb liver: 7,000–12,000 IU
- Cod liver oil (supplement form): can reach very high levels per tablespoon

Because of this potency, liver is nutritionally dense but should be consumed in moderation to avoid excessive retinol intake.

### Other Meats and Organ Cuts

Muscle meats contain much lower retinol concentrations but still contribute to overall intake. Examples (approximate, per 100 g cooked): beef (chuck roast) 20–40 IU, pork loin ~70 IU, chicken breast ~13 IU, turkey dark meat ~60 IU. Other organ meats such as kidney and heart provide more vitamin A than muscle cuts but less than liver. Processed products made with liver (pâté, liverwurst) can be significant sources but may include added fats and sodium that affect dietary choice.

### Retinol, Bioavailability, and Cooking Effects

Retinol from animal foods is more bioavailable than provitamin A carotenoids from plants because it does not require enzymatic conversion. Cooking reduces vitamin A modestly; gentle methods (roasting, steaming) generally preserve more retinol than prolonged high-heat frying or boiling. Fat improves absorption since vitamin A is fat-soluble; pairing liver with healthy fats supports uptake.

### Nutrient Synergy in Liver

Liver is not only a retinol source but a micronutrient-dense food providing heme iron, vitamin B12, folate, B-group vitamins, choline, and trace minerals (copper, zinc, selenium). These nutrients interact—zinc plays a role in mobilizing vitamin A from liver stores, and iron and B12 support processes complementary to vitamin A’s roles.

### Practical Guidance and Safety

To manage intake precisely, some people use desiccated liver supplements or freeze-dried organ powders. When combining dietary liver with multivitamins or supplements, monitor total vitamin A intake; chronic intakes above established tolerable upper limits can cause hypervitaminosis A. Pregnant people and those on high-dose supplements should consult a healthcare professional.

### Bottom line

Liver is the single most concentrated meat source of vitamin A. Other meats and organs contribute smaller amounts and can help round out intake when combined with a varied diet and appropriate supplementation under professional guidance.
