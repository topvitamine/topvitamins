Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, nervous system integrity, and DNA synthesis. Deficiency can develop slowly and present with non-specific symptoms, yet its consequences are well documented and potentially serious. This article summarizes the main risks associated with inadequate B12 status and highlights clinical considerations for at-risk groups.

Early clinical features of B12 deficiency often include fatigue, weakness, pallor, and cognitive changes such as decreased concentration or ‘‘brain fog.’’ Hematologically, insufficient B12 impairs DNA synthesis in erythropoiesis, producing megaloblastic anemia characterized by large, dysfunctional red cells and systemic hypoxia. If left untreated, anemia can contribute to cardiac strain, shortness of breath, and reduced exercise tolerance.

Neurological and neuromuscular consequences are among the most concerning. B12 is required for myelin maintenance; deficiency can cause demyelination of peripheral nerves and spinal tracts. Patients may report paresthesia (numbness and tingling), limb weakness, gait instability, and reduced proprioception. Progressive demyelination can lead to subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord, with motor and sensory deficits that may become only partially reversible if correction is delayed.

Cognitive decline is also associated with low B12. Deficiency interferes with methylation pathways and neurotransmitter synthesis, and can raise homocysteine levels—an independent risk factor for vascular disease and cognitive impairment. In older adults, long-standing deficiency may mimic or accelerate dementia-like syndromes; early detection and correction improve outcomes in many, but not all, cases.

Absorption problems are central to why some people develop deficiency despite adequate intake. Pernicious anemia (autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor), gastric surgery, inflammatory bowel disease, and chronic use of certain medications (e.g., proton pump inhibitors, metformin) reduce B12 uptake. For these patients, oral intake alone may be insufficient and alternative delivery routes (high-dose oral, sublingual, intramuscular, or nasal) may be necessary to restore physiological levels.

Diagnosis relies on clinical assessment plus laboratory testing: serum B12, complete blood count, and adjunctive markers such as methylmalonic acid (MMA) or homocysteine to improve sensitivity. Management depends on severity and cause: correcting deficiency typically reverses hematological abnormalities within weeks, while neurological recovery can be slower and sometimes incomplete, emphasizing the importance of earlier recognition.

Preventive strategies in clinical practice focus on screening at-risk populations (older adults, strict vegetarians/vegans, patients with malabsorptive disorders, and those on long-term medications that impair absorption) and tailoring supplementation based on individual needs rather than universal dosing. Evidence supports the effectiveness of appropriate supplementation in preventing anemia and reducing the progression of neurological symptoms when instituted promptly.
