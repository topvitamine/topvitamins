Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, neurological function, and DNA synthesis. Because it is water-soluble and not stored in large amounts, regular intake through diet or supplementation is often recommended. Whether daily supplementation is appropriate depends on diet, age, medical history, and absorption capacity.

Most people who consume animal products regularly obtain adequate B12 from meat, dairy, eggs, and fortified foods. However, groups at higher risk of insufficiency include vegans and vegetarians, older adults with reduced stomach acid, people with gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., Crohn’s, celiac disease), those who have had bariatric surgery, and patients on long-term metformin or proton pump inhibitors. For these groups, targeted supplementation prevents deficiency and associated neurological or hematological complications.

Supplement forms influence effectiveness. Oral tablets and capsules are convenient for people with normal gastrointestinal function. Sublingual preparations and nasal sprays bypass parts of the digestive tract and can be useful when absorption is impaired. Intramuscular injections are reserved for severe deficiency or pernicious anemia and are administered under medical supervision. Absorption depends on intrinsic factor and gastric acidity. Older adults may struggle to absorb food-derived B12 due to reduced stomach acid; in such cases, oral crystalline B12 supplements, high-dose oral formulations, sublingual products, or periodic injections are practical alternatives. Recommended daily intakes vary by age and life stage. European guidance generally suggests about 4 µg/day for adults, with slightly higher needs during pregnancy and lactation. Practical supplementation doses commonly range from low microgram amounts in daily multivitamins (5–25 µg) to higher therapeutic doses (500–1000 µg) for older adults or those with impaired absorption. Severe deficiency may warrant medical dosing protocols.

To enhance absorption and efficacy, consider timing and co-nutrients: taking B12 on an empty stomach can improve uptake for some individuals, and pairing B12 with adequate folate and iron supports optimal red blood cell production and DNA synthesis. Safety: vitamin B12 has low toxicity and excess is excreted in urine; no tolerable upper intake level has been established. Nonetheless, very high doses can sometimes cause mild adverse effects in sensitive individuals and may mask underlying deficiencies such as folate. Regular blood monitoring (serum B12 or holotranscobalamin) guides appropriate dosing adjustments.

In practice, daily supplementation is reasonable for those with dietary gaps or absorption risk factors. For people with reliable dietary intake and normal absorption, routine supplementation may not be necessary.
