Multivitamin tablets are popular for filling dietary gaps, but like any supplement they carry potential side effects and risks. Understanding what can go wrong — from mild digestive complaints to serious vitamin toxicity — helps people use multivitamins safely and appropriately.

Multivitamins typically combine vitamins A, C, D, E, K and B-complex nutrients with minerals such as calcium, magnesium, zinc and selenium. When taken at recommended doses they can prevent deficiencies. However, adverse effects may occur when formulations are poorly matched to an individual’s needs, taken in excess, or combined with interacting medicines.

Common, short-term side effects include nausea, bloating, diarrhea or a metallic aftertaste. These symptoms often result from taking supplements on an empty stomach or from transient intolerance to specific ingredients. Less common but more significant problems arise from interactions: for example, vitamin K can reduce the effectiveness of warfarin-like anticoagulants, and high doses of vitamin E can increase bleeding risk in people on blood thinners. Minerals such as calcium and magnesium can also interfere with absorption of some antibiotics and thyroid medications.

Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are stored in body tissues and, if chronically overconsumed, can cause toxicity. Hypervitaminosis A may present with headaches, blurred vision, liver dysfunction and bone pain. Excessive vitamin D can cause hypercalcemia, potentially damaging kidneys and affecting cardiac conduction. Water-soluble vitamins are generally excreted when taken in excess, but very large doses of certain B vitamins or vitamin C can cause gastrointestinal upset or, in rare cases, neurologic symptoms (e.g., chronic high-dose B6 and peripheral neuropathy).

Vulnerable groups warrant special caution. Pregnant or breastfeeding people should avoid formulations with high preformed vitamin A due to teratogenic risk. Older adults and those with impaired kidney or liver function may accumulate nutrients more readily. Children require age-appropriate formulations and dosing. People with autoimmune conditions, malabsorption syndromes or multiple chronic illnesses should consult clinicians before starting supplements.

Quality and labeling matter. Products from unclear sources may contain contaminants or incorrect dosages. Reading labels carefully and understanding the percent of the recommended dietary allowance (RDA) can prevent accidental duplicate dosing when combining supplements.
