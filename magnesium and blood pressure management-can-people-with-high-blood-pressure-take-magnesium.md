## Can people with high blood pressure take magnesium?

Quick answer: magnesium is generally safe for most people with hypertension and may modestly reduce blood pressure when used appropriately, but individual factors—especially kidney function and concurrent medications—determine safety and effectiveness.

High blood pressure is a major cardiovascular risk factor worldwide. Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in vascular tone, endothelial function, and ion transport in cardiac and smooth muscle cells. By antagonizing calcium-mediated vasoconstriction and supporting potassium balance, magnesium can promote vasodilation and improve blood-flow regulation.

Evidence from randomized trials and meta-analyses suggests a modest antihypertensive effect. For example, pooled data show average reductions of a few mm Hg in systolic and diastolic blood pressure with supplemental magnesium—effects that are more notable in people with low baseline magnesium or established hypertension. Forms with higher bioavailability, such as magnesium citrate and glycinate, tend to produce larger changes than poorly absorbed salts.

Supplement types and dosing

- Common forms include magnesium oxide, citrate, glycinate, malate, and chloride. Oxide contains more elemental magnesium per pill but is less well absorbed; citrate and glycinate are usually better tolerated.
- Clinical studies targeting blood pressure often use supplemental ranges of roughly 300–500 mg elemental magnesium per day for 8–12 weeks. Recommended dietary allowances are lower (approximately 300–400 mg/day depending on age and sex), so supplements are used primarily to correct insufficiency or achieve therapeutic ranges.

Safety and interactions

- Kidney impairment increases the risk of magnesium accumulation; serum magnesium monitoring is advised for people with renal dysfunction.
- Certain diuretics can alter magnesium status (some cause loss, others reduce excretion), and magnesium can interfere with absorption of some antibiotics when co-administered. It may also potentiate antihypertensive effects in combination with other agents.
- Common adverse effects at higher doses include diarrhea and abdominal cramping; severe toxicity (rare with normal kidney function) can cause hypotension, bradycardia, and muscle weakness.

Practical guidance

- Discuss magnesium supplementation with your healthcare provider before starting, particularly if you take blood pressure medications or have kidney disease.
- Prefer bioavailable formulations if treating deficiency or aiming for cardiovascular effects; take magnesium with food to reduce gastrointestinal upset.
- Use supplementation as one element of a comprehensive plan that emphasizes diet (leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes, and whole grains), regular physical activity, sodium reduction, and weight management.
