Omega-3 fatty acids are widely recommended for cardiovascular and brain health, yet some people report feeling 'weird' after starting supplementation. Common complaints include brain fog, nausea, dizziness, digestive upset, and mood changes. These reactions are usually transient and can often be explained by physiological adjustments, product quality, or interactions with existing medications and conditions.

Many side effects stem from digestion and absorption. Omega-3s are dietary fats that require bile and pancreatic enzymes to be broken down; if fat digestion is impaired, unabsorbed oil can cause bloating, reflux, or diarrhea. Capsules that dissolve in the stomach can also release oil that produces an unpleasant taste or reflux, which some people experience as nausea. Enteric-coated formulations or taking supplements with a meal that contains fat can reduce these symptoms.

Cognitive symptoms such as temporary brain fog may occur when fatty acid ratios in neural membranes shift quickly. In some cases this reflects an adaptive period as neurotransmitter dynamics adjust to increased EPA and DHA. Another contributor is oil oxidation: rancid or oxidized omega-3s can provoke systemic inflammation and discomfort. Choosing fresh, laboratory-tested products and storing oils in cool, dark conditions helps reduce that risk. Dizziness or lightheadedness after omega-3 intake is less common but mechanistically plausible. Omega-3s can reduce platelet aggregation and modestly affect blood viscosity and blood pressure. In people on antihypertensives or anticoagulants, these effects could contribute to orthostatic symptoms. Monitoring blood pressure and discussing combined use with prescriptions is sensible for anyone concerned.

Mood shifts—ranging from increased irritability to transient emotional blunting—may appear during an adjustment period as neural signaling changes. While omega-3s are generally associated with mood benefits over longer periods, any persistent or severe mood changes should prompt consultation with a mental health professional and prescriber, especially if you are taking psychiatric medications.

Practical steps to reduce adverse reactions:
- Start at a low dose and increase gradually to allow metabolic adaptation.
- Take omega-3 supplements with food, ideally containing other dietary fats.
- Use enteric-coated or triglyceride-form supplements if you have sensitive digestion.
- Store oils correctly and choose third-party tested products to avoid oxidized oils.
- If you have cardiovascular or bleeding risks, check interactions with medications.

If symptoms such as severe nausea, prolonged dizziness, or marked mood instability persist, stop supplementation and seek medical evaluation to rule out underlying conditions such as gallbladder disease, pancreatitis, or significant drug interactions.
