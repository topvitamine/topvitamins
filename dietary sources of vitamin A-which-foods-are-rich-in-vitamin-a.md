Vitamin A is an essential, fat-soluble micronutrient required for vision, immune function, cellular communication, and skin health. It exists as preformed vitamin A (retinol and retinyl esters) in animal foods and as provitamin A carotenoids (notably beta‑carotene) in plant foods that the body converts to retinol as needed. Because humans cannot synthesize vitamin A, regular dietary intake from diverse sources is important for optimal health.

Key dietary sources of vitamin A include liver and other animal products that supply readily usable retinol, and colorful fruits and vegetables that provide carotenoids. Animal-based foods such as beef liver, cod liver oil, eggs, whole milk, and fortified dairy deliver preformed vitamin A with high bioavailability. Conversely, plant foods — carrots, sweet potatoes, spinach, kale, red bell peppers, pumpkin, mango, and cantaloupe — supply beta‑carotene and related compounds, which are safer at high intakes because conversion to retinol is regulated by the body.

Preparation and dietary context affect how much vitamin A you absorb. Carotenoids are fat‑soluble, so combining plant sources with modest amounts of healthy fats (olive oil, avocado, or nuts) improves absorption. Light cooking of carotenoid‑rich vegetables, such as steaming or sautéing carrots and leafy greens, can increase bioavailability compared with raw consumption. For example, the body typically converts about 12 micrograms of dietary beta‑carotene to 1 microgram of retinol activity equivalent (RAE), though this varies by food matrix and individual factors.

Meeting recommended intakes is generally attainable through balanced eating: the RDA for adult men is approximately 900 mcg RAE per day and for adult women about 700 mcg RAE. Small servings of high‑value foods—such as a modest portion of liver once a week, regular servings of orange and dark green vegetables, eggs, and full‑fat dairy—help maintain adequate status without excessive intake. Excessive consumption of preformed vitamin A (particularly from liver or high‑dose supplements) can cause toxicity, so moderation and attention to total intake are important.

Plant‑forward approaches are effective for most people. Smoothies with spinach and mango, roasted sweet potatoes, and mixed salads with red peppers and avocado are practical strategies to increase carotenoid intake. Those with malabsorption, certain medical conditions, or pregnancy should consult a healthcare professional about monitoring vitamin A status and whether supplementation is appropriate.
