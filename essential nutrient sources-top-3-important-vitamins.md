Introduction

Vitamins are micronutrients required in small amounts but essential for many physiological processes including immunity, energy metabolism, and tissue repair. In populations with limited sun exposure, restricted diets, or certain medical conditions, deficiencies are common and can meaningfully affect health. This article summarizes why vitamin D, vitamin B12, and vitamin C are frequently prioritized in public health guidance and supplementation decisions.

Why these three?

Selection here is evidence-based: these vitamins have high physiological impact, relatively common deficiency in many populations, and distinct clinical consequences when inadequate. Considerations include prevalence of deficiency, roles in metabolism and immunity, bioavailability, and the potential for dietary insufficiency.

Vitamin D: bone, muscle, and immune regulation

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble prohormone synthesized in skin exposed to UVB radiation and obtained from fatty fish and fortified foods. It facilitates intestinal calcium absorption and contributes to bone mineralization, muscle function, and immune regulation. Deficiency has been linked to osteomalacia in adults, rickets in children, and associations with increased infection risk and muscle weakness in observational studies.

Recommendations vary by authority, but many suggest around 15 µg (600 IU) daily for adults as a baseline, with individualized adjustments for limited sun exposure, darker skin, older age, or malabsorption. Because vitamin D is fat-soluble, concurrent dietary fat improves absorption; vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) generally shows superior bioavailability to D2 in comparative studies.

Vitamin B12: neurological function and hematopoiesis

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is required for DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation, and neurological maintenance. Deficiency can present with macrocytic anemia, neuropathy, cognitive changes, and fatigue. Food sources are primarily animal-derived (meat, fish, dairy, eggs); therefore, vegetarians and especially vegans are at elevated risk.

Absorption depends on intrinsic factor produced in the stomach; conditions that reduce stomach acid, gastric surgery, or autoimmune gastritis can impair uptake. Typical adult recommendations are approximately 2.4 µg/day, but clinical management may require higher oral doses, sublingual forms, or intramuscular administration in cases of malabsorption.

Vitamin C: antioxidant and connective tissue support

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble antioxidant involved in collagen synthesis, wound healing, and enhancement of non-heme iron absorption. Rich dietary sources include citrus fruits, bell peppers, berries, and cruciferous vegetables. Severe deficiency causes scurvy, while milder insufficiency can present with fatigue and impaired healing.

Recommended intakes often center around 80 mg/day for adults, with higher needs during smoking, infection, or increased oxidative stress. Because vitamin C is water-soluble, regular dietary intake or divided dosing can help maintain plasma levels.

Practical guidance

A mixed approach—diverse diet, sensible sun exposure, and targeted supplementation when indicated—addresses common gaps. Clinical assessment with blood tests (25-hydroxyvitamin D, serum B12, and dietary evaluation) can guide individualized recommendations. Summary

Vitamin D, B12, and C address complementary physiological domains—bone and immune health, neurological and hematologic function, and antioxidant and connective tissue support, respectively. Their prevalence of deficiency in specific groups and clear clinical consequences justify their prioritization in dietary assessment and, when necessary, supervised supplementation.
