Magnesium is essential for hundreds of biochemical reactions — from ATP production and nerve conduction to muscle relaxation and blood pressure regulation. Yet many patients report that their clinicians rarely recommend magnesium supplementation. This article explains the main reasons for that gap, reviews common deficiency signs and absorption issues, and outlines safe, evidence-based steps for people considering supplementation.

Why magnesium is overlooked

Several factors contribute to clinicians not routinely recommending magnesium: limited nutrition training in medical education, reliance on pharmaceutical treatments for acute conditions, and uncertainty about diagnostic testing. Standard serum magnesium tests reflect only a small fraction of total body stores, so clinicians may miss subclinical deficiencies. Additionally, concerns about interactions with medications and the need for individualized dosing make some providers cautious.

Signs of possible deficiency and who is at risk

Symptoms of low magnesium can be nonspecific — fatigue, muscle cramps, restless legs, headaches or worsened sleep — and they overlap with many other conditions. Groups at higher risk include older adults, people with gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease), those on certain diuretics or proton-pump inhibitors, heavy alcohol users, and individuals with diets low in whole grains, nuts, and green vegetables. Forms, absorption issues, and practical considerations

Magnesium comes in multiple forms with differing absorption and tolerability. Magnesium citrate and glycinate are generally better absorbed and gentler on the gut than magnesium oxide, which may cause laxative effects at higher doses. Absorption can be reduced by high dietary phytates, low stomach acid, chronic diarrhea, or certain medications. Dividing doses, taking magnesium with food, and addressing gut health can improve uptake. Evidence for specific conditions

There is moderate evidence supporting magnesium for prevention of migraine attacks, with typical preventive doses in trials around 300–600 mg/day of elemental magnesium. Some studies also show modest benefits for sleep quality, premenstrual symptoms, and blood pressure control when deficiency is present. Benefits are most consistent when supplementation corrects an existing shortfall rather than by adding excess magnesium to already adequate intakes.

Safety and testing

Dietary magnesium from foods does not have a defined upper limit, but supplemental magnesium has a tolerable upper intake level (UL) for adults from non-food sources; excess can cause diarrhea, abdominal cramping, and in extreme cases, cardiac issues—particularly in people with impaired kidney function. Discussing symptoms and medication lists with a clinician, and considering targeted testing (including intracellular assessments when available), can guide safe, individualized use.

Practical takeaway

Doctors may not routinely recommend magnesium because of diagnostic uncertainty, training gaps, and concerns about interactions and dosing. When symptoms or risk factors suggest a deficiency, evidence-based supplementation under clinical guidance — using well-absorbed forms and appropriate dosing — can be a reasonable, low-risk strategy.
