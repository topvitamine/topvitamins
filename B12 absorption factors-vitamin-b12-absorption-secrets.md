## Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, neurological function, and DNA synthesis. Despite its importance, many people struggle to maintain adequate B12 status because absorption involves multiple physiological steps and can be impaired by age, gastrointestinal disorders, medications, or dietary choices. This article summarizes the mechanisms of B12 uptake, common absorption barriers, and evidence-based strategies to optimize supplementation.

## How B12 Is Absorbed

Dietary B12 is released from food proteins in the stomach by gastric acid and pepsin, then binds to intrinsic factor (IF), a glycoprotein secreted by parietal cells. The B12–IF complex is recognized by receptors in the ileum and internalized. A minority of high oral doses can be absorbed by passive diffusion without IF. ## Key Factors That Affect Absorption

- Gastrointestinal health: Conditions such as atrophic gastritis, celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, or prior gastric/ileal surgery can reduce IF production or ileal uptake.
- Age: Older adults commonly have reduced stomach acid and IF, increasing deficiency risk.
- Medications: Proton pump inhibitors, H2 blockers, and long-term antacid use lower gastric acidity and can impair B12 release from foods.
- Dose and form: High-dose oral supplements exploit passive diffusion; sublingual and nasal formulations aim to bypass some gut steps, though evidence varies.
- Alcohol and antibiotics: Excessive alcohol damages gastric mucosa; antibiotics can alter gut flora and potentially influence B12 dynamics.

## Intrinsic Factor and Clinical Implications

Intrinsic factor is central to classical B12 absorption. Autoimmune destruction of parietal cells (pernicious anemia) leads to IF deficiency and impaired absorption regardless of intake. In such cases, parenteral B12 (intramuscular or subcutaneous injections) or very high-dose oral therapy are commonly used because they bypass IF-dependent uptake.

## Dietary Sources and Vegan Considerations

Natural, reliable B12 sources are primarily animal-derived (meat, dairy, eggs, shellfish). Vegans should rely on fortified foods (plant milks, cereals, nutritional yeast) or supplements. Some algae claims are inconclusive regarding bioavailable B12. Regular monitoring and appropriate supplementation dosing are advised for plant-based diets.

## Supplementation Methods and Practical Tips

- Oral high-dose tablets: Effective for many people; passive diffusion can deliver clinically meaningful amounts when doses are large (e.g., 500–1000 µg or more).
- Sublingual and nasal forms: May help in select cases; evidence is mixed but these options can be considered when oral absorption is uncertain.
- Injections: Indicated for severe deficiency, neurological symptoms, or confirmed IF deficiency.

## Testing and Monitoring

Serum B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA), and homocysteine testing provide complementary information. Clinicians tailor dosing and route based on symptomatology, lab results, and comorbid conditions.
