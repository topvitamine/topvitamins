Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of enzymatic reactions, supporting muscle and nerve function, energy production, and bone and cardiovascular health. While foods like leafy greens, nuts, seeds, and whole grains are primary sources, drinking water can also meaningfully contribute to daily magnesium intake depending on its origin and mineral profile. Understanding which waters supply the most magnesium helps inform dietary strategies that are both practical and passive.

Groundwater, spring water, and mineral water pick up dissolved minerals as they pass through soil and rock. Two major factors determine magnesium concentration: geology and hydrology. Waters that flow through dolomitic, serpentine, or volcanic rock tend to have higher magnesium levels, while waters sourced from limestone (calcium carbonate) or glacial/runoff systems often contain lower amounts. Climatic and seasonal factors can further influence concentrations through dilution or evaporation.

Typical magnesium ranges in natural and treated waters vary widely. Freshwaters may contain as little as 1 mg/L in rain-fed or glacial systems and exceed 100 mg/L in mineral-rich springs. Many tap waters fall between 1–50 mg/L depending on local geology and treatment; softened municipal waters may be low because ion-exchange softening replaces magnesium with sodium. Household filtration systems such as reverse osmosis or distillation also remove much of the mineral content unless they specifically re-mineralize the output.

Mineral and certain spring waters generally provide the highest and most reliable magnesium content. Bottled mineral waters are usually analyzed and labeled for their mineral content, making it easier to compare options. Some well-known mineral and spring waters contain tens to hundreds of mg/L of magnesium. For example, specific European mineral springs have been measured at 70–150 mg/L, and a handful of therapeutic springs can exceed these values substantially. For consumers wanting a quick reference, the World Health Organization reports average drinking-water magnesium concentrations in a broad range of 10–35 mg/L, but many bottled mineral waters are considerably higher.

When choosing water for magnesium intake, look for clearly stated mineral analyses on labels or municipal water quality reports. Mineral water and high-magnesium spring water will typically provide the largest per-liter contributions and can supply a meaningful fraction of the recommended 300–400 mg/day for adults when consumed regularly. However, for individuals with increased needs (athletes, pregnant people, certain medical conditions), water alone may not be sufficient and should be considered alongside dietary sources and clinically appropriate supplementation.
