## Quick overview

Cardiologists commonly point to three categories of food to limit or avoid because of their clear links to higher cardiovascular risk: processed foods, trans fats, and refined sugars. Dietary patterns that reduce these foods and emphasize whole, minimally processed items are associated with better blood pressure, lipid profiles, and lower inflammation.

For a focused review of this guidance, see [what cardiologists recommend avoiding](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-are-three-foods-cardiologists-say-not-to-eat).

## 1) Processed foods

Ultra-processed foods — including many frozen dinners, packaged snacks, canned soups, and processed meats — often contain high levels of sodium, preservatives, and added fats. Excess sodium raises blood pressure, while common additives and refined ingredients can promote systemic inflammation and insulin resistance. Observational studies published in major journals link high consumption of ultra-processed products with increased incidence of cardiovascular events. Practical steps: prioritize whole fruits and vegetables, whole grains, legumes, and minimally processed proteins.

## 2) Trans fats

Industrial trans fats (from partially hydrogenated oils) are among the most reliably harmful dietary components for heart health. They raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol, promote vascular inflammation, and accelerate atherosclerosis. Although many countries have restricted or banned partially hydrogenated oils, trans fats can still be present in some imported goods and older recipes used by bakeries or restaurants. Check ingredient lists for “partially hydrogenated oils” and replace baked or fried items made with these fats with alternatives using olive oil, nuts, seeds, or fatty fish as primary fat sources.

## 3) Refined sugars

Added sugars in sodas, sweetened beverages, confectionery, and many processed foods contribute to excess calorie intake, elevated triglycerides, abdominal obesity, and insulin resistance — all risk factors for heart disease. Liquid sugars are particularly problematic because they increase liver production of triglycerides and do not trigger the same satiety responses as whole foods. Aim to reduce intake of sugary drinks and processed snacks, and prefer whole fruit, plain dairy or unsweetened alternatives, and recipes that use minimal added sugar.

## Practical considerations and nutrient support

Avoiding these food categories is complementary to a dietary pattern rich in vegetables, fiber, unsaturated fats, and lean proteins. When nutrient intake is inadequate, evidence-based supplementation (for example, omega-3 fatty acids or specific micronutrients) can be considered under medical guidance. ## Bottom line

Reducing ultra-processed foods, eliminating industrial trans fats, and limiting added sugars are consistent, evidence-based recommendations from cardiology experts. Combined with whole-food eating, regular activity, and individualized medical advice, these changes lower cardiovascular risk without requiring extreme diets.
