Introduction

Vitamin A is an essential fat‑soluble micronutrient required for vision, immune function, reproduction and cellular communication. Because humans cannot synthesize vitamin A de novo, dietary sources of preformed vitamin A (retinol) or provitamin A carotenoids are necessary to meet daily needs. Eggs—particularly the yolks—are a widely available animal source of preformed vitamin A and are often considered when assessing routine intake.

How much vitamin A is in eggs?

A single egg yolk contains roughly 64 micrograms (mcg) of retinol on average; two conventional whole eggs therefore supply about 128 mcg. Better hen feeding practices (free‑range, pasture‑raised, organic) can increase yolk retinol content to roughly 75–90 mcg per yolk, meaning two high‑quality eggs may provide upwards of ~150–180 mcg of retinol. These values are consistent with nutrient composition studies that link hen diet and rearing conditions to yolk micronutrient density.

How this compares to recommendations

Recommended intakes vary: adult men ≈ 900 mcg retinol activity equivalents (RAE)/day and adult women ≈ 700 mcg RAE/day (with higher needs in pregnancy and lactation). Two conventional eggs therefore provide only about 15–22% of typical adult requirements; even pasture‑raised eggs supplying ~180 mcg represent under a quarter of the RDA for most adults. Thus, two eggs daily make a meaningful contribution but are not sufficient alone to meet standard vitamin A RDAs.

Bioavailability and dietary context

Egg‑derived retinol is highly bioavailable because it is preformed vitamin A and is emulsified in yolk lipids. Absorption is enhanced when eggs are eaten with dietary fat (olive oil, avocado, butter) and when gut fat absorption is intact. Cooking that breaks the yolk matrix (e.g., scrambling) can increase release of fat‑soluble nutrients; boiling generally preserves nutrients well. Conditions that impair fat absorption (certain gastrointestinal disorders) or low status of cofactors (zinc) can reduce effective uptake.

Practical implications

- For most adults, two eggs per day are a valuable but incomplete source of vitamin A; they pair well with carotenoid‑rich vegetables (carrots, spinach, sweet potato) and other animal sources if available.  
- Choosing higher‑quality eggs (pasture‑raised or organic) increases retinol intake per egg.  
- Risk of vitamin A toxicity from egg consumption alone is negligible; tolerable upper intake levels for preformed vitamin A are much higher than amounts obtainable from typical egg intake.

Conclusion

Two eggs a day provide a meaningful contribution to vitamin A intake, especially when sourced from pasture‑raised hens and consumed as part of a fat‑containing meal. They do not, however, reliably deliver a full day’s requirement for most adults and are best treated as one component of a varied diet or targeted nutritional plan.
