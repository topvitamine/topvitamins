# What does vitamin C do to your muscles?

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) supports several physiological processes that are relevant to muscle health, recovery, and performance. Its roles include collagen synthesis for connective tissue integrity, antioxidant protection against exercise-induced oxidative stress, and modulation of immune responses that can influence training availability.

## Collagen formation and connective tissue

One of vitamin C’s best-established functions is as a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase enzymes that stabilize collagen’s triple helix. Collagen is a major component of tendons, ligaments, fascia, and the extracellular matrix of muscle. Adequate vitamin C intake helps maintain tensile strength and supports repair after microtrauma caused by resistance training or repetitive loading. Low vitamin C status has been associated with impaired wound and tissue healing in clinical settings.

## Antioxidant protection during exercise

Intense and prolonged exercise increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in working muscle. Vitamin C donates electrons to neutralize aqueous-phase ROS, reducing biomarkers of lipid and protein oxidation. This antioxidant activity can limit cellular damage and inflammation following high oxidative-load sessions such as interval training or endurance events. However, very high antioxidant dosing has been shown in some studies to attenuate beneficial training adaptations (for example, mitochondrial biogenesis), so moderation is advised.

## Immune support and training continuity

Sufficient vitamin C supports innate and adaptive immune function, including the activity of neutrophils and lymphocytes. Regular supplementation (commonly ≥200 mg/day in trials) has been associated with shorter duration or lower severity of upper respiratory infections in physically active people, which in turn reduces training interruptions. By helping prevent or reduce infection-related downtime, vitamin C indirectly supports consistent progress in strength and endurance programs.

## Recovery, soreness, and inflammation

Vitamin C contributes to faster tissue repair through combined antioxidant and collagen-supporting actions. Some randomized trials report reductions in delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) and improvements in subjective recovery metrics when moderate supplemental doses are used around periods of heavy training. The nutrient also influences inflammatory mediators; lowering excessive post-exercise inflammation can help restore function without eliminating the signaling needed for adaptation.

## Practical considerations and dosing

Dietary sources (citrus fruits, berries, bell peppers, kiwi) usually cover recommended intakes for most people. Typical reference intakes are about 75 mg/day for women and 90 mg/day for men (EFSA). Athletes or those experiencing high training loads may use supplemental intakes in the 200–500 mg/day range under guidance to support recovery and immune resilience, while avoiding excessive chronic dosing that could interfere with training adaptations.

## Summary

Vitamin C supports connective tissue repair, reduces oxidative damage after exercise, and bolsters immune defenses that help maintain consistent training. Moderate, evidence-based supplementation can be useful for active individuals, combined with a balanced diet and attention to training load and recovery timing.
