Introduction

Brain plasticity — or neuroplasticity — is the brain’s ability to reorganize structure and function in response to learning, experience, and injury. Nutrition shapes the biochemical environment that allows plasticity to occur. Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is integral to methylation reactions, myelin maintenance, and mitochondrial metabolism, positioning it as a nutrient of interest when considering strategies to support neurogenesis, cognitive function, and neurological resilience. How B12 supports plasticity

Vitamin B12 is a cofactor for methionine synthase (methylation) and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (mitochondrial metabolism). Methylation produces S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the primary methyl donor for DNA and histone methylation, processes that regulate gene expression in neural progenitors and mature neurons. Succinyl-CoA links B12 to energy pathways that fuel synaptic activity and cell maintenance. Together, these roles help preserve myelin, support neurotransmitter metabolism, and maintain membrane integrity needed for synaptic plasticity.

Evidence summary

Preclinical studies: Animal and cell studies show B12 deficiency impairs myelination and neuronal development, while repletion restores biochemical markers and, in some models, structural features. These mechanistic findings support plausible pathways by which B12 status influences neurogenic niches.

Observational human studies: Low serum B12 or elevated functional biomarkers (methylmalonic acid, homocysteine) are associated with worse cognitive performance, greater brain atrophy in some cohorts, and neuropathic signs. Such associations are strongest in older adults and those with malabsorption or restrictive diets.

Intervention trials: Randomized trials produce mixed results. Cognitive or structural benefits are most likely when supplementation corrects a demonstrable deficiency or elevated homocysteine. Trials in replete individuals rarely demonstrate consistent cognitive gains, suggesting supplementation is most effective as deficiency correction rather than enhancement beyond sufficiency.

Practical considerations

Assessment: Screen at-risk individuals (older adults, strict vegetarians/vegans, people with gastrointestinal conditions, or long-term acid suppression) using serum B12 and, when needed, methylmalonic acid or homocysteine to clarify tissue-level status.

Forms and dosing: Methylcobalamin and cyanocobalamin are commonly used; methylcobalamin is the biologically active methylated form. Oral high-dose regimens enable passive absorption and can be alternatives to injections when intrinsic factor or gastric absorption is impaired, while injections are often used in severe deficiency or neurological involvement.

Integrated approach: B12 supports the biochemical substrate for plasticity but is not a standalone solution. Combine adequate B12 status with exercise, cognitive engagement, sleep optimization, vascular risk management, and complementary nutrients such as omega-3s, vitamin D, and magnesium. Conclusion

Vitamin B12 has clear biochemical roles that support processes fundamental to neuroplasticity. Evidence indicates that preventing and correcting deficiency is a reasonable, low-risk strategy to maintain the metabolic environment necessary for neurogenesis, cognitive function, and nerve health. Decisions about testing and supplementation should be guided by risk profile, laboratory assessment when indicated, and integration within a broader lifestyle and nutritional plan.
