Introduction

Sun exposure generates oxidative stress that contributes to photoaging, pigmentation changes and an increased risk of skin cancer. Nutritional approaches can complement topical photoprotection by strengthening skin defenses from the inside. Among dietary constituents, beta‑carotene — a carotenoid precursor of vitamin A — has attracted attention for its antioxidant properties and its capacity to support skin resilience.

Beta‑Carotene and Skin Health

Beta‑carotene is found in orange and dark‑green produce such as carrots, sweet potatoes and spinach. Once ingested, it can be converted into retinol (vitamin A) and also acts directly as an antioxidant. Experimental and human studies indicate that adequate carotenoid status is associated with reduced biomarkers of oxidative damage and modest improvements in skin texture and coloration. Importantly, dietary sources contribute to tissue deposition of carotenoids, and supplementation can ensure consistent intakes when dietary consumption is low.

Mechanisms of Antioxidant Protection

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation produces reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage lipids, proteins and DNA. Beta‑carotene’s conjugated double‑bond structure enables quenching of singlet oxygen and scavenging of free radicals, which reduces the burden of oxidative stress in skin cells. In addition, beta‑carotene can interact with and help regenerate other antioxidants such as vitamin E, forming part of an integrated defense network in the epidermis.

Evidence and Practical Considerations

Clinical trials have observed that sustained beta‑carotene supplementation may increase the minimal erythema dose in some individuals and reduce certain markers of photoaging, although effects vary by dose, baseline nutrient status and skin phototype. Typical supplemental ranges reported in research and practice are roughly 6–30 mg per day; benefits usually require consistent intake over weeks to months rather than immediate protection. High doses may cause benign carotenemia (skin yellowing) and may interact with medications, so personalized guidance from a healthcare professional is advisable.

Integrating Nutrition with Other Strategies

Internal nutritional measures should be combined with external sun‑safety behaviors. Wearing protective clothing, seeking shade during peak UV hours and using broad‑spectrum sunscreens remain primary measures for UV protection. Nutritional strategies, including a diet rich in a variety of carotenoids, can act as a complementary layer of protection. Conclusion

Beta‑carotene is a biologically plausible component of a multifaceted approach to reducing sun‑related skin damage. Its antioxidant capacity and role as a vitamin A precursor support skin integrity, but it is not a substitute for topical sunscreens and behavioral sun precautions. Combining dietary carotenoids, appropriate supplementation when needed, and established sun‑safety measures offers a balanced, evidence‑informed strategy for enhancing skin resilience.
