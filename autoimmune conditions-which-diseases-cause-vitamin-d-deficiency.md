## Which diseases cause a vitamin D deficiency?

Vitamin D is essential for calcium homeostasis, bone mineralization, and immune regulation. While inadequate sun exposure and poor dietary intake are common contributors, several diseases and physiological states directly cause or exacerbate vitamin D deficiency by impairing synthesis, absorption, conversion, or increasing metabolic loss.

Primary disease-related mechanisms

- Malabsorption syndromes: Conditions that disrupt intestinal fat absorption reduce uptake of fat‑soluble vitamins, including vitamin D. Examples include celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, chronic pancreatitis, and cystic fibrosis. Patients with these disorders frequently require higher oral doses or specialized formulations to achieve adequate 25(OH)D levels.

- Hepatic dysfunction: The liver catalyzes the 25‑hydroxylation step that produces calcidiol (25[OH]D). Chronic liver disease and cholestatic disorders can lower circulating 25(OH)D concentrations and impair storage, making deficiency more likely.

- Renal disease: The kidney converts 25(OH)D to the active form, 1,25(OH)2D (calcitriol). Chronic kidney disease (CKD) reduces this conversion and is a common cause of secondary vitamin D deficiency and associated mineral bone disorders.

- Obesity and adiposity: Vitamin D is sequestered in adipose tissue, reducing bioavailability in circulation. Obese individuals often demonstrate lower serum 25(OH)D despite similar intakes, and may need higher supplementation to normalize levels.

- Medications and metabolic inducers: Drugs that induce hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes (for example, anticonvulsants like phenytoin or carbamazepine, certain rifamycins, and glucocorticoids) accelerate vitamin D catabolism. Long‑term use is associated with lower 25(OH)D and higher fracture risk.

- Genetic disorders and rare conditions: Mutations affecting the vitamin D binding protein, the vitamin D receptor, or enzymes involved in activation (e.g., CYP27B1) can produce functional deficiency states that are resistant to conventional supplementation and may require specialized care.

Diseases associated with deficiency and clinical consequences

Prolonged or severe vitamin D deficiency can directly cause rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults, conditions characterized by defective bone mineralization. In older adults, chronic insufficiency contributes to osteoporosis and increased fracture risk by impairing calcium absorption and altering bone remodeling.

Autoimmune and chronic inflammatory diseases such as multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and type 1 diabetes are epidemiologically associated with lower vitamin D status. While causality is complex and bidirectional, vitamin D has recognized immunomodulatory effects that may influence disease activity and progression.

Clinical implications and management

Diagnosis relies on measuring serum 25(OH)D, assessing risk factors, and identifying underlying disorders that limit absorption or conversion. Management should address the primary disease (for example, treating malabsorption or optimizing liver and renal function) while correcting vitamin D status with appropriate dosing and formulation. Patients with CKD or hepatic impairment may require activated forms or supervised regimens.

Monitoring is important to ensure efficacy and avoid toxicity, particularly in patients receiving high doses or those with altered metabolism. Nutrient co‑factors such as calcium, magnesium, and vitamin K can affect bone outcomes and may be considered in comprehensive management plans.
