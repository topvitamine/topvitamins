## Introduction

Maintaining adequate micronutrient status is essential for normal physiology. Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is a water-soluble vitamin with a cobalt-containing corrin ring that plays key roles in the nervous system, red blood cell formation, and one-carbon metabolism. This article summarizes the biology, clinical significance, common deficiency features, absorption mechanisms, dietary sources for plant-based diets, and practical considerations for supplementation.

## Biological roles and evidence-based benefits

Vitamin B12 functions as a cofactor in two central enzymatic reactions: methylcobalamin-dependent methionine synthase, which supports homocysteine remethylation and methylation reactions, and adenosylcobalamin-dependent methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, which is involved in mitochondrial metabolism. Clinically substantiated contributions include support for normal energy-yielding metabolism, nervous system function, red blood cell formation, psychological function, and normal homocysteine metabolism. While observational studies explore associations with cognitive aging and mood, benefits are clearest when correcting inadequate status.

## Recognizing B12 deficiency

Symptoms of low B12 can be hematologic (macrocytic anemia, pallor, fatigue) and neurologic (paresthesia, balance problems, cognitive changes). Gastrointestinal signs such as glossitis and appetite changes may occur. Risk factors include strict vegan diets without reliable fortified foods or supplements, older age with reduced gastric acidity, gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., ileal resection, Crohn’s disease), pernicious anemia, and certain long-term medications (for example, proton pump inhibitors and metformin). Laboratory assessment often begins with serum B12 and, when results are equivocal, methylmalonic acid and total homocysteine as functional markers.

## Absorption and implications for supplementation

Dietary B12 is released from protein in the stomach, temporarily binds haptocorrin, and then associates with intrinsic factor in the small intestine; the intrinsic factor–B12 complex is absorbed in the terminal ileum via specific receptors. Disruption at any step (low gastric acid, intrinsic factor deficiency, ileal disease or resection, bacterial overgrowth) reduces bioavailability. High-dose oral regimens can allow passive diffusion to supply some B12 without intrinsic factor, but intramuscular injections (hydroxocobalamin or cyanocobalamin) are standard when malabsorption or neurologic involvement is established.

## Forms and routes of supplements

Oral supplements commonly contain cyanocobalamin (stable, converted to active forms) or methylcobalamin (an active form involved in methylation). Sublingual preparations, nasal sprays, and injectable forms offer alternatives tailored to absorption capacity and clinical needs. Safety is favorable because excess B12 is typically excreted; adverse events are uncommon but include injection-site reactions and rare hypersensitivity.

## Vegan B12 sources and practical advice

Reliable sources for people following plant-based diets are fortified foods (certain plant milks, fortified cereals, nutritional yeast) and supplements. Consistent intake or routine supplementation combined with periodic testing is the most dependable prevention strategy, particularly during pregnancy and lactation when maternal status determines fetal and infant stores.

## Conclusion

Vitamin B12 is essential for several well-established physiological functions. Individuals at higher risk of insufficiency should monitor status and discuss appropriate dietary or supplemental strategies with a healthcare professional. Decisions about route, dose, and form depend on absorption capacity, clinical urgency, and laboratory assessment, and should follow evidence-based clinical guidance.
