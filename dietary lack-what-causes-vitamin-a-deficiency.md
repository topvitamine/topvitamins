Introduction: Understanding the Importance of Vitamin A in Our Diet

Vitamin A is a fat‑soluble micronutrient essential for vision, immune function, epithelial integrity, and reproductive health. Deficiency can present as night blindness, increased infection risk, and in severe cases xerophthalmia and permanent vision loss. This short review outlines the common causes of vitamin A deficiency and practical considerations for prevention and management; see [this guide on causes of vitamin A deficiency](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-causes-vitamin-a-deficiency) for further reading.

Dietary insufficiency

One of the most frequent drivers of vitamin A deficiency is inadequate dietary intake. Animal sources (liver, egg yolk, full‑fat dairy, oily fish) provide preformed vitamin A that is readily bioavailable. Plant sources like carrots, sweet potatoes, and dark leafy greens supply provitamin A carotenoids (e.g., beta‑carotene) that require enzymatic conversion to retinol. Diets lacking diversity, economic food insecurity, cultural avoidance of animal products, or reliance on staple grains can produce chronic low intake. Fortification programs and public education are established population strategies to counteract these gaps.

Impaired absorption and fat malabsorption

Because vitamin A is fat soluble, disorders that reduce fat absorption often lead to deficiency. Conditions such as pancreatic insufficiency, cholestatic liver disease, celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and post‑bariatric surgery states can limit micelle formation and uptake. Chronic diarrheal disease and cystic fibrosis are other examples. In clinical practice, diagnosing fat malabsorption and addressing its cause is as important as replacing the vitamin; specialized formulations (emulsified or oil‑based preparations) may improve bioavailability.

Increased requirements and physiological losses

Certain life stages and conditions increase vitamin A requirements: pregnancy, lactation, and periods of rapid growth in infancy and childhood. Acute or chronic infections increase turnover and loss of retinoids, placing further strain on reserves. Alcohol misuse and chronic liver disease can impair storage and mobilization, amplifying deficiency risk.

Conversion inefficiency and genetic factors

Individual variability in converting carotenoids to retinol affects susceptibility. Genetic polymorphisms in carotenoid cleavage enzymes and differences in intestinal health or co‑nutrient status (iron, zinc) can reduce conversion efficiency, making plant‑based diets less protective for some people.

Medication interactions and supplement considerations

Some drugs that impair fat absorption or hepatic processing may lower vitamin A status. Conversely, supplementation requires caution because preformed vitamin A (retinol) is toxic at high doses. Beta‑carotene supplements are safer in terms of toxicity but may be less effective for those with conversion issues. Xerophthalmia and public health responses

Severe, prolonged deficiency causes xerophthalmia, beginning with night blindness and progressing to corneal damage. WHO recommends targeted high‑dose supplementation for at‑risk children and fortification strategies where dietary change is not immediately feasible. Screening high‑risk populations and addressing underlying malabsorption or increased demand are key clinical steps.

Conclusion

Vitamin A deficiency arises from a mix of inadequate intake, malabsorption, increased physiological demand, and individual conversion differences. Prevention relies on dietary diversity, attention to fat intake for absorption, targeted supplementation when appropriate, and public health measures such as fortification.
