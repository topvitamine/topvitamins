## Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is an essential, water-soluble vitamin required for DNA synthesis, nervous system function, and red blood cell production. Because humans cannot synthesize B12, adequate intake depends on dietary sources or supplementation. Recent research has highlighted B12’s potential role in cardiovascular health, particularly through its influence on homocysteine metabolism and vascular integrity.

This article summarizes evidence-based mechanisms by which B12 may support heart health, identifies populations at risk of deficiency, and links to additional resources for readers who want deeper background.

## Mechanisms linking B12 to cardiovascular health

One of the most consistently studied pathways connecting B12 to heart health is homocysteine regulation. Homocysteine is an intermediate amino acid formed during methionine metabolism. Elevated plasma homocysteine (hyperhomocysteinemia) has been associated with endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and atherosclerotic plaque development. Vitamin B12 acts as a cofactor for methionine synthase, the enzyme that remethylates homocysteine to methionine, thereby helping to lower circulating homocysteine concentrations.

Beyond homocysteine, B12 contributes to maintaining the myelin sheath of neurons and supports erythropoiesis (red blood cell formation). Adequate red blood cell production ensures efficient oxygen delivery and reduces compensatory cardiac workload that can arise from anemia.

## Evidence and clinical context

Randomized trials and meta-analyses investigating B-vitamin combinations (B12 with folate and B6) have shown consistent reductions in homocysteine levels. Results for hard cardiovascular endpoints (heart attacks, strokes) are more mixed and appear to depend on baseline nutrient status, dosage, and population characteristics. In people with documented B12 deficiency or elevated homocysteine, correcting deficiencies is a rational component of cardiovascular risk management, while population-wide supplementation for primary prevention remains less definitive.

## Who is at risk of deficiency?

Groups at increased risk of B12 deficiency include older adults, strict vegetarians and vegans, individuals with malabsorption syndromes (e.g., pernicious anemia, post-gastric surgery), and those taking certain medications that impair absorption. Biochemical screening (serum B12, methylmalonic acid, and homocysteine when indicated) helps identify deficiency before irreversible neurological changes occur.

## Practical considerations

When deficiency is identified, clinicians may recommend dietary adjustments or supplementation using bioavailable forms such as methylcobalamin or cyanocobalamin, with the route and dose individualized based on absorption status. Decisions about supplementation should be made in consultation with a healthcare professional.

## Conclusion

Vitamin B12 supports several physiological processes relevant to cardiovascular health, notably homocysteine metabolism, red blood cell formation, and neurological function. Identification and correction of deficiency are important clinical goals, especially in at-risk populations. Evidence supports biochemical benefits of B12 supplementation in deficient individuals; the impact on long-term cardiovascular events depends on individual context and should be considered alongside established lifestyle and medical risk-reduction strategies.
