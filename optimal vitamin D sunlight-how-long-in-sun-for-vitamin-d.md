Vitamin D is primarily synthesized in the skin when ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation converts 7-dehydrocholesterol into previtamin D3. This article summarizes evidence-based guidance on how long to be in the sun to support vitamin D status while minimizing skin damage.

Basics of vitamin D from sunlight

Production begins almost immediately on UVB exposure, but effective synthesis depends on several variables: skin pigmentation, age, latitude, season, time of day, cloud cover and clothing. Midday sun—usually between 10:00 AM and 3:00 PM—offers the strongest UVB for vitamin D production, but also poses higher risks for sunburn and cumulative photodamage.

How long is enough?

General guidance for many light-skinned individuals is 10–30 minutes of direct, unprotected sun exposure to face, arms or legs, two to three times per week. People with darker skin may require several times longer exposure because melanin reduces UVB penetration. Older adults synthesize less vitamin D in the skin and may need greater reliance on dietary sources or supplements.

A practical rule of thumb is the “shadow rule”: if your shadow is shorter than you are, UVB intensity is likely sufficient for vitamin D synthesis. Start with short exposures (5–15 minutes) and adjust based on skin response and local UV index.

Safety and timing

Balance is essential. Brief, regular exposure during midday can stimulate vitamin D production; after this exposure, apply sun protection if remaining outdoors. Avoid prolonged unprotected exposure that causes redness or burns. Wearing protective clothing, hats and broad-spectrum sunscreen outside of the target exposure window reduces cumulative damage.

UV index reports are useful: a UV index of about 3 or higher generally allows efficient production, while in high latitudes during winter there may be insufficient UVB even at midday. Monitoring and special situations

Serum 25(OH)D is the standard test for status; target concentrations commonly cited are roughly 50–125 nmol/L (20–50 ng/mL), though optimal ranges may vary by guideline. Individuals with limited sun exposure, those who are older, have darker skin, are obese, or live at higher latitudes may need dietary fortification or supplementation after professional assessment.

Key takeaways

Short, regular midday sun exposures can support vitamin D synthesis for many people, but exposure time varies widely by skin type and environment. Avoid sunburn, monitor vitamin D status clinically when needed, and combine sensible sunlight exposure with dietary sources or supplements when natural UVB is insufficient.
