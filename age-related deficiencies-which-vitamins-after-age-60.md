Aging alters nutrient needs and absorption, so people over 60 benefit from a focused, evidence-based approach to vitamins and minerals. Aging-related changes—reduced stomach acid, altered skin synthesis of vitamin D, medication interactions, and lower appetite—can create gaps that affect bone strength, cognition, immune resilience, and cellular repair.

Key nutrients to prioritize

- Vitamin D (cholecalciferol): Essential for calcium absorption, muscle function, and immune regulation. Many guidelines recommend 600–800 IU (15–20 µg) daily for older adults, with individualized higher dosing only under clinical supervision when deficiency is documented.

- Vitamin B12 (methylcobalamin preferred form): Critical for nerve health and red blood cell formation. Decreased intrinsic factor and hypochlorhydria raise deficiency risk in seniors; routine screening and replacement (oral, sublingual, or intramuscular when indicated) are common strategies. For practical guidance on timing and forms, see <a href='https://topvitamine.github.io/topvitamins/when-to-take-vitamin-b12'>When to take vitamin B12</a>.

- B6 and folate: Part of homocysteine metabolism; combined supplementation can help normalize elevated homocysteine, a biomarker linked to vascular and cognitive risk.

- Calcium, magnesium, and vitamin K2: Work together to support bone mineralization and vascular health. Calcium intake around 1,000–1,200 mg/day is typical for older adults, balanced with vitamin D and cofactors like magnesium and K2.

- Antioxidants (vitamin C, vitamin E, carotenoids): Protect against oxidative stress that accumulates with age. Vitamin C supports collagen and immune function; vitamin E protects lipid membranes. Lutein and zeaxanthin concentrate in the retina and may support macular health.

- Zinc and selenium: Important for immune competence and antioxidant enzyme function; deficiency is not uncommon and can impair wound healing and infection responses.

- Omega-3 fatty acids (DHA/EPA): Although not vitamins, DHA supports neuronal membrane integrity and cardiovascular health; supplementation may complement dietary intake for brain and eye support.

Assessment and personalization

Blood testing (25‑OH vitamin D, serum B12, complete blood count, ferritin, and other targeted assays) provides objective evidence of deficiency and guides dosing. Because seniors often take multiple medications, interactions (for example, vitamin K with anticoagulants or malabsorption from proton-pump inhibitors) should be reviewed by a clinician.

Formulation and bioavailability

Choose forms with demonstrated bioavailability: cholecalciferol (D3) for vitamin D, methylcobalamin for B12, and chelated minerals when absorption is a concern. Consider ease of administration—sublingual, liquid, or small tablets—for those with swallowing difficulties. Practical points

- Routine screening and individualized plans are preferable to blanket high-dose regimens.  
- Combine diet, lifestyle (safe sunlight exposure, physical activity), and targeted supplementation when indicated.  
- Use clinically validated dosing and monitor labs when prescribing higher therapeutic doses.
