# Why Don't Doctors Recommend Magnesium?

Magnesium is essential for hundreds of enzymatic reactions, including muscle and nerve function, energy metabolism, and cardiac rhythm. Yet it is not uncommon for clinicians to refrain from broadly recommending magnesium supplements. Understanding why requires looking at diagnostic limitations, absorption challenges, and risk–benefit trade-offs.

Clinicians often prioritize evidence-based testing and targeted treatment. Unlike iron or vitamin D, magnesium status is difficult to assess with routine blood work because only about 1% of total body magnesium circulates in serum. As a result, a serum magnesium level can be misleading when cellular or bone stores are low. Symptoms of magnesium deficiency are often non-specific—fatigue, muscle cramps, insomnia, anxiety, and palpitations—so clinicians commonly evaluate other more common causes first (thyroid disease, anemia, mood disorders). Because symptom overlap is broad, physicians generally recommend testing or conservative dietary changes before prescribing supplements.

Absorption and excretion complicate clinical decisions. Gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease), chronic diarrhea, and certain medications (proton pump inhibitors, some diuretics, certain antibiotics) impair magnesium uptake or increase losses. Aging and renal dysfunction further alter magnesium handling: reduced intake, polypharmacy, and impaired kidney clearance change both need and risk. These considerations explain why doctors tailor guidance to individual risk factors rather than issue universal supplementation advice.

Supplementation carries potential side effects. Oral magnesium can cause gastrointestinal upset—particularly diarrhea—depending on the salt form (e.g., oxide vs. glycinate). In people with impaired renal function, supplementation can lead to elevated serum magnesium and cardiovascular complications. Interactions with medications and the possibility of exceeding tolerable upper intake levels also make clinicians cautious without clear indications or monitoring.

For many people, dietary approaches are both effective and lower risk. Magnesium-rich foods include dark leafy greens, legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds, and some fatty fish. Food sources provide magnesium within a matrix of other nutrients and are self-limiting in excess intake; the body generally regulates absorption from foods efficiently.

When is supplementation appropriate? Clinicians often consider supplements for patients with documented deficiency (when advanced testing is used), those with malabsorptive disorders, people taking chronic medications that deplete magnesium, or older adults with demonstrated low intake or symptoms suggestive of deficiency. Even then, dosing and formulation are individualized and may involve monitoring.

In short, doctors do not usually recommend magnesium universally because of diagnostic limitations, variable absorption and excretion, symptom overlap with other conditions, and potential risks in vulnerable populations. An individualized approach—prioritizing dietary sources, assessing risk factors, and using targeted testing—better balances benefits and harms.
