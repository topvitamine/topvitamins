Introduction

Vitamin D is widely known for its role in bone health, but its effects on skeletal muscle are increasingly recognized. At a cellular level, active vitamin D (calcitriol) binds to vitamin D receptors (VDRs) in muscle tissue and influences gene expression related to protein synthesis, calcium handling, and regenerative processes. These actions help explain observed associations between vitamin D status and measures of muscle strength, balance, and recovery.

Mechanisms linking vitamin D to muscle function

Vitamin D affects muscle through several mechanisms. First, it modulates calcium uptake in muscle cells, supporting excitation–contraction coupling necessary for efficient contractions. Second, VDR-mediated genomic signaling influences pathways such as Akt/mTOR, which are central to muscle growth and maintenance. Third, vitamin D supports satellite cell activation and muscle repair after injury. Together, these molecular effects favor the maintenance of type II (fast-twitch) fibers that contribute to power and quick force generation—attributes that decline with age and with vitamin D insufficiency.

Evidence from clinical studies

Cross-sectional and intervention studies suggest a relationship between serum 25(OH)D concentrations and physical performance. Lower levels have been correlated with reduced grip strength, slower gait, and impaired balance in older adults. Randomized trials indicate that correcting deficiency can reduce fall risk and modestly improve measures of strength, particularly in persons with low baseline status. Combining vitamin D supplementation with resistance training tends to produce larger functional gains than training alone.

Deficiency: presentation and consequences

Vitamin D deficiency often presents subtly in the musculoskeletal system: proximal weakness (hip and thigh), difficulty rising from chairs, fatigue, and increased fall risk are common complaints. At the tissue level, reduced VDR activity impairs regenerative and anti-inflammatory signaling, promoting atrophy and delayed recovery. Athletes with insufficient vitamin D may also experience higher rates of stress fractures and slower post-exercise recovery.

Practical considerations for supplementation

Assessment of serum 25(OH)D is the standard for diagnosing deficiency. Recommended corrective and maintenance doses vary by guideline and individual needs; clinicians commonly use higher loading doses for deficiency and lower daily doses for maintenance. Nutrient interactions matter: magnesium is required for vitamin D metabolism, and adequate calcium supports muscle contraction. Conclusion

Vitamin D is a contributor to muscle health through effects on calcium transport, genomic signaling, and tissue repair. Deficiency can impair strength, balance, and recovery, while repletion—combined with appropriate exercise and attention to cofactors like magnesium and calcium—may improve functional outcomes.
