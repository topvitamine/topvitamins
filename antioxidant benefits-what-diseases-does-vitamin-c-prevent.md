## Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble micronutrient with well-established roles in human health, including antioxidant protection, collagen synthesis, iron absorption, and immune function. Because the body cannot synthesize vitamin C, regular dietary intake or supplementation is required to maintain adequate levels. This article summarizes evidence-based relationships between vitamin C and the prevention or mitigation of several diseases and conditions.

## Key disease-prevention roles

Vitamin C prevents scurvy, the classic deficiency disease characterized by bleeding gums, impaired wound healing, and fatigue; even small daily intakes reliably avert this condition. Beyond deficiency prevention, vitamin C contributes to reducing risk or severity for a range of health issues through antioxidant and immune-supporting actions.

- Immune and respiratory infections: Vitamin C accumulates in leukocytes and supports functions such as phagocytosis and lymphocyte proliferation. Randomized trials and meta-analyses indicate that routine supplementation does not markedly reduce cold incidence in the general population but can shorten duration and lessen symptom severity. Subgroups under heavy physical stress (e.g., athletes, military personnel) appear to receive stronger preventive benefit.

- Oxidative stress–related chronic disease: As an antioxidant, vitamin C neutralizes free radicals and helps regenerate other antioxidants such as vitamin E. Observational studies associate higher plasma vitamin C with lower risk of cardiovascular events and stroke, and experimental data suggest it may protect endothelial function and reduce LDL oxidation — early steps in atherogenesis. While vitamin C alone is not a cure for chronic disease, it contributes to a broader dietary strategy that reduces oxidative burden.

- Skin health and wound healing: Vitamin C is required for hydroxylation of collagen constituents, supporting skin integrity, elasticity, and repair. Both oral and topical vitamin C improve wound healing parameters and offer photoprotective antioxidant effects that can reduce photoaging and some precancerous skin changes.

- Inflammatory and immune-mediated conditions: By modulating cytokine responses and limiting oxidative tissue damage, vitamin C may attenuate inflammation in conditions where oxidative stress contributes to pathology (for example, certain pulmonary or rheumatologic disorders). Evidence is still emerging and typically supports adjunctive rather than primary therapeutic roles.

## Practical considerations

Dietary sources such as citrus fruits, berries, kiwifruit, bell peppers, and cruciferous vegetables remain the preferred approach to achieving adequate vitamin C and delivering complementary phytonutrients. Supplementation can be appropriate for individuals with limited intake, increased requirements (smokers, some chronic illnesses), or during periods of heightened infection risk.

Megadoses are generally unnecessary for prevention and can cause gastrointestinal symptoms in some individuals. Clinical protocols that use higher intravenous doses are a separate context and should be managed by healthcare professionals.

## Conclusion

Vitamin C is essential for preventing deficiency disease and contributes to immune resilience, antioxidant defense, skin health, and supportive prevention of certain chronic conditions. It is most effective as part of a balanced approach that includes a varied diet, adequate sleep, and other lifestyle measures. Where appropriate, moderate supplementation can help maintain optimal levels and support preventive health goals.
