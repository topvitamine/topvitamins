Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation, and nervous system maintenance. Deficiency develops slowly and can present with hematological, neurological, cognitive, and gastrointestinal signs. Early recognition helps guide diagnostic testing and appropriate supplementation strategies.

Many clinical features stem from impaired DNA synthesis and disrupted myelin maintenance. Hematologically, B12 deficiency produces megaloblastic anemia with fatigue, pallor, shortness of breath, and reduced exercise tolerance. Neurologically, impaired myelin repair can cause paresthesia (tingling or numbness in hands and feet), gait instability, and muscle weakness. Cognitive effects range from subtle attention problems and “brain fog” to significant memory loss and mood changes. Gastrointestinal complaints may include glossitis, appetite loss, nausea, and altered bowel habits.

Risk factors include limited dietary intake (notably strict vegan diets), age-related reductions in gastric acid and intrinsic factor production, autoimmune pernicious anemia, and gastrointestinal disorders or surgeries that impair absorption. Medications that reduce stomach acidity (e.g., proton pump inhibitors) or affect intrinsic factor can also contribute. Laboratory evaluation typically includes serum B12 level, complete blood count, and, when indicated, methylmalonic acid and homocysteine concentrations to improve diagnostic sensitivity.

Treatment depends on cause and severity. Dietary counseling is appropriate for those with insufficient intake, while individuals with malabsorption or pernicious anemia often require high-dose oral, sublingual, or parenteral B12 to bypass impaired gastrointestinal uptake. Early intervention often reverses hematological abnormalities and can improve neurological and cognitive symptoms; however, prolonged deficiency may lead to irreversible nerve damage in some cases.

Clinical management benefits from a comprehensive approach. Coexisting nutrient deficiencies (for example, folate, vitamin B6, vitamin D, magnesium, and omega-3 fatty acids) can influence symptoms and recovery, so assessment and correction of related nutrients may be warranted. Monitoring therapy with follow-up blood tests helps ensure restoration of adequate B12 stores and normalization of hematologic indices.
