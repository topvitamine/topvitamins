## Introduction

Vitamin A is an essential fat-soluble micronutrient important for vision, immune function, epithelial integrity and growth. Whether it is appropriate to take vitamin A every day depends on dietary intake, life stage, health status and the form of vitamin A consumed. Daily low-dose supplementation can be useful for people with insufficient dietary intake or malabsorption, but excess preformed vitamin A carries toxicity risks.

## Recommended intake and sources

Authorities such as EFSA define population reference intakes in retinol activity equivalents (RAE). For adults this is typically 700 µg RAE for women and 750 µg RAE for men, with increased needs during lactation. Vitamin A appears in two main dietary forms: preformed vitamin A (retinol and retinyl esters) from animal foods, and provitamin A carotenoids (e.g., beta‑carotene) from plant foods, which the body converts to retinol. A balanced diet including dairy, eggs, liver (used sparingly), and colorful vegetables usually meets requirements without supplements.

## Risks of daily supplementation

Because vitamin A is fat soluble it accumulates in the liver and adipose tissue. Chronic intakes of preformed vitamin A above the tolerable upper intake level (3,000 µg/day) increase the risk of hypervitaminosis A. Symptoms include nausea, headaches, dizziness, skin changes and, with prolonged excess, liver abnormalities and bone loss. Acute toxicity is rare and requires very large single doses.

High-dose retinol supplements are contraindicated in pregnancy because of teratogenic risk. People with liver disease, those taking retinoid medications, and anyone using multiple fortified products should consult a clinician before starting daily vitamin A.

## Practical guidance for safe daily use

- Prefer meeting needs through a varied diet; use supplements to correct low intake or malabsorption.  
- Distinguish between preformed vitamin A and beta‑carotene on labels; excess beta‑carotene does not cause retinoid teratogenicity but may have other risks in certain populations (e.g., smokers).  
- Aim to remain within recommended intakes for your age/sex; avoid combining several high‑dose products.  
- If prescribed higher doses, monitor liver function and follow medical advice.

## Who may benefit from daily supplementation?

Individuals with diagnosed deficiency, poor dietary intake (especially restricted diets), malabsorption disorders, or populations targeted by public‑health fortification programs may benefit from controlled supplementation. Children, pregnant or lactating women and older adults require age‑appropriate dosing and clinical oversight when doses approach upper limits.

## Conclusion

Daily vitamin A can be safe and useful when doses are appropriate and tailored to individual needs. Balance, awareness of form (retinol vs beta‑carotene), and attention to cumulative intake from foods and supplements are key to preventing both deficiency and toxicity. When in doubt, consult a healthcare professional for personalized assessment and monitoring.
