Eggs are a modest but meaningful dietary source of vitamin A, concentrated in the yolk and present primarily as preformed retinol and supporting carotenoids. According to USDA data and published analyses, a typical large egg yolk contains roughly 64–75 µg retinol (about 7–9% of an adult’s daily requirement), making eggs valuable for steady intake though not a primary therapeutic source.

Eggs provide vitamin A in two useful forms: retinol (preformed vitamin A) and carotenoids such as lutein and zeaxanthin. Retinol requires no conversion and is readily used by the body, while carotenoids contribute to eye health and may convert to active vitamin A when needed. The fat-rich yolk enhances absorption, so vitamin A from eggs is highly bioavailable compared with some plant sources.

Several variables affect vitamin A levels in eggs. Hen diet is the main determinant: birds with access to carotenoid-rich plants or feed supplemented with retinyl esters produce yolks with higher retinol content. Research has shown pasture-raised and some organic eggs can contain substantially more vitamin A and carotenoids than conventional cage-raised eggs. Enriched eggs—made by altering feed—can further increase retinol content, sometimes by 50% or more. Cooking has a modest impact on yolk vitamin A. Heat can reduce retinol and carotenoid levels by roughly 10–20% depending on method and duration, so gentler cooking (soft boiling, poaching) tends to preserve more nutrients than high-temperature frying. Even after cooking losses, eggs remain nutrient-dense because fat-soluble vitamins are relatively stable when lipids are present.

Compared with supplements and fortified foods, eggs deliver lower absolute quantities of vitamin A but offer advantages in bioavailability and nutrient synergy. Supplements provide precise, often high doses useful for correcting deficiencies; an egg supplies about 60–75 µg RAE versus supplements that may deliver milligram-level retinol in therapeutic contexts. Practical implications: including eggs regularly supports vitamin A intake as part of a varied diet. For most adults, consuming one egg per day is consistent with nutritional guidelines and provides useful retinol alongside protein, choline, vitamin D and selenium. However, eggs should not replace clinically indicated high-dose supplementation in deficiency or medical treatment plans.
