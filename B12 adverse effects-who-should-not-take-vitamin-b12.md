## Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for neurological function, red blood cell production, and DNA synthesis. It is abundant in animal foods, and supplements are commonly used by people with dietary restrictions or absorption issues. While deficiency has clear risks, supplementation is not universally benign. This article summarizes situations and populations who should avoid or approach B12 supplementation cautiously, based on clinical considerations and known interactions.

## When to Avoid or Use Caution

- Known allergy to cobalamin or cobalt: True allergic reactions to vitamin B12 are uncommon but documented. If there is a history of immediate hypersensitivity—hives, throat swelling, breathing difficulty—supplementation should be avoided until allergy testing or specialist advice is obtained.

- Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON): Some reports suggest that certain forms of B12, particularly cyanocobalamin, may worsen optic nerve injury in LHON. Individuals with this genetic condition should consult a geneticist or ophthalmologist before taking B12.

- Myeloproliferative disorders (e.g., polycythemia vera): Because B12 can stimulate erythropoiesis, patients with disorders involving abnormal increases in red blood cells should have specialist oversight before supplementation.

- Severe chronic kidney disease and some malignancies: Observational data have linked high serum B12 to poorer outcomes in certain chronic diseases. This does not prove causation, but it does support individualized assessment and monitoring in these populations.

## Allergies and Adverse Reactions

Allergies to the vitamin itself are distinct from reactions to excipients in formulations. Symptoms of true allergy include sudden itching, swelling, respiratory distress, or anaphylaxis—events that require immediate medical attention. Milder effects like gastrointestinal upset, transient rash, or headache are more often tolerability issues rather than IgE-mediated allergy.

If an adverse reaction occurs, discontinue the product and seek medical evaluation. Allergy testing or supervised challenge may clarify whether the active ingredient or an additive is responsible.

## Drug and Condition Interactions

Several drugs interfere with B12 absorption or function: metformin, proton pump inhibitors, H2 blockers, certain anticonvulsants, and nitrous oxide (which can inactivate B12). Gastrointestinal conditions that impair absorption—pernicious anemia, celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, or post-gastric surgery states—often require alternative administration routes such as intramuscular or high-dose sublingual forms under medical guidance.

## Special Populations

- Pregnancy and breastfeeding: B12 is important for fetal and neonatal neural development, but doses should align with obstetric guidance. High-dose injectable regimens are generally reserved for documented deficiency.

- Older adults: Age-related hypochlorhydria reduces food-derived B12 absorption. Monitoring and individualized dosing are appropriate rather than routine megadoses.

- Athletes and high-dose users: Chronic excessive supplementation is unnecessary for most and may mask underlying conditions or create unintended metabolic effects.

## Practical Recommendations

Testing serum B12 (and functional markers like methylmalonic acid when indicated) helps determine need and proper form. Use injectable or methylated forms when malabsorption is present, and review medications that affect B12 status.
