## Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential water-soluble nutrient with antioxidant properties that support collagen synthesis, iron absorption and immune cell function. Humans cannot synthesize vitamin C endogenously, so dietary intake is required to prevent deficiency conditions such as scurvy. Whole foods provide ascorbic acid alongside fiber, polyphenols and other micronutrients that can influence absorption and biological effects.

For a practical overview compiled for consumers, see [Which food is rich in vitamin C?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/which-food-is-rich-in-vitamin-c).

## Top food sources (evidence-based highlights)

- Guava: One of the richest common fruits, guava can provide well over 200 mg of vitamin C per 100 g in many varieties, exceeding typical citrus fruits.
- Red bell peppers: Raw red peppers often supply 150–200 mg per cup (chopped), making them a top vegetable source.
- Kiwi and strawberries: These fruits typically provide 50–100 mg per serving and deliver complementary antioxidants and fiber.
- Broccoli, Brussels sprouts and kale: Cruciferous and leafy greens supply vitamin C (often 50–90 mg per 100 g or serving) along with phytochemicals such as sulforaphane and carotenoids.

These foods not only supply vitamin C but also a matrix of nutrients that can enhance its effectiveness in human physiology.

## Bioavailability and dosing considerations

Dietary vitamin C is well absorbed at moderate intakes (roughly 30–180 mg/day), with fractional absorption decreasing at higher doses. Studies show absorption efficiency declines as intake increases; large supplemental doses (e.g., >1,000 mg) result in proportionally greater urinary excretion. Therefore, distributing vitamin C-rich foods across meals is a physiologically sensible approach to maintain steady plasma levels.

Cooking and storage affect vitamin C retention because the vitamin is water-soluble and heat-labile. Short steaming, raw consumption, or adding citrus to meals preserves more ascorbic acid than prolonged boiling.

## Role in immune function and antioxidant protection

Controlled trials and observational research indicate vitamin C supports several immune processes: it accumulates in leukocytes, contributes to barrier integrity, and participates in reactive oxygen species scavenging. While supplementation does not guarantee prevention of infection, adequate vitamin C status is associated with shorter duration and reduced severity of some respiratory infections in vulnerable populations.

Combining vitamin C–rich foods with other antioxidant sources—such as berries or dark leafy greens—can provide synergistic effects because vitamin C helps regenerate other antioxidants like vitamin E.

## Conclusion

Vitamin C-rich foods are widely available and include both familiar citrus and higher-density non-citrus options. Prioritizing whole fruits and vegetables delivers vitamin C in a nutrient-rich context that supports absorption and broader health benefits.
