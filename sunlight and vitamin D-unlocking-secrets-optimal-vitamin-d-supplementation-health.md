Introduction

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble nutrient essential to bone integrity, immune function, muscle health, and inflammatory regulation. Modern lifestyles, geographic factors, and seasonal variation contribute to widespread insufficiency, prompting many to consider supplementation. Absorption and Bioavailability

Dietary and supplemental vitamin D require fat for effective intestinal absorption. After ingestion, vitamin D is incorporated into micelles and transported across the intestinal lining, then bound to carrier proteins in plasma for activation or storage. Age, gut health, and certain medications can reduce absorption efficiency. Cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) is generally more effective at raising serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D than ergocalciferol (D2). Taking supplements with a meal containing healthy fats (for example, avocado, nuts, or olive oil) improves uptake.

Sunlight Contributions and Limitations

Cutaneous synthesis from UVB exposure remains the most efficient natural source of vitamin D. Factors that influence production include latitude, season, time of day, skin pigmentation, and sunscreen use. Short, regular exposures of uncovered arms and face (commonly 10–30 minutes several times weekly) can be adequate for many, but individual needs vary. People with darker skin, older adults, or those living at higher latitudes often require supplemental vitamin D, especially in winter months.

Recognizing Deficiency

Symptoms of insufficient vitamin D can be nonspecific: fatigue, muscle weakness, bone pain, and mood changes. Laboratory assessment via serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D is the standard for diagnosis: levels below 20 ng/mL are commonly classified as deficient, 20–30 ng/mL as insufficient, and above 30 ng/mL as sufficient for most individuals. Persistent deficiency is associated with reduced bone mineral density and increased fracture risk, among other health concerns.

Dosage Principles and Monitoring

Recommended intakes vary by age and condition. General public guidance often cites 600 IU/day for adults, with higher needs for older individuals; upper tolerable intake is typically 4,000 IU/day for adults unless supervised clinically. Clinically corrected regimens for deficiency may use higher short-term doses, but individualized dosing and periodic monitoring of serum levels and calcium are advised to avoid hypercalcemia and other adverse effects.

Interactions and Considerations

Several medications (e.g., anticonvulsants, glucocorticoids) and conditions affecting the gut or liver can alter vitamin D metabolism. Nutrients such as calcium and magnesium interact synergistically with vitamin D but should be balanced to avoid complications like hypercalcemia or nephrolithiasis. Lifestyle factors that impair liver function may reduce vitamin D conversion.
