# Introduction

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of enzymatic reactions, supporting energy production, muscle and nerve function, bone integrity, and cardiovascular regulation. Insufficient intake or increased losses can lead to a spectrum of signs—from mild fatigue to clinically relevant electrolyte disturbances. This article summarizes who is most likely to need additional magnesium, common causes and signs of deficiency, and practical considerations for assessment and supplementation.

For a concise overview, see [Who needs magnesium the most?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/who-needs-magnesium-the-most)

## Why deficiency is common

Modern diets that are high in processed foods and low in whole grains, nuts, seeds, and leafy greens often provide inadequate magnesium. Agricultural soil depletion and certain food processing methods further reduce magnesium content in the food supply. In addition, some health conditions and medications increase magnesium losses or reduce absorption, creating populations with elevated risk.

## Key signs and clinical features

Common manifestations of magnesium insufficiency include muscle cramps and spasms, generalized fatigue, sleep disturbances, mood changes (including anxiety and irritability), numbness or tingling, and cardiac rhythm irregularities. Headaches and increased migraine frequency have also been associated with low magnesium status in some studies. Because serum magnesium represents a small fraction of total body magnesium, mild deficiencies may be missed on routine blood tests; targeted testing or assessment of red blood cell magnesium can be informative in selected cases.

## Populations most at risk

- Athletes and physically active people: Increased losses through sweat and urine plus higher metabolic demands can raise magnesium requirements, especially in endurance sports.
- Pregnant and lactating women: Fetal growth and milk production increase micronutrient demand, and magnesium supports fetal bone and muscle development.
- Older adults: Absorption may decrease with age, and polypharmacy can increase excretion or interfere with intake.
- People with gastrointestinal disorders: Conditions like Crohn’s disease, celiac disease, chronic diarrhea, or post-surgical malabsorption reduce magnesium uptake.
- Individuals with metabolic conditions: Type 2 diabetes and insulin resistance are associated with increased urinary magnesium losses.
- Long-term medication users: Proton pump inhibitors, certain diuretics, and some antibiotics are linked to reduced magnesium levels.
- People under chronic stress: Stress-related hormonal changes can increase magnesium turnover and deplete stores.

## Assessment and evidence-based management

Assessment should begin with a clinical review of symptoms, dietary intake, medications, and medical history. Basic serum magnesium testing is a reasonable starting point but has limitations; consider more specialized testing if clinical suspicion is high. Dietary strategies emphasize magnesium-rich foods such as leafy greens, legumes, nuts, seeds, and whole grains.

When dietary measures are insufficient or when malabsorption/medication-related loss is present, supplementation may be appropriate. Clinical trials support magnesium supplementation for prevention and reduction of migraine frequency in some patients and for symptomatic relief of cramps in certain groups. Choice of formulation (e.g., magnesium citrate, oxide, bisglycinate) can affect absorption and tolerability.

## Takeaway

Certain groups—athletes, pregnant or lactating women, older adults, people with GI or metabolic disorders, those on specific medications, and individuals under chronic stress—have higher risk of magnesium insufficiency. Recognition of symptoms, attention to dietary intake, and appropriate testing are the first steps toward evidence-based management.
