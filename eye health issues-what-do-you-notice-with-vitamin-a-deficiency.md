Introduction

Vitamin A is an essential fat‑soluble nutrient that supports vision, epithelial integrity, cellular differentiation, and immune competence. Insufficient intake or impaired absorption can produce a range of subtle to severe signs. This short review summarizes common clinical and functional symptoms, how they relate to physiology, and where to look for further reading. For a detailed treatment specific to supplementation and health implications, see [Topvitamine's article on vitamin A deficiency](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-do-you-notice-with-vitamin-a-deficiency).

Early and non‑specific symptoms

Mild vitamin A insufficiency often begins with non‑specific complaints such as persistent fatigue, generalized weakness, dry or rough skin, and slowed wound healing. These occur because retinoic acid (the active metabolite of vitamin A) influences gene expression involved in mitochondrial function, keratinocyte turnover, and immune cell differentiation. Recurrent upper respiratory or gastrointestinal infections may also appear as mucosal barriers and mucous secretions decline.

Vision‑related signs

The most characteristic early symptom is night blindness (nyctalopia) — difficulty seeing in low light or adapting from bright to dim environments. Vitamin A is a precursor for retinal, a component of rhodopsin in rod photoreceptors; inadequate supply impairs scotopic (low‑light) vision. Progressive deficiency can lead to xerophthalmia (conjunctival and corneal dryness), Bitot’s spots (foamy conjunctival plaques), corneal ulceration, and in severe cases keratomalacia and irreversible visual loss.

Immune system impact

Historically called the “anti‑infective vitamin,” vitamin A modulates innate and adaptive immunity. It supports mucosal barrier integrity and influences T‑ and B‑cell maturation, antibody responses, and the balance of inflammatory pathways. Deficiency is associated with increased susceptibility to respiratory and diarrheal illnesses and can prolong recovery from infections.

Context of other nutritional deficits

Vitamin A deficiency rarely occurs in isolation. Zinc, protein, and iron status are closely interrelated: zinc is required for conversion of provitamin A carotenoids to active retinoids, adequate protein supports retinol‑binding protein synthesis for transport, and vitamin A influences iron metabolism. Therefore, assessment and management should consider the wider micronutrient context rather than focusing on retinol alone.

When to investigate and how to act

Clinicians often evaluate suspected deficiency through dietary assessment, clinical signs (especially ocular findings), and, when indicated, serum retinol or retinol‑binding protein measurements. Management depends on severity: dietary correction and targeted supplementation can reverse early signs, but advanced corneal damage may be irreversible. Avoiding excessive supplemental intake is also important; chronic hypervitaminosis A carries distinct risks.

Conclusion

Recognition of vitamin A deficiency requires attention to vision changes, recurrent infections, dermatologic signs, and poor wound healing. Evaluation should be holistic, acknowledging interactions with zinc, protein, and iron. Early detection and appropriately dosed nutritional interventions can prevent progression to permanent ocular damage and support immune and epithelial health.
