## Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is an essential water-soluble vitamin required for red blood cell formation, nervous system integrity, and DNA synthesis. Many people—older adults, vegans, those with gastrointestinal disorders, and individuals taking certain long-term medications—are at risk of inadequate intake or absorption. Deficiency can progress insidiously, producing symptoms that range from mild fatigue to severe and potentially irreversible neurological damage.

## Neurological and nerve-related effects

Cobalamin is critical for myelin production and for normal neuronal function. Early neurological signs include paresthesia (tingling or numbness), poor coordination, and balance difficulties. Prolonged deficiency can produce demyelination of peripheral nerves and the spinal cord, sometimes manifesting as subacute combined degeneration with weakness, spasticity, and gait disturbance.

Timely supplementation—particularly with bioavailable forms such as methylcobalamin—can halt progression and often reverse early changes. Diagnosis is aided by measuring serum B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA), and clinical neurological assessment.

## Hematological consequences: megaloblastic anemia

B12 is indispensable for DNA synthesis during erythropoiesis. Deficiency typically causes megaloblastic anemia, characterized by large, immature red blood cells and reduced oxygen-carrying capacity. Symptoms include persistent fatigue, pallor, shortness of breath, tachycardia, and cognitive fog.

Laboratory evaluation (CBC, mean corpuscular volume, and serum B12 levels) distinguishes B12-deficiency anemia from iron-deficiency anemia. Appropriate replacement therapy generally yields hematological improvement within weeks, though neurological recovery may require longer treatment.

## Metabolic and systemic implications

Cobalamin serves as a cofactor in methylation cycles and in the conversion of homocysteine to methionine. Low B12 often elevates homocysteine, a marker associated with increased cardiovascular risk and adverse effects on cognitive function and bone health. Impaired mitochondrial energy production linked to B12 deficiency can produce generalized weakness and exercise intolerance.

Clinical trials and meta-analyses indicate that B12 supplementation, frequently combined with folate and B6, can reduce homocysteine and support metabolic recovery.

## Cognitive decline and mood changes

Epidemiological and clinical studies have associated low B12 with memory impairment, slower cognitive processing, depression, and an increased risk of dementia in older adults. Mechanisms involve disrupted methylation, altered neurotransmitter synthesis, and vascular effects related to elevated homocysteine.

Early recognition and correction of deficiency may stabilize or improve certain cognitive symptoms, particularly when started before advanced neurodegeneration.

## Conclusion

Vitamin B12 deficiency has multifaceted consequences—hematological, neurological, metabolic, and cognitive. Early detection and tailored supplementation strategies reduce risks and improve outcomes. Clinical assessment and appropriate laboratory testing remain the foundation for safe and effective management.
