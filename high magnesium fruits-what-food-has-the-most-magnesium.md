# What Food Has the Most Magnesium?

## Quick summary
Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in over 300 enzymatic reactions, including energy production, muscle and nerve function, and bone health. Whole foods provide the most bioavailable magnesium; top examples include seeds, dark leafy greens, nuts, legumes, and some vegetables and fruits. For a focused overview, see [what food has the most magnesium](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-food-has-the-most-magnesium).

## Top natural sources
- Seeds: Pumpkin seeds (pepitas) are among the richest sources, often exceeding 500 mg per 100 g. Sunflower and flax seeds also contribute meaningfully. 
- Leafy greens: Cooked spinach and Swiss chard provide high magnesium per serving and supply complementary nutrients such as iron and vitamin K.
- Nuts: Almonds, cashews, and Brazil nuts are dense sources of magnesium and healthy fats.
- Legumes and whole grains: Black beans, lentils, quinoa, and brown rice support steady magnesium intake while adding fiber and plant protein.
- Fruits and vegetables: Avocado and bananas contribute moderate amounts; vegetables like broccoli, zucchini, and okra add consistent magnesium when included regularly.

These foods generally supply magnesium in forms that are well integrated into dietary patterns, and absorption tends to be better when paired with a balanced intake of protein and healthy fats.

## Vegetarians, vegans, and bioavailability
Dark leafy greens and legumes are particularly valuable for plant-based diets. While phytates and oxalates in some plant foods can reduce mineral absorption, preparation techniques—such as soaking, fermenting, or lightly steaming—help improve bioavailability. Combining magnesium-rich foods across meals (for example, spinach with quinoa and seeds) supports better overall uptake.

## Fortified foods and supplementation
When dietary intake is insufficient or physiological needs are higher (e.g., intense training, certain medical conditions), fortified foods and supplements can be useful. Fortified cereals, plant milks, and electrolyte formulations often list the magnesium form on the label; citrate, glycinate (bisglycinate), and malate are typically better tolerated and more bioavailable than oxide forms. ## Practical tips
- Prioritize whole-food sources: seeds, greens, nuts, legumes, and whole grains.
- Use cooking methods that retain minerals (steaming or roasting rather than prolonged boiling).
- Spread magnesium-rich foods throughout the day to support steady absorption.
- If symptoms suggest deficiency—muscle cramps, fatigue, or sleep disturbance—seek medical evaluation; laboratory testing can confirm low magnesium and inform treatment. ## Evidence-based conclusion
Dietary magnesium is most concentrated in seeds, leafy greens, nuts, legumes, and some fruits and vegetables. A varied whole-food approach typically meets needs for most adults; targeted supplementation can be considered under professional guidance when dietary sources are limited or requirements are elevated.
