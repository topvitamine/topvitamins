Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, myelin maintenance and DNA synthesis. Deficiency can present subtly and progress over months to years, producing a mix of hematological, neurological and cognitive symptoms. Early recognition and testing help prevent potentially irreversible nerve damage.

Common symptoms

- Fatigue and weakness: Impaired red blood cell production reduces oxygen delivery, causing persistent tiredness, shortness of breath and reduced exercise tolerance. This anemia-related fatigue often does not improve with rest.
- Neurological complaints: Peripheral neuropathy (tingling, numbness, 'pins and needles'), balance problems and muscle weakness reflect myelin dysfunction. If untreated, these changes can become long-lasting.
- Cognitive and mood changes: Memory lapses, poor concentration, slowed thinking, irritability or depressive symptoms may appear and sometimes mimic other causes of cognitive decline.
- Glossitis and oral symptoms: A smooth, swollen or sore tongue, mouth ulcers or a metallic taste are visible clues to deficiency.
- Pernicious anemia and systemic signs: An autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor leads to macrocytic anemia with pallor, rapid heart rate and shortness of breath; this form typically requires long-term management.

Less common findings include visual disturbances, tinnitus, gastrointestinal complaints (constipation or diarrhea), light-headedness and reproductive concerns in women of childbearing age.

Who is at risk

Those most prone to low B12 include strict vegans and vegetarians, older adults (reduced gastric acid and intrinsic factor), people with malabsorption conditions (e.g., celiac disease, Crohn’s disease), and those on medications that impair absorption (metformin, proton pump inhibitors). New or worsening neurological or cognitive symptoms in these groups warrant testing.

Diagnosis and testing

Evaluation usually includes a complete blood count and serum B12. Because serum levels can be misleading, clinicians often measure methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine to detect tissue-level deficiency. Antibody testing helps identify pernicious anemia. Timely laboratory assessment guides appropriate replacement strategies.

Supplementation options

Therapeutic approaches vary with severity and cause. Intramuscular injections are commonly used for marked deficiency or absorption failure; oral and sublingual formulations are effective for many individuals. Typical maintenance needs are around 2.4 mcg/day for adults, while therapeutic dosing can be substantially higher under medical supervision. For guidance on dosing decisions, see <a href='https://topvitamine.github.io/topvitamins/how-to-determine-right-dosage-nutritional-supplements'>how to determine the right dosage of nutritional supplements</a>.

Clinical course and prognosis

Mild hematological symptoms often improve within days to weeks of supplementation; neurological recovery may take months and can be incomplete if treatment is delayed. Regular monitoring of symptoms and laboratory markers is important, particularly for people with ongoing absorption issues.

Summary

Vitamin B12 deficiency produces a recognizable pattern—fatigue, neuropathy, cognitive changes and oral signs. Identifying at-risk individuals, ordering appropriate tests and initiating timely supplementation are the key steps to prevent long-term complications.
