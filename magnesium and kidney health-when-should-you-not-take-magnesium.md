**Introduction**

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of enzymatic reactions, supporting muscle and nerve function, bone health, and cardiac rhythm. While dietary magnesium is safe for most people, supplemental magnesium carries specific risks and contraindications. This article summarizes situations in which magnesium supplementation should be avoided or undertaken only with medical supervision.

**When magnesium is contraindicated**

- Impaired kidney function: The kidneys excrete excess magnesium. In chronic kidney disease (CKD), especially stages 3–5, magnesium can accumulate and cause hypermagnesemia. A glomerular filtration rate (GFR) below ~60 mL/min/1.73 m² suggests the need for careful evaluation before supplementing. Routine supplementation without assessment may lead to weakness, hypotension, and cardiac abnormalities.

- Known hypersensitivity: Though rare, allergic reactions to magnesium salts can occur and present with rash, swelling, or respiratory symptoms requiring immediate care.

- Cardiac and neuromuscular conditions: People with heart block or those receiving neuromuscular blockers should avoid unsupervised magnesium because it can act as a calcium channel blocker and worsen conduction abnormalities or muscle weakness.

- Gastrointestinal disorders affecting absorption: Conditions such as Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, or significant malabsorption can produce unpredictable magnesium levels and responses to oral supplements.

**Drug interactions and timing**

Magnesium binds to and can reduce the absorption of certain oral medications. Notable examples include tetracycline and fluoroquinolone antibiotics and bisphosphonates used for osteoporosis. To reduce interaction risk, separate doses by at least 2–4 hours when concurrent use is medically required. Magnesium may also enhance the effects of sedatives or interact with blood pressure medications; close medical review is recommended whenever multiple agents are used.

**Side effects and overdose risk**

Common side effects of oral magnesium are gastrointestinal—diarrhea, abdominal cramping, and nausea—especially with forms like magnesium oxide or sulfate that have laxative effects. Excessive intake can progress to systemic toxicity: drowsiness, slowed reflexes, hypotension, respiratory depression, and arrhythmia. Overdose risk rises with high-dose supplements, chronic use of antacids or laxatives containing magnesium, and reduced renal clearance.

**Pregnancy considerations**

Pregnancy increases demand for many minerals, but supplementation should follow prenatal care guidance. Typical recommended intakes for pregnant adults are in the range of dietary reference values (about 350–360 mg/day), and higher supplemental doses should only be used under supervision. Intravenous high-dose magnesium is used clinically in obstetrics (e.g., to manage eclampsia) but requires hospital monitoring because of risks to both mother and newborn, including neonatal hypotonia if excess exposure occurs.

**Practical guidance**

Before starting magnesium supplements, review your medical history, current medications, and kidney function tests with a healthcare professional. If you are taking antibiotics, bone drugs, diuretics, or sedatives, discuss timing and dose adjustments. If you have CKD or unexplained cardiovascular or neuromuscular symptoms, avoid self-supplementation.

**Conclusion**

Magnesium supplementation can be beneficial but is not universally safe. Key situations to avoid or carefully manage supplementation include impaired kidney function, certain cardiac and neuromuscular disorders, potential drug interactions, and pregnancy without clinical oversight. Professional assessment and monitoring ensure supplementation supports health without introducing preventable risks.
