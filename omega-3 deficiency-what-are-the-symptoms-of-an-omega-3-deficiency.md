## Introduction

Omega-3 fatty acids, primarily EPA and DHA, are essential components of cell membranes and have important roles in inflammation regulation, cardiovascular function, and neural health. Because the body cannot synthesize sufficient amounts of these long-chain polyunsaturated fats, dietary intake or supplementation is necessary. Deficiency can be subtle at first and may present with both subjective symptoms and measurable clinical markers.

## Early signs and neurocognitive symptoms

One of the earliest and most commonly reported clusters of symptoms involves cognition and mood. People with low omega-3 status often describe mental fog, difficulty concentrating, and reduced mental clarity. Epidemiological and clinical data also link lower EPA/DHA levels with higher rates of depressive symptoms and mood instability. Fatigue that is not explained by sleep disturbance or other medical conditions can also be associated with inadequate omega-3 intake, potentially reflecting effects on cellular energy metabolism.

## Dermatologic, hair, and immune signs

Because omega-3s help maintain skin barrier function and modulate inflammation, deficiency may present as dry, flaky, or sensitive skin and slower wound healing. Thinning or brittle hair has also been reported anecdotally and in small studies. Immune alterations—such as more frequent or prolonged minor infections—may reflect impaired regulation of inflammatory responses when omega-3 levels are low.

## Musculoskeletal and ocular symptoms

Joint stiffness and achiness can occur when anti-inflammatory omega-3 pathways are underrepresented, particularly in people with underlying inflammatory conditions. Eye symptoms, including dry eyes or intermittent blurred vision, can arise because DHA is a key structural lipid in retinal tissue; inadequate intake may compromise ocular surface lubrication and retinal resilience.

## Biochemical indicators and cardiovascular markers

Objective laboratory findings that can accompany an omega-3 deficit include elevated triglycerides, altered HDL/LDL balance, and higher inflammatory biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) or interleukin-6 (IL-6). Mildly raised blood pressure has also been associated with lower omega-3 intake in some population studies. These markers are not specific to omega-3 status, but when combined with clinical features they can prompt further nutritional assessment.

## Long-term consequences

Sustained low intake of EPA and DHA has been associated with increased long-term risks for cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, and certain chronic inflammatory disorders. In pregnancy, inadequate maternal DHA is linked to suboptimal fetal neurodevelopment outcomes. These associations are complex and influenced by overall diet, genetics, and lifestyle, but they underscore the importance of addressing persistent insufficiency.

## Assessment and practical considerations

Assessment typically combines dietary history with clinical signs and, when indicated, blood testing for omega-3 index or lipid profile markers. Reliable dietary sources include fatty fish and certain algal products for those following plant-based diets.
