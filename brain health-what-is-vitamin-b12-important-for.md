Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is an essential water-soluble vitamin required for multiple physiological processes, including neurological function, red blood cell formation, and energy metabolism. Humans cannot synthesize B12 and must obtain it from dietary sources or supplements. Deficiency can lead to fatigue, cognitive changes, neuropathy, and megaloblastic anemia, so understanding its roles helps in prevention and timely treatment.

Introduction

Vitamin B12 participates in DNA synthesis and methylation reactions and is a cofactor for enzymes that metabolize homocysteine and support cellular energy pathways. In the nervous system, B12 is necessary for myelin maintenance; in hematopoiesis, it supports proper red blood cell development. Because absorption requires intrinsic factor and healthy gastrointestinal function, people with gastric disorders, those on certain medications (e.g., proton pump inhibitors, metformin), older adults, and strict vegetarians or vegans are at higher risk of deficiency.

Brain and cognitive health

B12 contributes to myelin synthesis and neurotransmitter production, which underpins memory, attention, and mood regulation. Elevated homocysteine—often a biomarker of insufficient B12—has been associated with cognitive decline in observational studies. Supplementation that corrects deficiency can improve mental clarity and mood in affected individuals; however, evidence is strongest for correction of clinical deficiency rather than routine prevention of age-related cognitive decline in already-replete populations.

Nerve function

Adequate B12 preserves peripheral and central nervous system integrity by maintaining myelin and supporting neuronal repair mechanisms. Clinical presentations of deficiency may include paresthesia, numbness, balance disturbances, and gait instability. Some neuropathic symptoms are reversible with prompt treatment, whereas prolonged deficiency can produce lasting nerve damage.

Red blood cell production and anemia

B12 is integral to erythropoiesis; deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia characterized by large, immature red cells and impaired oxygen delivery. Symptoms include fatigue, pallor, shortness of breath, and tachycardia. Diagnosis relies on serum B12, methylmalonic acid, and mean corpuscular volume (MCV) measurements. Treatment options depend on severity and absorption capacity and range from oral high-dose supplementation to parenteral administration in cases of malabsorption.

Practical considerations

Forms of supplemental B12 commonly include cyanocobalamin, methylcobalamin, and hydroxocobalamin. Methylcobalamin is often highlighted for its bioactivity in neurological pathways, while cyanocobalamin is stable and widely used in research. Choice of form and route should be individualized based on clinical context and laboratory results. Testing and monitoring

When symptoms or risk factors are present, clinicians typically evaluate serum B12, methylmalonic acid, and homocysteine. Correcting deficiencies is evidence-based and can prevent progression to neurologic or hematologic complications. Summary

Vitamin B12 is central to neural integrity, cognition, blood formation, and metabolic health. Identifying at-risk individuals and confirming deficiency with laboratory testing enables targeted, evidence-based interventions that reduce the risk of irreversible outcomes.
