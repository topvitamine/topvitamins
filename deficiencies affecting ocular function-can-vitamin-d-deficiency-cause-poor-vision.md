## Can a Vitamin D Deficiency Cause Poor Vision?

### Introduction

Emerging research has examined links between low vitamin D status and a range of ocular problems. Vitamin D is a fat-soluble nutrient with hormone-like properties that influence inflammation, vascular function, and immune responses—processes relevant to eye health. This concise review summarizes current evidence about whether insufficient vitamin D can contribute to visual symptoms and what corrective steps are reasonable based on available data.

### Why vitamin D matters to the eye

Vitamin D receptors have been identified in ocular tissues including the retina and cornea, suggesting biologically plausible roles in local cellular regulation. Observational studies report associations between lower serum 25(OH)D concentrations and higher prevalence of conditions such as dry eye syndrome, age-related macular degeneration (AMD), and diabetic retinopathy. Mechanisms proposed in the literature include modulation of inflammation, preservation of retinal microvasculature, and regulation of calcium signaling in retinal cells—each of which can indirectly affect visual function.

### Symptoms and clinical observations

Reported ocular symptoms potentially linked to low vitamin D include increased light sensitivity, dry or irritated eyes, eye fatigue, and blurred vision—especially under low-light conditions. Dry eye has the most robust observational link: several case-control studies have found lower mean vitamin D levels among patients with clinically diagnosed dry eye compared with controls. However, association does not equal causation; confounding factors such as age, comorbidities, and outdoor activity patterns can influence both vitamin D status and eye symptoms.

### Evidence on supplementation and outcomes

Interventional data are limited but suggest modest benefits for some symptoms. Small trials and case reports indicate that vitamin D repletion in deficient individuals can reduce dry eye symptoms and may improve markers of ocular surface health. Evidence is less clear for structural retinal diseases (e.g., advanced AMD); while vitamin D’s anti-inflammatory and vascular effects are mechanistically relevant, longitudinal and randomized studies are required to determine whether supplementation alters disease course.

### Practical considerations

Routine screening for vitamin D deficiency is often considered in high-risk groups (older adults, individuals with limited sun exposure, darker skin, or malabsorption). Standard markers use serum 25(OH)D; many guidelines define sufficiency at or above 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL), though some experts propose higher targets for extra-skeletal benefits. Correction approaches include sensible sunlight exposure, dietary sources, and supplements when indicated. Management decisions should be individualized and supervised by a healthcare professional.

### Conclusion

Current evidence supports an association between low vitamin D and certain ocular symptoms—most convincingly dry eye—but does not prove that deficiency directly causes permanent vision loss. Repleting deficient individuals is reasonable as part of comprehensive care, particularly when ocular symptoms coexist with systemic risk factors. Well-designed clinical trials are needed to clarify the extent to which vitamin D supplementation can prevent or modify specific eye diseases.
