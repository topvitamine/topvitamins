Vitamins are small molecules with large roles: they support metabolism, immune function, bone health, and neurological processes. Detecting a deficiency early can prevent functional decline, but symptoms are often nonspecific and overlap with other conditions. This article outlines common signs, likely causes, and appropriate steps for assessment and management.

## Common signs and what they might mean

- Fatigue and weakness: Persistent, unexplained tiredness can reflect low iron, vitamin B12, or vitamin D. Objective testing is needed because sleep issues and thyroid disease produce similar symptoms.
- Skin, hair, and nail changes: Dry skin, brittle nails, hair thinning, or cracks at the mouth corners can indicate deficits in B vitamins, biotin, or essential minerals.
- Frequent infections or slow wound healing: Recurrent colds or poor tissue repair may point toward vitamin C or D insufficiency and compromised immune support.
- Neurological symptoms: Tingling, numbness, memory problems, or mood changes can arise from low B12, folate, or magnesium levels.
- Muscle cramps and irregular heartbeat: Electrolyte-related nutrients such as magnesium and calcium play a role in muscle and cardiac function.

These signs are clues—not confirmations. A systematic approach helps separate nutritional causes from medical or psychosocial contributors.

## Who is at higher risk

Certain groups have increased likelihood of deficiencies: older adults (reduced absorption and intake), people following restrictive diets (e.g., vegans lacking B12), individuals with malabsorption disorders (celiac disease, Crohn’s), those taking specific medications (metformin, proton pump inhibitors), and people with limited sun exposure who are prone to low vitamin D.

## How deficiencies are diagnosed

Clinical evaluation begins with history and physical exam to contextualize symptoms. Laboratory tests provide objective evidence: for example, serum 25(OH)D for vitamin D status, ferritin and complete blood count for iron deficiency, methylmalonic acid for functional B12 deficiency, and plasma vitamin C when indicated. More comprehensive micronutrient panels or genetic tests may be useful in complex cases. Interpretation should be done by a qualified clinician who can integrate lab values with clinical findings.

## Evidence-based management principles

When a deficiency is confirmed, treatment typically combines dietary adjustments and targeted supplementation tailored to the specific nutrient, dose, and duration. Food-first strategies—emphasizing varied whole foods, fortified items, and appropriate sun exposure for vitamin D—are foundational. Supplements can correct deficits more rapidly but require appropriate formulation and monitoring to avoid excess, especially with fat-soluble vitamins.

Follow-up testing ensures that interventions restore adequate levels and resolve symptoms. In severe cases or when absorption is impaired, alternative delivery methods (e.g., intramuscular B12) may be necessary.
