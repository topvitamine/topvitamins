## Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential, water-soluble nutrient known for antioxidant activity, collagen synthesis, and immune support. When consumed in food amounts it is generally safe; however, supplemental vitamin C—especially in high doses—can produce side effects in some people. This article summarizes common and rare adverse reactions, overdose signs, allergy considerations, and practical guidance for safer use.

## Common side effects

The most frequent complaints with supplemental vitamin C are gastrointestinal: nausea, abdominal cramping, bloating, heartburn and diarrhea. Large single doses draw water into the intestines and stimulate motility, which explains dose-dependent diarrhea. Buffered forms (e.g., calcium ascorbate or sodium ascorbate) are often better tolerated than crystalline ascorbic acid for people with acid sensitivity.

Minor neurologic symptoms such as headache, dizziness or sleep disturbances have been reported anecdotally at high doses; these are uncommon and typically resolve when supplementation is reduced.

## Overdose and toxicity risks

Vitamin C has a relatively low toxicity profile due to urinary excretion, but the adult Tolerable Upper Intake Level is generally set at 2,000 mg/day. Chronic intake well above this level can increase urinary oxalate and, in susceptible individuals, raise the risk of calcium oxalate kidney stones. Excess vitamin C may also worsen iron overload disorders (e.g., hemochromatosis) by enhancing non-heme iron absorption.

Acute overdose can produce severe GI upset, dehydration, and electrolyte shifts. Most adverse effects are reversible once intake is corrected, but people with impaired renal function should use extra caution because oxalate clearance may be reduced.

## Allergies and additive reactions

True allergy to the vitamin molecule is exceptionally rare. More commonly, reactions stem from inactive ingredients in supplements—fillers, colorants, sweeteners or flavorings. Symptoms consistent with allergic response include hives, itching, swelling, respiratory difficulty and, in extremely rare cases, anaphylaxis. Individuals with multiple sensitivities should select hypoallergenic formulations and consult an allergist when reactions occur.

Histamine-related responses may mimic allergic reactions in some people; because vitamin C participates in histamine metabolism, dosing should be individualized for those with histamine intolerance.

## Rare but important considerations

- Hemolytic anemia: People with G6PD deficiency may be at risk when exposed to oxidative stressors; very high vitamin C doses have been implicated in rare cases of hemolysis.
- Laboratory interference: High plasma vitamin C can interfere with certain point-of-care glucose tests and other assays, potentially producing misleading results.
- Dental erosion: Repeated exposure to acidic chewables could affect enamel over time.

## Practical takeaways

Stick to recommended dietary amounts and avoid routine megadoses unless supervised by a clinician. People with kidney disease, iron overload, G6PD deficiency, or those on interacting medications should consult their healthcare provider before starting supplements. If you experience persistent GI symptoms, allergic-type reactions, unexplained fatigue, or changes in urine color, stop supplementation and seek medical advice.

Moderation, label reading, and clinical context make vitamin C supplementation safe and effective for most people.
