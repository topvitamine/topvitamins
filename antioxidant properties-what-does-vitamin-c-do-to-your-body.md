Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water‑soluble micronutrient that performs multiple, well‑documented roles in human physiology. It acts as an antioxidant, a cofactor for enzyme reactions such as collagen synthesis, and a modulator of immune and iron‑metabolism processes. Because the body cannot synthesize or store meaningful amounts of vitamin C, regular dietary intake or supplementation is necessary to maintain physiological function.

At the cellular level, vitamin C donates electrons to neutralize free radicals and regenerate other antioxidants like vitamin E and glutathione. This antioxidant activity reduces oxidative stress, which is implicated in aging and chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease and neurodegeneration. Maintaining adequate vitamin C status supports cellular integrity by protecting DNA, proteins, and lipids from oxidative damage.

One of the most important biochemical roles of vitamin C is as a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases, enzymes that stabilize and cross‑link collagen fibers. Collagen is the primary structural protein in skin, tendons, ligaments, blood vessels, and bone. Sufficient vitamin C improves wound healing, supports joint and vascular elasticity, and contributes to overall tissue repair — functions especially relevant for older adults and physically active individuals.

Vitamin C also supports the immune system through multiple mechanisms. It concentrates in immune cells, where it reduces oxidative damage incurred during microbial killing, supports phagocyte function, and promotes lymphocyte proliferation. Clinical evidence indicates that while routine vitamin C supplementation does not reliably prevent the common cold in the general population, it can reduce the duration and severity of upper respiratory infections in individuals under physical stress or with low baseline levels.

Dietary vitamin C enhances non‑heme iron absorption by reducing ferric iron to the more absorbable ferrous form in the gut. Combining plant‑based iron sources with vitamin C–rich foods or a modest dose of vitamin C can substantially increase iron uptake, a practical strategy for vegetarians, vegans, and those with iron‑deficiency anemia.

Forms and dosing: vitamin C is available as ascorbic acid, buffered formulations (with minerals like calcium or magnesium), and liposomal preparations that aim to improve bioavailability. Typical daily intakes for general maintenance vary by guideline, but many regions recommend around 80–100 mg/day for adults; therapeutic regimens sometimes use higher doses under clinical supervision. Vitamin C is water‑soluble and excess is excreted, but very high intakes may cause gastrointestinal symptoms.

Topical vitamin C (formulated to ensure skin penetration) and oral supplementation can act synergistically for skin health. Systemic vitamin C supports dermal collagen production and cellular antioxidant capacity, while topical applications can target photodamage and pigmentation locally.
