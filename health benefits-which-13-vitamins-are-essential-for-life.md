Introduction

Vitamins are organic micronutrients required in small amounts for growth, maintenance, and repair. The thirteen vitamins generally considered essential for human health include four fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and nine water‑soluble vitamins (the B‑complex: B1, B2, B3, B5, B6, B7, B9, B12; plus vitamin C). For a focused overview, see the detailed guide [which 13 vitamins are essential for life](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/which-13-vitamins-are-essential-for-life).

Fat‑soluble vitamins: A, D, E, K

- Vitamin A supports vision (especially night vision), epithelial integrity, and immune function. Sources: liver, dairy, egg yolks, and provitamin A carotenoids in orange/green produce. Excess preformed vitamin A can be toxic, especially in pregnancy.
- Vitamin D acts hormonally to regulate calcium and phosphate for bone health; it’s produced in skin with UVB exposure and found in fatty fish, fortified foods, and supplements. Risk groups include those with low sun exposure or malabsorption.
- Vitamin E is an antioxidant protecting lipid membranes; found in vegetable oils, nuts, and seeds. Deficiency is rare except in fat‑malabsorption disorders.
- Vitamin K is essential for activation of clotting factors and bone proteins; present in leafy greens and fermented foods. Newborn vitamin K administration is a standard preventive practice in many settings. Water‑soluble vitamins: B‑complex and C

- B vitamins (B1 thiamine, B2 riboflavin, B3 niacin, B5 pantothenic acid, B6 pyridoxine, B7 biotin, B9 folate, B12 cobalamin) largely act as coenzymes in energy metabolism, DNA synthesis, and nervous system function. Deficiencies produce characteristic syndromes: thiamine deficiency can cause beriberi or Wernicke’s encephalopathy; niacin deficiency causes pellagra; folate and B12 deficiencies cause megaloblastic anemia with important implications in pregnancy and neurology. B12 absorption requires intrinsic factor and intact ileum, so older adults and strict vegans are at increased risk—see further discussion on <a href='https://topvitamine.github.io/topvitamins/how-vitamin-b12-supports-skin-health-anti-aging'>Vitamin B12 and skin health</a>.
- Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water‑soluble antioxidant required for collagen synthesis and enhances non‑heme iron absorption. Severe deficiency causes scurvy; dietary sources include citrus, berries, peppers, and cruciferous vegetables.

Food‑first approach and supplementation considerations

A varied diet of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, dairy or fortified alternatives, nuts, and seeds meets most vitamin needs. Targeted supplementation is appropriate when evidence indicates deficiency, increased physiological need (e.g., pregnancy for folic acid), restricted diets, or malabsorption. Routine monitoring, awareness of upper intake limits, and clinician guidance reduce the risk of harm from excess intake. Practical guidance on timing and compatibility of supplements with eating patterns is discussed in resources about supplements and fasting such as <a href='https://topvitamine.github.io/topvitamins/should-i-take-supplements-during-intermittent-fasting'>supplements during intermittent fasting</a> and the related overview <a href='https://telegra.ph/Should-I-Take-Supplements-During-Intermittent-Fasting-for-Better-Results-12-20'>Should I Take Supplements During Intermittent Fasting</a>.

Summary

The thirteen essential vitamins each contribute uniquely to metabolism, tissue integrity, and systemic function. A food‑first strategy, targeted supplementation when indicated, and clinical assessment where risk exists are evidence‑based steps to preserve vitamin sufficiency without unnecessary supplementation.
