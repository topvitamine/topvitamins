# What do you notice with a vitamin B12 deficiency?

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for DNA synthesis, healthy red blood cell production, and nervous system function. Deficiency can develop slowly and present with a mixture of physical, neurological, and cognitive symptoms. Early recognition and testing are important because some effects—particularly neurological damage—may become irreversible if untreated.

## Common signs and symptoms

Physical and hematologic signs

- Persistent fatigue and generalized weakness
- Pale or mildly jaundiced skin due to megaloblastic anemia
- Shortness of breath and heart palpitations on exertion
- A swollen, sore, or 'beefy' tongue

Neurological and cognitive signs

- Paresthesia: pins-and-needles or numbness in hands and feet
- Gait imbalance, poor coordination, or frequent falls
- Memory lapses, slowed thinking, and difficulties with attention
- Mood changes such as depression, irritability, or apathy

Because symptoms overlap with other conditions, clinicians often consider a combination of signs rather than a single finding when evaluating B12 status.

## Why deficiency occurs

Deficiency results from low intake, poor absorption, or increased requirements. Common causes include strict vegetarian/vegan diets without fortified foods or supplements, pernicious anemia (autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor), gastrointestinal disorders (Crohn’s disease, celiac disease), gastric surgery (gastric bypass), and certain medications (long-term proton pump inhibitors or metformin). Older adults are also at higher risk due to decreased stomach acid and intrinsic factor production.

## Diagnostic approach

Evaluation generally starts with a focused clinical history and blood testing. Typical laboratory tests include:

- Serum vitamin B12 concentration (initial screen)
- Methylmalonic acid (MMA) — a sensitive indicator of cellular B12 deficiency
- Homocysteine — elevated in B12 or folate deficiency

When neurological symptoms are present, prompt testing and referral for neurological assessment may be warranted. Monitoring response to treatment with follow-up labs is standard practice.

## Management and prevention

Treatment depends on cause and severity. Options include dietary correction (animal foods, fortified foods), high-dose oral supplementation (often 250–1000 mcg daily for deficiency), and intramuscular injections for cases of malabsorption or severe neurologic involvement. Addressing underlying conditions—such as celiac disease or medication review—is also key. Long-term monitoring ensures levels remain adequate and symptoms resolve.

## Practical notes

- Consider testing if you have unexplained fatigue, neurological symptoms, or risk factors (vegan diet, gastrointestinal disease, certain medications, or older age).
- Oral supplements are effective for many people; injections are used when absorption is impaired or when rapid correction is needed.
- Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment usually prevent progression and can reverse most symptoms.
