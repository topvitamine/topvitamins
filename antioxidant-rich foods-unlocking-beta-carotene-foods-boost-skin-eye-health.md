## Introduction

Beta-carotene is a dietary carotenoid and provitamin A precursor found in many orange, red, and dark-green fruits and vegetables. It supports visual function and epithelial tissue maintenance through conversion to retinol and contributes antioxidant activity that helps neutralize reactive oxygen species. A food-first approach supplies beta-carotene alongside complementary nutrients that improve absorption and biological effect. For an overview of the topic, see [Unlocking Beta-Carotene: Top Foods to Boost Your Skin and Eye Health](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/unlocking-beta-carotene-foods-boost-skin-eye-health).

## How beta-carotene supports skin and eyes

As a provitamin A carotenoid, beta-carotene contributes to vitamin A pools needed for the visual cycle and for healthy epithelial differentiation in skin. Carotenoids also accumulate in lipid-rich tissues, where they can reduce oxidative damage from UV exposure and environmental stressors. Clinical and observational studies indicate that regular intake of carotenoid-rich foods can influence skin tone, photoprotection markers, and macular pigment density, though individual responses depend on dose, matrix, and baseline status.

## Top food sources and practical tips

High-yield foods include carrots, sweet potatoes, spinach, kale, red bell peppers, mangoes, apricots, and cantaloupe. To maximize bioavailability:

- Pair carotenoid-rich foods with a modest amount of dietary fat (olive oil, avocado, nuts).
- Use light cooking (steaming, roasting) to break down plant matrices without excessive heat.
- Combine varied carotenoids (e.g., lutein-rich greens with beta-carotene foods) for broader tissue coverage.

Whole foods also supply vitamin C, vitamin E, zinc, and healthy fats that synergize with carotenoids to support collagen maintenance, antioxidant regeneration, and membrane integrity.

## Supplements: when and how to consider them

Supplements can help people with limited access to fresh produce, restrictive diets, or malabsorption. Formulations that deliver carotenoids in an oil matrix or mixed-carotenoid blends tend to have better absorption and broader tissue distribution. Typical formats include oil-based softgels, powdered mixes, and standardized fortified foods. Pay attention to dose, population context, and evidence: high-dose isolated beta-carotene showed adverse outcomes in some trials among smokers, and excessive preformed vitamin A carries toxicity risk. ## Combining nutrients for broader benefits

A holistic approach pairs beta-carotene with lutein and zeaxanthin for macular support, omega-3 fatty acids (DHA) for retinal membrane health, and antioxidants like vitamin C and E for regeneration of antioxidant networks. Minerals such as zinc support vitamin A metabolism and visual pigment formation.

## Safety and final considerations

Dietary beta-carotene from mixed foods is generally safe; excessive supplemental beta-carotene may cause carotenodermia and has context-specific risks. Pregnant people and those with chronic disease should consult healthcare professionals before starting supplements.
