## Introduction

Maintaining cognitive performance across the lifespan depends on multiple factors, including sleep, exercise, stress management, and nutrition. Among micronutrients, Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) performs several biochemical tasks that directly affect neural integrity, neurotransmitter synthesis, and cellular energy. This article summarizes the mechanisms by which B12 supports brain health and highlights practical considerations for maintaining adequate levels.

## Biological roles relevant to cognition

Vitamin B12 is a cofactor for methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. Through methionine synthase it participates in one-carbon metabolism, supporting DNA methylation and converting homocysteine to methionine; elevated homocysteine has been associated with increased risk of cognitive decline. By enabling proper methylation reactions, B12 influences myelin maintenance and the synthesis of neurotransmitters such as dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine, which modulate attention, mood, and executive function.

B12 also supports mitochondrial function and cellular energy production in neurons. Adequate cobalamin helps maintain axonal integrity and the myelin sheath that speeds electrical conduction—both essential for rapid information processing and memory retrieval.

## Evidence for neuroprotection and cognitive effects

Epidemiological studies link low serum B12, high methylmalonic acid (MMA), or elevated homocysteine with worse cognitive performance and higher rates of cognitive decline in older adults. Randomized controlled trials show mixed but generally favorable results for supplementation in individuals who are deficient or have borderline levels: improvements are most consistent for memory, processing speed, and attention when biochemical deficiency is corrected.

Mechanistically, lowering homocysteine and supporting myelin repair are plausible pathways for observed benefits. In some early-stage neurodegenerative conditions, B12 supplementation has been associated with slower progression of specific symptoms, although it is not a disease-modifying therapy on its own.

## At-risk populations and screening

Groups at greater risk of deficiency include older adults, people with pernicious anemia or gastrointestinal disorders, and those following strict vegetarian or vegan diets without fortified foods or supplements. Certain medications (for example, long-term metformin or proton pump inhibitors) can impair absorption. Screening typically combines serum B12 with functional markers such as MMA or homocysteine to improve diagnostic accuracy.

## Practical considerations for supplementation and diet

Dietary sources (meat, dairy, eggs, shellfish) provide readily bioavailable B12. For those who cannot meet needs from food, supplemental forms such as methylcobalamin or cyanocobalamin can restore levels. Dosing and route (oral vs. intramuscular) depend on cause of deficiency and clinical context; individualized decisions are best made with medical evaluation. Integrating B12 with a broader nutritional strategy—vitamin D, omega-3 fatty acids, and adequate iron and folate—can support overall neural health.

## Conclusion

Vitamin B12 has clear biochemical roles that support myelination, neurotransmitter synthesis, and homocysteine regulation—processes tied to cognitive performance and neural resilience. Detecting and correcting deficiency in at-risk individuals can yield measurable cognitive benefits.
