Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water‑soluble micronutrient with multiple biochemical roles that influence immunity, antioxidant defense, collagen synthesis, and respiratory health. Understanding what happens after you ingest vitamin C clarifies why consistent, moderate intake—rather than intermittent megadoses—is generally recommended.

Absorption and distribution

After oral intake, vitamin C is absorbed in the small intestine via active transport and passive diffusion; absorption efficiency declines with very large single doses. Once absorbed, it circulates in plasma and accumulates within cells (notably leukocytes and the adrenal glands) where it participates in enzymatic reactions and redox processes. The European Food Safety Authority sets a tolerable upper intake level of 2,000 mg/day for adults to reduce the risk of adverse effects such as gastrointestinal upset or, in susceptible people, increased risk of kidney stones.

Antioxidant activity and cellular protection

Vitamin C donates electrons to neutralize reactive oxygen species and regenerate lipid‑soluble antioxidants like vitamin E, forming part of an integrated antioxidant network. This reduces oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA, which has implications for cardiovascular health, mitochondrial function, and age‑related cellular decline. Evidence links higher plasma vitamin C with improved endothelial function and lower markers of oxidative stress in some observational and interventional studies.

Immune modulation

Cells of the innate and adaptive immune systems concentrate vitamin C, which supports barrier integrity, leukocyte function, chemotaxis, and phagocytosis. Regular supplementation has been associated with modest reductions in the duration and severity of common cold episodes in pooled studies, particularly in individuals under physical stress. However, vitamin C is not a guaranteed preventive for respiratory infections; maintaining adequate status is the more realistic goal.

Collagen synthesis and connective tissue

Vitamin C is an essential cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase enzymes, which stabilize collagen triple helices. Adequate intake is therefore critical for wound healing, vascular integrity, and maintaining tendon and cartilage function. Severe deficiency produces scurvy, characterized by impaired collagen formation, bleeding gums, and delayed wound repair. Supplementation in deficient or malnourished populations improves healing outcomes in clinical settings.

Skin and respiratory effects

Through both systemic and topical pathways, vitamin C supports dermal structure by promoting collagen production and reducing photooxidative damage. It also inhibits tyrosinase activity, which can help modulate hyperpigmentation. In the respiratory system, vitamin C helps maintain epithelial function, reduce histamine levels, and protect alveolar cells from oxidative injury; these effects may translate to reduced symptom burden in certain high‑risk groups.

Practical considerations

Balanced intake from fruits and vegetables (citrus, bell peppers, dark leafy greens) combined with supplements if needed is an effective strategy to achieve adequate status. Different formulations (ascorbic acid, buffered salts, liposomal preparations) offer varied tolerability and absorption characteristics.
