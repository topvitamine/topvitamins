Vitamin D, often called the “sunshine vitamin,” is a fat‑soluble nutrient with hormone‑like actions that influence multiple physiological systems. Its best‑established role is supporting skeletal health by enabling efficient calcium and phosphorus metabolism, but research also links vitamin D status with immune function, mood regulation, and other cellular processes. Modern lifestyles, limited sun exposure, and dietary patterns make maintaining adequate levels a common public‑health concern.

The vitamin facilitates intestinal absorption of calcium and contributes to bone mineralization. When intake or synthesis is insufficient, the body compensates by mobilizing calcium from bone, which over time can increase the risk of osteopenia and osteoporosis. Clinical trials and meta‑analyses indicate that vitamin D supplementation, often combined with calcium, can help preserve bone mineral density and reduce fracture risk in older adults.

Vitamin D also interacts with the immune system. Immune cells express vitamin D receptors (VDRs) and can convert inactive vitamin D into its active form locally, influencing innate and adaptive responses. Randomized and observational studies suggest that supplementation may reduce the incidence of acute respiratory infections in people with low baseline levels, although the magnitude of benefit varies by population and dosing regimen.

Beyond bone and immunity, vitamin D is implicated in mood and cognitive health. Observational data link lower serum 25‑hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) concentrations with higher rates of depressive symptoms and seasonal affective disorder. Interventional studies report modest improvements in mood among deficient individuals following repletion; however, vitamin D is not a standalone treatment for clinical depression and should be considered one component of a broader care plan when appropriate.

Identifying deficiency depends on testing and clinical context. Serum 25(OH)D is the accepted biomarker for status assessment. Common deficiency indicators include persistent fatigue, muscle weakness, bone or joint pain, recurrent infections, and mood changes; severe deficiency in children causes rickets and in adults can cause osteomalacia. Groups at higher risk include older adults, people with limited sun exposure, individuals with darker skin pigmentation, those with malabsorption syndromes, and people following restrictive diets.

Supplementation strategies vary by need. Maintenance doses for adults commonly range from 800 to 2,000 IU daily, while higher therapeutic regimens may be used short‑term to correct deficiency under medical supervision. Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is generally preferred over D2 (ergocalciferol) because of better bioavailability. Because vitamin D is fat‑soluble, taking it with a meal that contains fat enhances absorption.
