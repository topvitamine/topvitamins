Vitamin B12 deficiency can produce a wide range of symptoms across neurological, hematologic, and musculoskeletal systems. Understanding where and how it hurts helps clinicians and individuals identify potential deficiency early and take appropriate steps, such as laboratory testing and evidence-based supplementation.

Neurological and peripheral nerve symptoms are among the most characteristic complaints. Damage to myelin, the insulating sheath around nerves, may cause paresthesia (tingling or “pins and needles”), numbness, burning sensations and shooting pains—often beginning in the hands and feet and sometimes progressing proximally. These features can be diffuse rather than following a single nerve distribution, and they may be poorly responsive to ordinary analgesics. Persistent neuropathic symptoms merit evaluation for B12 insufficiency and other systemic causes.

Fatigue and low energy are common early manifestations and are primarily related to impaired red blood cell production. In megaloblastic anemia caused by low B12, red blood cells are large and functionally inefficient, reducing oxygen delivery to tissues and producing persistent exhaustion, shortness of breath on exertion, and reduced exercise tolerance. Correction of B12 levels typically improves hematologic function and energy, but the timeline can vary with severity and comorbidities.

Cognitive complaints—such as memory lapses, slowed processing, difficulty concentrating and increased irritability—may also arise with prolonged deficiency. B12 participates in neuronal maintenance and neurotransmitter synthesis; when it is insufficient, cognitive processing and mood regulation can be affected. In older adults, long-standing deficiency can contribute to functional decline and should be considered in differential diagnosis.

Muscle weakness and impaired coordination can occur indirectly because of compromised nerve signaling and reduced oxygenation. Patients may report unsteady gait, frequent stumbling, or difficulty rising from seated positions. These signs are especially important to investigate in older individuals where nutritional deficiency may be overlooked as an alternative to age-related decline.

Management focuses on identifying the deficiency (serum B12, methylmalonic acid, homocysteine where indicated) and instituting appropriate replacement. Common supplementation forms include cyanocobalamin and methylcobalamin; methylcobalamin is often preferred when neurological recovery is a priority because of its role in neuronal metabolism. Administration routes and dosing depend on the severity of deficiency and underlying absorption issues. Absorption can be influenced by intrinsic factor status, gastric surgery, certain medications, and dietary patterns.

Practical measures to enhance absorption include sublingual or intramuscular options when oral uptake is likely impaired, and attention to concomitant nutrients—such as folate, vitamin D, magnesium and omega-3 fatty acids—that support recovery and overall neurological health. Evidence underscores the importance of timely treatment: prolonged untreated deficiency may lead to irreversible neurological injury.
