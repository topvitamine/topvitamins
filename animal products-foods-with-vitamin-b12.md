Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for nerve function, red blood cell formation, and DNA synthesis. Because humans cannot synthesize B12, dietary intake or supplementation is necessary. This article summarizes reliable food sources, highlights limitations of plant-only diets, and points to practical options for maintaining adequate status.

Dietary sources and bioavailability

The most dependable sources of B12 are animal-derived foods, where the vitamin is produced by bacteria and accumulates in tissues. Organ meats—especially liver—are particularly concentrated (for example, beef liver contains very high microgram amounts per 100 g). Lean meats, poultry, eggs (especially yolks), and dairy products such as milk, cheese, and yogurt provide bioavailable B12 and are effective for maintaining status in omnivorous diets.

Seafood and shellfish

Many fish and shellfish are rich in B12 and offer additional nutrients like omega-3 fatty acids. Salmon, tuna, sardines, and cod deliver substantial B12 per serving, while shellfish such as clams, oysters, mussels, and crab are among the highest natural sources—clams, for instance, can contain dozens of micrograms per 100 g. Fortified foods

Fortification is the primary way non‑animal diets obtain reliable B12. Breakfast cereals, fortified plant milks (soy, almond, oat), nutritional yeast fortified with cobalamin, and some meat substitutes are typical examples. Regular consumption of fortified staples can meet daily requirements when labels confirm the added B12 content and serving sizes are adequate.

Plant-based sources and limitations

Whole plant foods do not consistently supply bioactive B12. Some algae, fermented foods, or mushrooms may contain B12 analogs or inconsistent amounts that are not reliably usable by humans; these compounds can sometimes interfere with absorption of active cobalamin. Vegan and supplement options

Because natural plant sources are unreliable, vegans and many vegetarians should rely on fortified foods and/or supplements. Common supplement forms include cyanocobalamin and methylcobalamin, offered as tablets, sublingual lozenges, sprays, or transdermal patches. Doses used for routine prevention typically exceed the RDA (2.4 µg for most adults) because higher oral doses can offset variable absorption; choices should be individualized and discussed with a clinician when medical conditions affect absorption.

Practical recommendations

- If you eat animal products, include a variety of meats, dairy, eggs, and seafood to maintain B12.
- If you avoid animal products, consume fortified foods daily and consider a reliable B12 supplement.
- Monitor status in at-risk groups (older adults, those with gastric surgery or malabsorption) using serum B12 and functional markers when indicated.

Q&A (summary)

What foods are highest in B12? Organ meats, clams, sardines, tuna, salmon, beef and fortified cereals. Can vegans get B12 from plants? Not reliably—fortified foods and supplements are recommended. How much to take? The RDA is 2.4 µg, but supplementation often uses higher doses based on individual needs.
