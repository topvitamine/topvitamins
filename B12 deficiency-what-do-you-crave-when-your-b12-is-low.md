Vitamin B12 helps support energy production, red blood cell formation, DNA synthesis and nervous system function. When stores fall short, the body can produce a range of physical and mental signals — including surprisingly specific food cravings. For a focused review of these patterns, see [what you crave when your B12 is low](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-do-you-crave-when-your-b12-is-low).

## How B12 deficiency affects appetite and cravings

B12 is found primarily in animal-derived foods (meat, fish, eggs, dairy) and is required for metabolic processes that generate usable energy. Low B12 impairs red blood cell formation and mitochondrial function, which can lead to fatigue and altered neurotransmitter balance. Those biochemical changes may translate into altered appetite regulation: some people report stronger cravings for red meat, eggs or dairy — foods that are concentrated sources of B12 — while others experience reduced appetite or nausea.

Physiologically, reduced synthesis of serotonin and dopamine during deficiency can change reward signalling and hunger cues. In parallel, a sensation of low energy may be misinterpreted by the brain as the need for calorie- and protein-dense foods, prompting targeted cravings rather than generalized hunger.

## Typical foods people crave and why

Cravings linked to low B12 commonly involve:
- Red meat and organ meats (especially liver) — among the richest dietary sources of B12.
- Fish and shellfish — bioavailable forms of the vitamin are present in clams, salmon and tuna.
- Eggs and dairy — convenient sources that also supply other B vitamins and fat for absorption.

These preferences are consistent with the body seeking concentrated sources of the missing nutrient. For people following plant-based diets, sudden urges for these animal foods may be an early behavioral cue of insufficiency.

## Appetite: increased or decreased?

The relationship between B12 and appetite is not uniform. Increased appetite or selective cravings can arise from low energy availability, while gastrointestinal symptoms of deficiency (bloating, nausea, altered taste) can suppress appetite. Both pathways can worsen deficiency if dietary intake remains inadequate.

Because absorption can be compromised by conditions such as pernicious anemia, atrophic gastritis, or medications like proton pump inhibitors and metformin, dietary change alone may not resolve symptoms. ## When to test and balanced interventions

If cravings are accompanied by fatigue, numbness, cognitive changes or mood shifts, screening with serum B12 (and, if needed, methylmalonic acid or homocysteine) is reasonable. Management may include dietary adjustments, fortified foods, or supplementation under clinical guidance. Practical comparisons of supplement formats are discussed in <a href='https://topvitamine.github.io/topvitamins/are-gummy-vitamins-as-effective-as-pills-pros-and-cons-explained'>a related review</a>, and broader commentary on formulations can be found at <a href='https://telegra.ph/Are-Gummy-Vitamins-as-Effective-as-Pills-Pros-and-Cons-Explained-12-18'>this overview</a>.

If supplementation is used, clinicians may prefer bioavailable forms (e.g., methylcobalamin) or parenteral routes when absorption is impaired. Lifestyle measures that support absorption include managing alcohol intake, reviewing medications that affect gastric acidity, and ensuring adequate dietary cofactors.

## Practical takeaway

Specific cravings for meat, eggs or dairy can be an informative signal rather than a mere preference: they may reflect a physiological need for vitamin B12. When such cravings occur together with fatigue, neurological symptoms or mood changes, consider assessment of B12 status and discuss appropriate dietary or medical strategies with a healthcare provider.
