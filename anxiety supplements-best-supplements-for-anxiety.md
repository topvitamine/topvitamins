## Introduction

Anxiety ranges from occasional worry to persistent symptoms that impair daily life. Nutritional and botanical supplements are often considered as complementary strategies to support mental well-being alongside evidence-based treatments. They can supply cofactors, fatty acids, amino acids, and plant compounds that influence neurotransmission, sleep, inflammation, and stress physiology. Supplements are not replacements for clinical care; consult a qualified healthcare professional before starting any new regimen because of potential interactions, contraindications, and quality concerns.

## Categories and evidence

Supplements commonly discussed for anxiety fall into several categories: essential nutrients (vitamins, minerals, omega-3s), amino acids and precursors, single-compound agents, herbal extracts, and multi-ingredient formulations labeled for stress relief. The evidence varies: some agents (for example, magnesium and L-theanine) have randomized trials showing modest effects in particular populations, while others rely on small studies or observational associations. Correcting identified nutrient deficiencies (vitamin D, B12, iron, magnesium) is a rational first step because repletion can remove physiological contributors to symptoms.

## Herbal and plant-based options

Herbs such as valerian, passionflower, lemon balm, kava, and ashwagandha are frequently used for situational tension and stress. Mechanisms suggested in preclinical and clinical research include modulation of GABAergic signaling, influence on hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activity, and antioxidant effects. Trial results are mixed and often depend on extract standardization, dose, and study design. Kava has demonstrated short-term anxiolytic effects in some trials but has rare associations with hepatotoxicity; medical oversight is advised when considering it.

## Nutrients and single compounds

Magnesium (glycinate or citrate), B-complex vitamins, omega-3 fatty acids (EPA/DHA), L-theanine, and melatonin (for sleep-related issues) are among nutrients commonly considered. Dosage and formulation matter: different magnesium salts have different tolerability, and omega-3 benefits depend on EPA/DHA ratios. 5‑HTP and other serotonin-related precursors can interact with antidepressants and require caution.

## Safety, quality, and clinical integration

Key considerations are baseline nutrient status, medication interactions (antidepressants, anticoagulants), pregnancy/breastfeeding status, liver health, and product quality. Prefer transparent labeling, standardized extracts, and third-party testing. Avoid undisclosed proprietary blends and be mindful of cumulative dosing when combining products. ## Conclusion

Supplements can be a supportive element in a comprehensive plan that includes sleep hygiene, physical activity, nutrition, and evidence-based psychotherapy. Expect modest, context-dependent benefits, emphasize safety and quality, and involve a clinician when symptoms are moderate to severe or when medical conditions or medications are present.
