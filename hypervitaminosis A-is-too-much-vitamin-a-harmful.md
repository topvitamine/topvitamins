Vitamin A is vital for vision, immune function and cellular health, but because it is fat‑soluble it can accumulate in tissues and cause harm when consumed in excess. This article summarizes the evidence on sources, risks, symptoms and simple precautions to avoid hypervitaminosis A.

Balance and sources

Dietary vitamin A comes in two main forms: provitamin A carotenoids (for example beta‑carotene from carrots and sweet potatoes) and preformed vitamin A (retinol/retinyl esters from animal products and many supplements). The body regulates conversion of carotenoids to active vitamin A, making them less likely to cause toxicity. By contrast, preformed vitamin A in supplements and some fortified foods bypasses that control and can accumulate in the liver.

How toxicity develops

Hypervitaminosis A can be acute (large single doses) or chronic (regular intake above safe limits). Because the liver stores vitamin A, long‑term overconsumption gradually raises blood and tissue levels and can damage the liver, bones and central nervous system. Regulatory bodies such as EFSA set a Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) for adults (3,000 mcg RAE/day) to reduce this risk.

Common signs and clinical features

Early or mild symptoms often include dry, peeling skin, hair thinning, fatigue and appetite loss. More serious effects include persistent vomiting, severe headaches, blurred vision, intracranial hypertension, liver enlargement and bone pain or fractures. Children and pregnant people are particularly vulnerable: excess vitamin A during pregnancy increases the risk of birth defects.

Diagnosis and interactions

If toxicity is suspected, clinicians use symptom history, liver function tests and retinol measurements to assess exposure. Certain medications and conditions increase risk or complicate management: isotretinoin and other retinoids, alcohol or liver disease, and agents that alter fat absorption can all interact with vitamin A status.

Prevention and practical advice

Most people meet needs from a varied diet and do not require high‑dose supplements. Recommended intakes are about 900 mcg RAE/day for adult men and 700 mcg RAE/day for adult women, with the EFSA UL at 3,000 mcg RAE/day for adults. Practical steps to reduce risk include favoring provitamin A (carotenoids) in multivitamins, checking labels for total vitamin A from all sources (including fortified foods), and avoiding multiple products that supply preformed retinol unless medically advised.

When to seek care

Seek medical attention for persistent vomiting, new severe headaches, vision changes or neurological symptoms. Early recognition and stopping the offending supplement under medical supervision typically reduce long‑term harm, but chronic cases may need ongoing monitoring of liver and bone health.

Conclusion

Vitamin A is essential but potentially toxic in excess. Awareness of sources, reading labels carefully, and staying within established intake limits are the most effective measures to enjoy benefits while minimizing risk.
