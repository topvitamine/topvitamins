## Introduction

Vitamins are organic micronutrients required in small amounts to support metabolism, tissue maintenance, immune response and other essential functions. They are classified as fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) or water-soluble (vitamin C and the B-complex family), which influences absorption, storage and supplementation needs. This summary complements the Topvitamine guide [What are the 13 vitamins?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-are-the-13-vitamins) and provides a concise, evidence-based overview of each nutrient’s primary roles, common food sources and populations at higher risk of inadequate intake.

## Overview of the 13 nutrients

- Fat-soluble vitamins
  - Vitamin A: Supports vision, epithelial health and immune function. Found as retinol in liver, dairy and eggs and as provitamin A carotenoids in colorful vegetables.
  - Vitamin D: Important for calcium absorption and bone health; synthesized in skin with UVB exposure and present in fatty fish and fortified foods.
  - Vitamin E: A lipid-soluble antioxidant that protects cell membranes; abundant in nuts, seeds and vegetable oils.
  - Vitamin K: Required for blood clotting and bone protein activation; leafy greens provide K1 while fermented foods provide K2.

- Water-soluble vitamins and related compounds
  - Vitamin C: Cofactor in collagen synthesis and an antioxidant; rich in citrus, berries and peppers and enhances non-heme iron absorption.
  - B-complex vitamins: A group with diverse roles in energy metabolism, red blood cell formation, nervous system function and DNA synthesis. Key members include:
    - B1 (thiamine), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin), B5 (pantothenic acid), B6 (pyridoxine), B7 (biotin), B9 (folate/folic acid), and B12 (cobalamin).
    - Folate is especially important in early pregnancy to reduce neural tube defect risk; B12 is critical for red blood cells and neurologic health and is concentrated in animal-derived foods.
  - Choline: Often grouped with B vitamins, choline supports membrane synthesis, neurotransmission (acetylcholine) and liver lipid transport; eggs, meat and some legumes supply choline.

## Food-first approach and when testing or supplementation is appropriate

A diverse diet of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, lean proteins, dairy or fortified alternatives, nuts and seeds usually supplies most vitamins. Certain groups commonly need extra attention: older adults (B12, D), pregnant people (folic acid), individuals with limited sun exposure (vitamin D), people following strict vegan diets (B12), and those with malabsorption disorders (fat-soluble vitamin risks). When deficiency is suspected or risk factors exist, targeted laboratory assessment—such as serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D, serum B12, folate or other relevant markers—can guide interventions.

Supplements can safely complement diet when chosen with clinical guidance. Fat-soluble vitamins require attention to dosing because of storage potential; water-soluble vitamins are generally excreted when taken in excess, but very high doses may still cause adverse effects or interfere with laboratory testing.
