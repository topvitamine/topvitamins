Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for nerve function, red blood cell formation, and DNA synthesis. Deficiency can cause fatigue, neurological symptoms, and hematological changes. Understanding how different delivery methods influence absorption and bioavailability helps clinicians and individuals select the most appropriate supplementation strategy.

## How B12 is normally absorbed

Dietary B12 binds intrinsic factor in the stomach and is absorbed in the distal ileum via receptor-mediated uptake. Age, gastric atrophy, bariatric surgery, inflammatory bowel disease, and medications such as proton pump inhibitors or metformin can impair this pathway. When intrinsic factor–dependent absorption is compromised, alternative delivery routes that bypass the gut are often considered.

## Sublingual B12: mechanism and evidence

Sublingual administration places B12 under the tongue for transmucosal uptake into capillaries, partially bypassing gastrointestinal barriers. Several clinical studies and reviews report that well-formulated sublingual preparations (methylcobalamin or cyanocobalamin) can raise serum B12 comparably to oral high-dose regimens in many patients, and in some cases approach the steady-state levels achieved by injections. Absorption speed is typically slower than injections but faster than conventional oral tablets that rely on gastrointestinal mechanisms. Practical advantages include non-invasiveness and ease of daily use.

## Injectable B12: when it’s preferred

Parenteral administration (intramuscular or subcutaneous) delivers B12 directly into systemic circulation, producing rapid and predictable increases in serum levels. This makes injections the preferred option for severe deficiency, significant malabsorption, or acute neurological involvement where prompt correction is clinically important. In routine replacement, injection frequency can be tailored (for example, initial loading followed by maintenance dosing), but injections require trained administration and carry risks such as local reactions.

## Comparing absorption rate and bioavailability

Injectable B12 provides the fastest elevation of circulating cobalamin and effectively achieves near-complete bioavailability since it bypasses epithelial transport. Sublingual B12 can achieve high bioavailability in many individuals, though uptake depends on mucosal integrity, formulation quality, and dosing regimen. For chronic, mild deficiencies or preventive maintenance in people without profound malabsorption, sublingual routes can be an evidence-supported alternative. In acute or severe cases, parenteral therapy remains more reliable for rapid correction.

## Other delivery options

High-dose oral B12 can produce modest passive diffusion (~1% absorption) sufficient in some cases when administered at large doses. Nasal sprays, transdermal systems, and liposomal formulations are emerging alternatives; clinical data vary and longer-term comparative trials are limited. ## Practical takeaways

Choice of delivery should consider severity of deficiency, presence of malabsorption, urgency of correction, patient preference, and access to care. Both sublingual and injectable B12 have roles: injections for rapid, reliable correction and sublingual formulations as a non-invasive alternative for many maintenance scenarios. Clinical monitoring of serum B12 and symptoms guides ongoing management.
