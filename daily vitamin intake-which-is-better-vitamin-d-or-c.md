## Introduction

Vitamin D and vitamin C are two frequently discussed nutrients with distinct biological roles. Choosing which to prioritize depends on individual circumstances—sun exposure, diet, age, and specific health goals. ## Core functions and clinical relevance

Vitamin D is fat-soluble and central to calcium and phosphorus metabolism, bone mineralization, and immune modulation. It is synthesized in the skin via UVB radiation and converted to active metabolites through liver and kidney hydroxylation. Clinical deficiency can present with bone pain, muscle weakness, and increased fracture risk; in children, severe deficiency causes rickets. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D is the standard marker for status assessment.

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is water-soluble, a direct antioxidant, and essential for collagen synthesis, wound healing, and enhancing non-heme iron absorption. Deficiency causes scurvy, characterized by gum bleeding, poor wound healing, and fatigue. Vitamin C also supports several immune cell functions and protects cells from oxidative damage.

## Absorption and practical considerations

Because vitamin D is fat-soluble, oral absorption improves when taken with dietary fat, and status is influenced by sunlight exposure, skin pigmentation, age, obesity, and some medications. Vitamin C is absorbed via active transport in the small intestine; absorption efficiency declines as doses increase, with much higher intakes leading to lower fractional absorption and urinary excretion.

Timing and formulation matter: take vitamin D with a main meal containing fats; take vitamin C in divided doses if using larger daily amounts to improve steady-state plasma levels and reduce gastrointestinal side effects.

## Recommended intakes and safety

Authoritative bodies like EFSA recommend roughly 15–20 µg (600–800 IU) daily for most adults to maintain vitamin D-dependent functions, though requirements vary by exposure and physiology. For vitamin C, EFSA’s adult reference value is about 80 mg daily, with higher intakes considered tolerable up to established upper limits. Excessive vitamin D can cause hypercalcemia; very high vitamin C can cause gastrointestinal disturbance and, in susceptible individuals, increase oxalate kidney stone risk.

## When to prioritize one over the other

- Prioritize vitamin D if you have limited sun exposure, are older, have darker skin, or have conditions that impair synthesis or increase risk of bone loss.
- Prioritize vitamin C if your diet is low in fruits and vegetables, you have poor wound healing, low iron absorption from plant sources, or increased oxidative stress from heavy training or smoking.

They are complementary: vitamin D regulates immune responses while vitamin C protects immune cells from oxidative damage.
