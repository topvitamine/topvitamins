## Which omega-3 is better?

Omega-3 fatty acids are essential nutrients with well-documented roles in cardiovascular, cognitive, and joint health. The primary long-chain forms—EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid)—deliver most clinical benefits, while ALA (alpha-linolenic acid) from plant sources relies on limited conversion to EPA/DHA. Choosing the most appropriate omega-3 involves assessing source, EPA:DHA ratio, dosage, bioavailability, and personal preferences.

Marine-derived options such as fish oil typically supply both EPA and DHA in concentrated form and have the largest evidence base for reducing triglycerides, supporting heart function, and contributing to brain and eye health. Plant-based omega-3s include ALA-rich foods (flaxseed, chia, walnuts) and algal oil. Algal oil is notable because it provides preformed DHA and, in some formulations, EPA—bypassing the inefficient ALA→EPA/DHA conversion. This makes algal oil a viable alternative for vegans, pregnant people who avoid seafood, and those concerned about marine contaminants. EPA vs. DHA: function and ratio

EPA and DHA have overlapping but distinct physiological roles. EPA exerts stronger anti-inflammatory effects and is often emphasized in formulations targeting cardiovascular risk reduction and inflammatory conditions. DHA is a structural component of neuronal and retinal membranes and is prioritized for cognitive development and visual health. Balanced supplements (roughly equal EPA and DHA) suit general maintenance, while EPA-dominant or DHA-dominant products are chosen when addressing specific health endpoints.

Dosage and form

A commonly cited baseline for general cardiovascular maintenance is 250 mg/day of combined EPA+DHA, while therapeutic ranges can extend to 1–4 g/day under medical supervision for elevated triglycerides or inflammatory states. Bioavailability varies by chemical form: triglyceride and phospholipid forms are typically better absorbed than ethyl esters. Consider the stated EPA/DHA per serving rather than total oil volume when comparing products.

Sustainability and contaminants

Environmental impact and contaminant testing matter. Algal oils typically avoid heavy metals and PCBs associated with some marine sources and are produced with lower pressure on wild fish stocks. Certifications and batch-specific testing for purity and oxidative stability (peroxide/TOTOX values) provide objective quality assurance. Conclusion

No single omega-3 is universally “best.” Marine fish oil offers broad clinical evidence for EPA+DHA benefits; algal oil provides a sustainable, effective DHA (and sometimes EPA) alternative for non-fish consumers. Select a product based on EPA/DHA content, form, purity testing, and the specific health outcomes you aim to support, and consult a healthcare professional when higher therapeutic doses are under consideration.
