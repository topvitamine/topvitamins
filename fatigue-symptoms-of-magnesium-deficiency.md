Magnesium is a key mineral involved in more than 300 biochemical reactions, including energy production, muscle contraction and relaxation, nerve signaling, and cardiovascular regulation. Deficiency can present with a range of symptoms that are often nonspecific, making recognition and evaluation important for appropriate management.

Common clinical features of magnesium deficiency include muscle cramps, spasms, and twitching. Magnesium acts as a natural calcium antagonist in muscle cells—insufficient magnesium can increase neuromuscular excitability and lead to painful contractions, often reported in calves, feet, eyelids, or the hands.

Persistent fatigue and generalized low energy are another frequent complaint. Magnesium is essential for ATP synthesis; when stores are low, cellular energy production can be impaired, producing chronic tiredness that does not reliably improve with additional rest. Accompanying signs such as muscle weakness or reduced exercise tolerance can help point toward a nutritional basis.

Cardiac symptoms may also occur. Magnesium contributes to stabilizing electrical activity of the heart, and low levels can be associated with palpitations, skipped beats, or more pronounced arrhythmias in vulnerable individuals. These manifestations are clinically important and warrant evaluation, especially in people with existing cardiovascular risk factors or those taking medications that alter electrolyte balance.

Neurological and headache syndromes are linked to magnesium status. Research indicates that lower intracellular magnesium may be common among people with recurrent migraines, and supplementation has been used as a preventive approach in some clinical guidelines. Symptoms such as aura, light sensitivity, nausea, and increased headache frequency may correlate with suboptimal magnesium levels.

Mood and cognitive effects are also reported. Magnesium influences neurotransmitter systems—including GABA and glutamate—and low intake has been associated with anxiety, irritability, insomnia, and depressive symptoms in observational and interventional studies. Improvements in sleep quality and mood have been noted in some trials following magnesium supplementation, though individual responses vary.

Risk factors for deficiency include inadequate dietary intake (low consumption of leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes, and whole grains), gastrointestinal disorders that impair absorption, chronic alcohol use, certain medications (e.g., diuretics, proton pump inhibitors), diabetes, and advanced age. Laboratory assessment may start with serum magnesium, but clinicians sometimes consider intracellular measures or clinical context because serum values do not always reflect total body stores.

Management typically emphasizes dietary improvement and, when appropriate, supplementation under medical supervision. Food-first strategies prioritize magnesium-rich choices, while specific supplemental forms and dosages should be selected based on tolerability, absorption characteristics, and individual health conditions.
