Introduction

Dry skin (xerosis) often results from environmental exposures, but underlying micronutrient deficiencies can substantially affect skin barrier function and moisture retention. Nutrients that support skin hydration include fat-soluble vitamins, B-complex vitamins, and essential fatty acids; identifying which are lacking helps tailor dietary and supplemental strategies.

Vitamin D and skin barrier function

Vitamin D influences keratinocyte proliferation, immune regulation, and lipid synthesis in the stratum corneum. Low Vitamin D status has been associated with increased transepidermal water loss and chronic dry patches, particularly in populations with limited sun exposure or reduced cutaneous synthesis. Dietary sources (fatty fish, fortified foods, egg yolks) and measured serum 25(OH)D can guide correction strategies.

Antioxidant and lipid protection from Vitamin E

Vitamin E is a major lipophilic antioxidant in the epidermis and helps preserve cellular membranes and the skin’s lipid matrix. Deficiency may present as scaly, rough skin with impaired healing. Dietary sources include nuts, seeds, and vegetable oils; systemic intake supports intrinsic barrier repair beyond topical application.

Essential fatty acids (EFAs) and ceramide formation

Omega-3 and Omega-6 fatty acids are structural components of epidermal lipids. Linoleic acid (an Omega-6) is a precursor for ceramides, which are crucial for preventing water loss. Insufficient EFAs lead to flaky, itchy skin and compromised barrier integrity. Regular intake of oily fish, flaxseed, or algae-derived oils can restore lipid balance and reduce inflammatory contributors to dryness.

Other hydration-focused vitamins

Vitamin A supports normal keratinization and sebum production; deficiency can produce hyperkeratotic, rough skin. Vitamin C is required for collagen synthesis and offers antioxidant protection that indirectly preserves hydration by maintaining dermal structure. B-vitamins such as biotin (B7) and niacin (B3) support fatty acid metabolism and barrier function; their insufficiency can present with dermatitis and increased dryness.

Minerals and structural proteins

Zinc and magnesium participate in wound healing, cell turnover, and enzymatic processes relevant to hydration. Collagen peptides supply amino acids that support dermal matrix integrity and may improve skin elasticity and moisture over time. Broad dietary patterns rich in colorful produce, lean proteins, seeds, nuts, and whole grains usually cover these needs.

Practical assessment and integrated approach

When dry skin is persistent despite topical care, consider nutritional evaluation: dietary review, basic labs (e.g., serum 25(OH)D, potentially zinc), and clinical signs. Conclusion

No single nutrient exclusively causes dry skin in all cases; however, deficiencies in Vitamin D, Vitamin E, essential fatty acids, and certain fat‑ and water‑soluble vitamins frequently contribute to barrier dysfunction and increased water loss. An evidence-based approach combines dietary assessment, selective laboratory testing, and tailored nutritional strategies to restore skin hydration.
