Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water‑soluble micronutrient with systemic roles in antioxidant defense, collagen synthesis, immune function, and tissue repair. This article summarizes which organs and systems show the clearest physiological responses to Vitamin C, the mechanisms involved, and practical implications for maintaining adequate levels.

Primary organs and transport pathways

Although Vitamin C is required by nearly every tissue, the liver and the circulatory system function as central hubs for distribution and metabolism. After intestinal absorption, Vitamin C enters the portal circulation and is processed by the liver before being exported into the systemic bloodstream. Plasma levels and tissue uptake are regulated by sodium‑dependent Vitamin C transporters (SVCTs), which determine how much ascorbate reaches target organs.

Skin and connective tissues

The skin and connective tissues are high‑demand sites for Vitamin C because of its role as an essential cofactor in the hydroxylation of proline and lysine during collagen biosynthesis. Collagen provides structural integrity to skin, blood vessels, tendons, and bone; insufficient Vitamin C impairs collagen cross‑linking and delays wound healing. Topical and systemic Vitamin C both contribute to dermal antioxidant capacity and can reduce markers of photodamage, though Vitamin C is not a substitute for photoprotection.

Immune system

Immune cells accumulate Vitamin C to support innate and adaptive defenses. Neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes use Vitamin C to modulate reactive oxygen species, support chemotaxis, and assist in cellular proliferation and differentiation. Clinical studies indicate that regular Vitamin C supplementation can reduce the duration and severity of some respiratory infections in specific populations under physical stress; however, effects vary by baseline status and exposure.

Antioxidant defense and vulnerable tissues

Vitamin C is a potent aqueous‑phase antioxidant that neutralizes free radicals, regenerates oxidized Vitamin E, and helps protect proteins, lipids, and DNA from oxidative damage. Organs with high oxidative turnover—such as the liver, the retina, and the nervous system—benefit from adequate Vitamin C to limit oxidative injury. This antioxidant activity complements, but does not replace, other lifestyle measures to reduce oxidative exposures (e.g., smoking cessation, balanced diet).

Tissue repair and healing

During injury or surgery, Vitamin C supports fibroblast activity, collagen deposition, and endothelial function—processes critical for timely wound closure and tissue remodeling. Populations with increased repair demands (e.g., postoperative patients, older adults) may show improved recovery metrics when Vitamin C status is corrected, although optimal dosing should be individualized.

Conclusion

Vitamin C’s most direct organ‑level roles arise through the liver–circulatory distribution axis, with pronounced localized effects in skin, connective tissue, and immune cells. Maintaining adequate intake supports antioxidant protection, collagen formation, and tissue repair, which together contribute to overall physiological resilience.
