Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell production, neurological function, and DNA synthesis. Deficiency can arise from low intake, but more commonly from impaired absorption caused by specific diseases. Understanding which conditions reduce B12 uptake helps clinicians and patients target screening and appropriate supplementation.

Pernicious anemia is a primary example: an autoimmune attack destroys gastric parietal cells and the intrinsic factor they produce, preventing B12 absorption in the ileum. Symptoms often develop gradually and include fatigue, pallor, memory and concentration problems, paresthesia, and gait disturbances. Management requires lifelong B12 replacement, often via intramuscular injections or high-dose oral therapy when clinically appropriate. Malabsorption syndromes such as celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, and tropical sprue directly impair the intestinal mucosa and reduce B12 uptake. Celiac disease damages small intestinal villi after gluten exposure, while Crohn’s disease frequently involves the terminal ileum—the specific site for B12 absorption. Patients with these disorders often need targeted nutritional management alongside disease-specific therapy, and severely impaired absorption may necessitate parenteral B12.

Gastrointestinal disorders and surgical histories also influence B12 status. Atrophic gastritis and chronic hypochlorhydria reduce gastric acid and intrinsic factor production, limiting release of B12 from food. Bariatric procedures and resections that bypass or remove stomach and ileal tissue can create long-term malabsorption, making routine supplementation and monitoring essential for affected individuals.

Autoimmune conditions beyond pernicious anemia can indirectly lower B12. Autoimmune gastritis mimics pernicious mechanisms by eroding parietal cells. Systemic autoimmune diseases and their therapies may alter gut integrity or motility, further complicating nutrient absorption. Regular screening of B12 in patients with autoimmune disease is prudent because symptoms can overlap with primary disease manifestations.

A common iatrogenic cause is long-term metformin therapy for type 2 diabetes. Metformin disrupts calcium-dependent uptake of the B12–intrinsic factor complex in the distal ileum, and studies report variable deficiency rates among chronic users. Given that diabetic neuropathy symptoms can mirror B12 neuropathy, clinicians often recommend periodic B12 testing for patients on prolonged metformin regimens.

Diagnosis relies on laboratory assessment of serum B12, methylmalonic acid, and homocysteine when indicated, together with clinical correlation. Treatment choices—oral, sublingual, or intramuscular supplementation—depend on severity and the mechanism of impaired absorption. Early intervention often reverses hematologic and some neurologic symptoms; however, prolonged deficiency can cause irreversible nerve damage.

In summary, pernicious anemia, malabsorption syndromes, GI disorders, autoimmune disease, and metformin use are key contributors to B12 deficiency. Screening at-risk populations and tailoring supplementation to the underlying mechanism remain central to preventing long-term complications.
