## Understanding the difference between vitamin D and vitamin D3

Vitamin D is a group of fat‑soluble secosteroids essential for calcium balance, bone health, immune modulation, and other physiological processes. When people refer to “vitamin D” they usually mean the broader category that includes two main supplementable forms: vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) and vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol). Although both can prevent deficiency, they differ in source, stability, and how effectively they raise circulating vitamin D status.

Vitamin D3 is the form produced in human skin after exposure to ultraviolet B (UVB) sunlight and is structurally closer to the endogenous molecule the body uses. D2 is generally derived from fungal or plant sources following UV irradiation. Clinical evidence indicates that D3 more reliably increases and sustains serum 25‑hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) concentrations, the standard biomarker used to assess vitamin D status.

Bioavailability and metabolism

Bioavailability refers to the fraction of an ingested nutrient that becomes available to the body. Vitamin D3 typically shows greater bioavailability than D2: it binds more strongly to vitamin D binding protein, is more stable in circulation, and results in longer retention. After intake (or production in skin), vitamin D undergoes two hydroxylation steps: first in the liver to 25(OH)D, then in the kidney to the active hormone 1,25‑dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol). Genetic factors, body composition, gut health, and co‑nutrients such as magnesium and vitamin K influence these pathways and the functional outcome of supplementation.

Practical considerations for supplementation

Formulation affects absorption: oil‑based capsules, emulsified liquids, or liposomal preparations that include dietary fat usually enhance uptake of this fat‑soluble vitamin. Dosing should be individualized—age, baseline serum levels, sun exposure, skin pigmentation, and medical conditions that impair absorption matter. Routine monitoring via 25(OH)D testing helps guide both corrective and maintenance dosing and reduces the risk of excessive intake.

Diet, sunlight, and when supplements help

Sunlight is the most natural source of vitamin D3, but latitude, season, skin covering, sunscreen use, and time spent outdoors limit synthesis for many people. Dietary sources are few (fatty fish, fortified foods, egg yolks), so supplements are commonly used to achieve sufficient levels. Choosing between D2 and D3

For correcting deficiency and maintaining steady 25(OH)D levels, cholecalciferol (D3) is generally favored for its potency and pharmacokinetics. Ergocalciferol (D2) remains useful in some clinical contexts and for certain vegetarian formulations. Vegans can find plant‑sourced D3 derived from lichen when a cholecalciferol preparation is preferred.
