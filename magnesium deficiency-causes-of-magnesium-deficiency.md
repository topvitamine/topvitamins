Magnesium is a vital mineral involved in hundreds of enzymatic reactions, from energy production and muscle contraction to nerve signaling and bone health. Despite its importance, deficiency is common and can occur even in people who take multiple dietary supplements. Understanding the causes helps clarify why symptoms persist and how supplementation and lifestyle adjustments can restore balance.

Common causes of magnesium deficiency include inadequate dietary intake, impaired intestinal absorption, excessive losses, medication effects, and nutrient interactions. Diets high in processed foods and low in leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes, and whole grains provide less magnesium than whole-food diets. Chronic low intake gradually depletes body stores and increases susceptibility to symptoms such as muscle cramps, fatigue, and mood changes.

Gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, chronic diarrhea) impair absorption and often lead to deficiency despite oral supplementation. Similarly, aging reduces intestinal absorption efficiency and is frequently compounded by polypharmacy. Several common medications—particularly loop diuretics, proton pump inhibitors, and some antibiotics—can increase urinary magnesium loss or reduce absorption, making long-term users more vulnerable.

Metabolic and systemic conditions also explain many cases. Uncontrolled diabetes increases renal magnesium excretion, while chronic kidney disease can alter magnesium handling in complex ways. Heavy alcohol use leads to decreased intake, impaired absorption, and increased urinary losses. Intense physical activity and prolonged sweating can raise magnesium requirements and losses, and chronic psychological stress increases magnesium utilization.

Nutrient interactions are an often-overlooked cause. Excessive intake of certain minerals (e.g., calcium, phosphorus, zinc) or high-dose iron supplements may interfere with magnesium absorption or alter its balance. Conversely, adequate magnesium is required for the activation and metabolism of other nutrients, such as vitamin D; deficiency can blunt the expected effects of these nutrients. Diagnosis is not always straightforward: serum magnesium reflects only about 1% of total body magnesium, so laboratory tests can appear normal even when tissue stores are low. Clinical assessment of risk factors, symptoms, and, where appropriate, specialized testing can provide a clearer picture. Addressing the causes typically involves combined strategies: improving dietary sources, selecting appropriate magnesium formulations (e.g., citrate or glycinate for better absorption), reviewing interacting medications with a clinician, and correcting coexisting nutrient imbalances. Monitoring symptoms and, when needed, repeating laboratory or functional tests supports effective, individualized management. In summary, magnesium deficiency arises from multiple, interacting causes—dietary patterns, malabsorption, medication use, increased loss, and competing nutrients. A careful, evidence-informed approach that evaluates these factors improves the likelihood that supplementation and dietary changes will restore optimal magnesium status.
