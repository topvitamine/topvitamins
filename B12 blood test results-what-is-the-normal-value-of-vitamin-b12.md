Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is an essential, water-soluble nutrient involved in DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation, and nervous system maintenance. Accurate interpretation of blood levels matters for diagnosis and management, because serum concentrations do not always reflect cellular function or early deficiency.

Standard laboratory reference ranges for adults commonly fall between 200 and 900 pg/mL. Clinically, values below 200 pg/mL are usually considered deficient, 200–300 pg/mL are borderline or low-normal, and levels above ~300 pg/mL are often treated as adequate. Many clinicians, however, consider concentrations above 400–450 pg/mL to be more reliably protective against subtle neurological or cognitive symptoms, particularly in older adults or people with risk factors.

Serum B12 tests measure total circulating B12 (both active and inactive forms) and can miss functional deficiency. To improve diagnostic accuracy, physicians may measure methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine: elevated MMA is a sensitive marker of intracellular B12 insufficiency, while raised homocysteine can reflect deficiency of B12, folate, or B6. Interpreting results in context—symptoms, medications, and comorbidities—is essential.

Populations at higher risk of low B12 include strict vegans, older adults (due to reduced gastric acid and intrinsic factor), people with gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., celiac disease, Crohn’s disease), and those who have had bariatric surgery. Long-term use of medications such as metformin or proton pump inhibitors can also reduce absorption. Early symptoms are often nonspecific—fatigue, memory difficulties, paresthesia, mood changes, and anemia—so clinicians often act on borderline lab results when symptoms or functional markers suggest deficiency.

When supplementation is indicated, formulations include cyanocobalamin, methylcobalamin, hydroxocobalamin, and adenosylcobalamin; delivery options range from oral tablets and sublingual preparations to intramuscular injections for people with severe absorption problems. Interpretation should also consider unusually high levels. Persistently elevated serum B12 without supplementation can sometimes indicate liver disease, myeloproliferative disorders, or other underlying conditions and warrants further evaluation.
