Introduction

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in nerve transmission, muscle contraction, DNA synthesis and energy production. While supplements can correct deficiencies and support health, they are not appropriate for everyone. This concise, evidence-based overview summarizes common contraindications, adverse effects, interaction risks and signs of toxicity to help guide safer use.

Understanding contraindications

A contraindication is a specific situation in which a treatment may be harmful. For magnesium, key concerns arise when homeostasis, excretion or electrical signaling in the body is impaired. Populations at higher risk include people with reduced renal function, certain neuromuscular disorders, and those on medications that alter electrolyte balance or drug absorption.

Major contraindications and risk groups

- Kidney disease: The kidneys excrete excess magnesium. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) can lead to magnesium accumulation and hypermagnesemia. Patients with moderate-to-severe CKD should avoid unsupervised supplementation and follow monitoring guidance from clinicians.
- Cardiac conduction disorders: High magnesium levels can slow atrioventricular conduction and, in susceptible individuals, precipitate bradycardia or exacerbate heart block.
- Neuromuscular disorders: Conditions such as myasthenia gravis may worsen with excess magnesium because it impairs neuromuscular transmission.
- Severe hypotension or respiratory compromise: Because magnesium is a vasodilator and neuromuscular depressant, it can worsen low blood pressure or respiratory function at high levels.

Side effects and toxicity

Gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, nausea, cramping) are the most common side effects, particularly with forms such as magnesium oxide or citrate. More serious toxicity—hypermagnesemia—manifests as flushing, lethargy, reduced reflexes, hypotension, bradycardia and respiratory depression. Serum magnesium above ~4 mg/dL often produces symptoms; levels >6 mg/dL can be life-threatening and may require intravenous calcium or dialysis.

Allergic reactions

True allergy to the magnesium ion is rare. When allergic-type reactions occur, they are often due to inactive ingredients, fillers or coating agents in a product. Presentations can include rash, urticaria or, rarely, angioedema and respiratory symptoms. If an immediate hypersensitivity reaction is suspected, discontinue the product and seek medical evaluation.

Drug and nutrient interactions

Magnesium binds to certain oral drugs, reducing their absorption. Notable interactions include tetracycline and fluoroquinolone antibiotics, bisphosphonates and levothyroxine; separation of doses by 2–4 hours is commonly recommended. Magnesium can also potentiate the effects of calcium channel blockers and interact with diuretics that alter electrolyte balance. High intakes of calcium, zinc or iron may compete with magnesium absorption; spacing supplements can improve bioavailability.

Practical guidance

Assess kidney function, current medications and neuromuscular conditions before starting magnesium. Use lower doses and more bioavailable forms (e.g., glycinate or malate) if gastrointestinal tolerance is a concern. Monitor for early symptoms—persistent diarrhea, muscle weakness, dizziness or cardiac irregularities—and consult a clinician if these occur.
