## What Does Omega-3 Do for Women?

Omega-3 fatty acids (ALA, EPA, and DHA) are essential polyunsaturated fats that support multiple physiological systems. For women, adequate omega-3 intake is linked to cardiovascular protection, cognitive resilience, hormonal regulation, skin integrity, and inflammation control. This article summarizes current evidence and practical considerations for integrating omega-3s into women's health strategies.

### Cardiovascular and metabolic effects

EPA and DHA have been shown to lower triglyceride levels, support endothelial function, and reduce arrhythmic risk factors—mechanisms that contribute to cardiovascular risk reduction. Observational and randomized studies suggest benefits for people at elevated risk of heart disease; effects are dose-dependent and generally relate to combined EPA+DHA intake. During and after menopause, when estrogen-mediated cardioprotection declines, maintaining adequate omega-3 intake can be one component of a heart-focused approach.

### Brain health and mood

DHA is a major structural lipid in the brain and retina. Higher DHA status is associated with preserved brain volume and cognitive performance in older adults in some cohorts. EPA has been evaluated in clinical trials for mood disorders, with moderate evidence supporting its role as an adjunctive therapy for depressive symptoms. For pregnant and lactating women, maternal DHA contributes to fetal and infant neural and visual development.

### Hormonal balance and reproductive health

Omega-3s influence the production of eicosanoids—signaling molecules that regulate inflammation and reproductive processes. Clinical trials indicate that omega-3 supplementation can reduce menstrual pain and may ease some premenstrual symptoms. In pregnancy, adequate DHA intake is associated with longer gestation and may reduce the risk of preterm birth in some populations. For conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), omega-3s may modestly improve markers of inflammation and lipid profiles, though effects on ovulation and fertility are variable.

### Skin and connective tissue

EPA and DHA support skin barrier function, hydration, and anti-inflammatory responses. Evidence shows potential benefits for inflammatory skin conditions (e.g., acne, eczema) and for mitigating UV-induced damage to skin. Incorporating omega-3s as part of a balanced dietary pattern can complement topical care and other systemic nutrients important for dermal health.

### Inflammation and immune modulation

Omega-3 fatty acids compete with omega-6 pathways to produce less pro-inflammatory eicosanoids and are precursors for resolving mediators such as resolvins and protectins. In autoimmune and inflammatory conditions—many of which disproportionately affect women—supplementation has been associated with reduced joint pain and markers of inflammation in several trials. Effects vary by condition, dose, and baseline diet.

### Practical considerations

Dietary sources include fatty fish (EPA/DHA) and plant sources like flaxseed and chia (ALA); conversion of ALA to EPA/DHA is limited. Supplementation may be considered when dietary intake is insufficient, especially during pregnancy, lactation, or periods of increased need. Typical general recommendations for combined EPA+DHA range from 250–500 mg/day for adults, with higher therapeutic doses used under clinical supervision. ### Summary

Omega-3 fatty acids play multiple roles relevant to women’s health, including cardiovascular support, cognitive and mood benefits, hormonal modulation, skin health, and inflammation resolution. Decisions about supplementation should consider dietary intake, life stage (e.g., pregnancy, menopause), and clinical context, and be discussed with a healthcare professional when higher therapeutic doses are contemplated.
