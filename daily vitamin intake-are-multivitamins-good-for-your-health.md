## Quick overview

Multivitamins can help fill dietary gaps for people with restricted diets, increased nutrient needs, or absorption challenges, but they are not a substitute for a balanced diet. Effectiveness depends on individual factors such as age, sex, life stage, and health status. Consider medical testing and professional advice before starting regular supplementation.

## What multivitamins do

Multivitamins combine essential vitamins and minerals—often including A, C, D, E, K, B-complex, zinc, magnesium, and iron—to provide broad nutritional coverage. They are designed to complement food intake by addressing common shortfalls that occur with modern diets, limited sun exposure, or specific life stages such as pregnancy and older age.

## Evidence and benefits

Certain nutrients have strong, evidence-based roles: vitamin D supports bone health and immune function; folate prevents neural tube defects in pregnancy; vitamin B12 is essential for nerve function and blood formation. Supplementation shows clear benefit when a deficiency is identified or when dietary intake is insufficient.

Other outcomes, such as broad prevention of chronic disease in well-nourished populations, are less consistent in clinical trials. Multivitamins are most useful as targeted tools to correct or prevent deficiencies rather than as universal disease-prevention pills.

## Who may benefit most

Populations at higher risk of micronutrient shortfalls include:
- Older adults (reduced absorption of B12 and D)
- Pregnant or breastfeeding people (higher folate, iron needs)
- People on restrictive diets (e.g., vegans and B12)
- Those with malabsorption conditions (celiac disease, IBD)
- Individuals with very limited diets or low sun exposure

## Risks and practical advice

Overuse can be harmful. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) can accumulate and reach toxic levels if taken in excess. Iron supplementation should be guided by laboratory evidence because unnecessary iron carries risks. Interactions with medications and overlapping products can also cause unintended high intakes.

Practical points:
- Base supplementation on dietary assessment and lab results when possible.
- Choose formulations aligned with age, sex, and life stage.
- Take fat-soluble vitamins with a meal containing fats for better absorption.
- Avoid combining multiple supplements that duplicate the same nutrients without oversight.

## Conclusion

Multivitamins can be a useful strategy to address specific nutrient gaps, especially when tailored to the individual. They work best alongside a diverse, whole-food diet and informed clinical guidance. Routine use without evaluation provides limited benefit and may carry risks, so personalized assessment should guide decisions about supplementation.
