# Should You Take Vitamin C Every Day?

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential water-soluble vitamin involved in collagen synthesis, iron absorption, immune cell function, and antioxidant defense. Because humans cannot synthesize it, regular intake from food or supplements is necessary. Whether you should take a daily supplement depends on dietary patterns, lifestyle, health status, and specific needs.

Daily requirements are modest: adult men generally need about 90 mg/day and adult women about 75 mg/day, with a tolerable upper intake level of 2,000 mg/day. Evidence from randomized trials and meta-analyses indicates that routine supplementation does not dramatically reduce cold incidence in the general population but can shorten duration and severity of symptoms in some groups, and can reduce cold risk in people under intense physical stress.

Supplementation is most justifiable for identifiable groups with higher needs or lower intake: smokers (who require an extra ~35 mg/day), older adults with poorer absorption, people with restrictive diets or low fruit and vegetable intake, those with certain gastrointestinal conditions that impair absorption, and individuals experiencing frequent physical or psychological stress. Short-term increases in dose during acute infection are sometimes used to meet elevated demands; however, long-term megadoses are not routinely recommended.

Adverse effects are usually gastrointestinal and dose-related. Nausea, abdominal cramping, and osmotic diarrhea can occur at high intakes, and very large chronic doses may increase the risk of kidney stones in susceptible individuals. Because excess water-soluble vitamin is eliminated in urine, toxicity is less common than with fat-soluble vitamins, but megadosing offers diminishing returns and increased side-effect risk.

Formulations can influence tolerability and bioavailability. Buffered vitamin C reduces acidity and may be gentler on the stomach; liposomal preparations encapsulate ascorbic acid within lipid vesicles and can improve cellular uptake and reduce gastrointestinal distress. Time-release tablets can also decrease sudden high intestinal concentrations that cause laxative effects.

Optimal immune support is multi-factorial. Vitamin C performs best as part of a broader approach that includes adequate sleep, stress management, balanced nutrition, and complementary nutrients such as vitamin D, magnesium, and omega-3 fatty acids.
