Muscle and joint stiffness is a frequent, multifactorial problem that affects mobility and daily function. Nutrition plays a central role in maintaining connective tissue integrity, modulating inflammation, and supporting neuromuscular function. This concise review summarizes the vitamins with the strongest evidence for reducing stiffness and supporting recovery, and notes complementary nutritional strategies.

Vitamin D: evidence and practical notes

Vitamin D is integral to calcium homeostasis, bone health and muscle function. Observational studies consistently link low serum 25(OH)D levels with greater musculoskeletal pain and weakness, and randomized trials suggest supplementation can reduce pain in deficient individuals. Natural sources include fatty fish, fortified foods and sunlight, but many people require supplementation to reach therapeutic ranges. Clinical measurement of serum 25(OH)D is advised before high-dose replacement.

Vitamin C: collagen synthesis and antioxidant support

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is required for hydroxylation of proline and lysine residues during collagen formation, a critical process for tendon, ligament and cartilage repair. Its antioxidant properties also reduce oxidative stress after exercise. Adequate intake from fruits and vegetables supports tissue repair; higher supplemental doses (under clinical guidance) may be considered during periods of injury recovery.

Vitamin E and inflammatory modulation

As a lipid-soluble antioxidant, vitamin E protects cell membranes from oxidative damage that can propagate inflammation in joint tissues. Some trials report modest reductions in reported joint pain with vitamin E supplementation, though findings are heterogeneous. Dietary sources include nuts, seeds and leafy greens; supplementation should respect established upper limits to avoid adverse effects.

B vitamins and neuromuscular health

B6, B12 and folate contribute to energy metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis and nerve health. Deficiencies can present as neuropathic pain, cramps or impaired coordination, which may be mistaken for primary joint stiffness. Populations at risk (older adults, strict vegetarians, people on certain medications) benefit from evaluation and targeted correction with active B-vitamin forms when appropriate.

Vitamin K and plant-based considerations

Vitamin K, particularly K2, participates in calcium regulation and bone matrix protein activation. Adequate intake supports bone–joint interactions that influence mobility. Plant-derived phytochemicals and dietary nitrates (from leafy greens and beets) can also improve microcirculation and reduce muscular tension.

Adjuncts: glucosamine, chondroitin and MSM

Non-vitamin supplements such as glucosamine, chondroitin and MSM supply structural substrates or sulfur necessary for connective tissue maintenance. Clinical trials show variable but sometimes meaningful improvements in pain and function for degenerative conditions when these agents are used consistently.

Integrating diet, testing and safety

A practical approach includes dietary optimization (fish, vegetables, citrus, nuts), targeted blood testing (vitamin D, B12, 25(OH)D), and evidence-guided supplementation for identified deficiencies. Summary

Vitamin D, vitamin C, certain B vitamins, vitamin E and vitamin K each have plausible, evidence-based roles in reducing stiffness and supporting musculoskeletal health. Combine dietary measures, diagnostic testing and cautious supplementation as indicated, and consult a clinician when planning treatment for persistent or severe symptoms.
