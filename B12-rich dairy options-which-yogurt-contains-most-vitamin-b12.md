Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell formation, neurological function, DNA synthesis and energy metabolism. Because humans cannot synthesize B12, dietary intake matters. Yogurt—whether dairy or plant-based—can be a practical source, but B12 content varies widely across types and brands.

Understanding which yogurt contains the most vitamin B12 requires comparing natural dairy levels, fortified alternatives, and the potential role of probiotics. Plain whole-milk yogurt typically provides about 0.6–1.4 µg per 150 g serving, while strained varieties like Greek yogurt or skyr may have somewhat reduced B12 because straining removes whey where some B12 is concentrated. Plant-based yogurts usually lack naturally occurring B12 and rely on fortification to supply meaningful amounts.

Manufacturers commonly add cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin; fortified plant yogurts can provide 1–3 µg per serving, potentially covering a substantial proportion of the adult recommended intake (2.4 µg/day).

Plant-based options: soy and almond yogurts are most frequently fortified, with fortified soy yogurts often supplying around 0.75–1.5 µg per 125 g serving. Coconut, cashew and oat yogurts sometimes include B12 but vary by brand—label reading is essential. Dairy yogurts: natural sources of B12 are found in milk-derived products. Regular plain yogurts and European-style varieties tend to retain more B12 than heavily strained types. Factors that influence B12 levels include the milk source and any post-production fortification. For information on complementary nutrients and how long to take supplements like omega‑3s—often paired with fortified foods—see <a href='https://topvitamine.github.io/topvitamins/how-long-can-you-take-omega-3'>Topvitamine: How long can you take omega‑3?</a>.

Probiotics and absorption: fermented yogurts with live cultures may support gut health and improve nutrient absorption. Strains such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium do not reliably synthesize B12 in the human gut, but they can help maintain a healthy intestinal environment that supports absorption of dietary B12. Look for products labeled with “live and active cultures” if you want probiotic benefits alongside B12 intake.

Practical guidance: to maximize B12 from yogurt, choose fortified plant-based yogurts that list vitamin B12 on the nutrition panel or select dairy yogurts with higher natural B12 content (check product nutrition facts). If you rely primarily on plant-based foods, prioritize fortified yogurts providing at least 1 µg per serving and combine them with other fortified foods or supplements where appropriate. In summary, the highest vitamin B12 levels are typically found in fortified yogurts (1–3 µg per serving) and in some unfortified dairy yogurts (around 0.6–1.4 µg per typical serving). Careful label reading and choosing products with explicit B12 fortification is the most reliable way to ensure adequate intake.
