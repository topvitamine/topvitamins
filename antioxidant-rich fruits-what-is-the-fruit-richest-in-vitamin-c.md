## Introduction

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an essential water-soluble nutrient involved in collagen synthesis, iron absorption, antioxidant defense, and immune function. Because humans cannot synthesize vitamin C, dietary sources—particularly fruits—are important to maintain adequate levels and prevent deficiency symptoms such as fatigue, gum inflammation, and, in severe cases, scurvy.

## The fruit richest in vitamin C

Among whole fruits, small tropical cherries and berries top the vitamin C charts. Notably, the acerola cherry and camu camu are exceptionally concentrated sources. For an accessible overview, see [What is the fruit richest in vitamin C?](https://www.topvitamine.com/blogs/news/what-is-the-fruit-richest-in-vitamin-c) which summarizes comparative data and practical use.

Acerola (Malpighia emarginata) commonly provides hundreds to over a thousand milligrams of vitamin C per 100 g in fresh samples; some analyses report 1,500–2,000 mg/100 g under optimal conditions. Camu camu (Myrciaria dubia), a rainforest fruit, has even higher measured values in some studies, exceeding 2,000 mg/100 g. These concentrations far exceed common citrus sources and explain why both fruits are frequently used in concentrated powders and extracts.

## Other high-vitamin C fruits

Several widely available fruits also deliver meaningful vitamin C: guava (~200–230 mg/100 g), blackcurrants (~150–200 mg/100 g), kiwi (~90–100 mg/100 g), strawberries (~50–60 mg/100 g), and oranges (~50–55 mg/100 g). Berries such as blackcurrant and elderberry add anthocyanins and other polyphenols that synergize with vitamin C to enhance antioxidant capacity.

## Practical considerations for intake

Portion size matters: a single guava or a cup of strawberries can provide the recommended daily intake for many adults. Fresh consumption maximizes vitamin C availability because heat, light, and prolonged storage degrade ascorbic acid. When fresh fruit is not practical, standardized extracts and freeze-dried powders concentrate vitamin C while preserving phytochemicals; however, product quality varies, so review labeling and sourcing.

## Combining foods for broader benefit

Diverse fruit intake provides vitamin C alongside complementary nutrients: kiwi contributes vitamin E and potassium, papaya supplies beta-carotene, and pineapple offers bromelain. Berries add polyphenols that support antioxidant networks. For practical self-assessment and learning more about which vitamins you may lack, see this overview: <a href='https://telegra.ph/How-do-I-know-which-vitamins-I-am-lacking-02-28'>How do I know which vitamins I am lacking</a>.

## Summary

The highest vitamin C concentrations are found in acerola and camu camu, followed by guava, blackcurrant, and several kiwifruit and berry species. Fresh, minimally processed fruit provides optimal bioavailability, while concentrated forms can be useful when fresh produce is unavailable. For additional context about nutrient sourcing and product options, a general resource is available at <a href='https://www.topvitamine.com'>Topvitamine</a>.

Consuming a variety of vitamin C–rich fruits supports immune function, collagen formation, and antioxidant defenses while providing a spectrum of other beneficial phytochemicals.
