Quick summary

Vitamin K, B9 (folate), and B12 are essential micronutrients with distinct physiological roles. Vitamin K supports blood clotting and bone metabolism and is abundant in leafy greens, fermented foods, and some animal products. Folate (B9) is crucial for DNA synthesis and fetal development and is found in legumes, green vegetables, fruits, and fortified grains. Vitamin B12 is necessary for red blood cell formation and nerve function and occurs mainly in animal-derived foods and fortified products; supplementation or fortified foods are important for people on plant-based diets or with absorption issues. Combining nutrient-dense foods with targeted supplementation when needed helps maintain adequate status and supports overall health.

Introduction

Vitamins K, B9, and B12 perform complementary but non-redundant functions: K in coagulation and bone health, B9 in one-carbon metabolism and fetal neural development, and B12 in hematologic and neurologic processes. Dietary sources vary by vitamin type and bioavailability; understanding food patterns and potential need for supplements can guide practical dietary choices.

Natural food sources

- Vitamin K: Dark leafy greens (kale, spinach, collards), cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, Brussels sprouts), fermented foods such as natto (rich in K2), and animal-derived sources like certain cheeses and liver provide K1 and K2. Fat in a meal enhances absorption of this fat‑soluble vitamin.

- Folate (B9): Legumes (lentils, chickpeas), green leafy vegetables (spinach, asparagus), citrus fruits, avocados, and folic-acid–fortified grains supply folate. Recommended intake increases during pregnancy due to neural tube closure in early gestation.

- Vitamin B12: Animal foods (meat, liver, fish, shellfish, eggs, dairy) are principal sources. For vegetarians and vegans, fortified foods (cereals, plant milks, nutritional yeast) or supplements are reliable alternatives because B12 is not produced in appreciable amounts by plant foods.

Practical considerations for absorption and status

- Pair fat-soluble vitamin K sources with dietary fat to improve uptake.
- Vitamin C–rich foods can enhance folate utilization and support overall one-carbon metabolism.
- Age, gastrointestinal conditions (e.g., atrophic gastritis, celiac disease), certain medications, and bariatric surgery can impair B12 absorption; clinicians may recommend oral high-dose, sublingual, or injectable B12 depending on the cause.

When to consider supplementation

Supplements can be appropriate when dietary intake is insufficient, when physiological requirements increase (pregnancy), or when malabsorption is present. Clinical assessment (dietary review and blood testing) informs the need, form, and dose. Follow evidence-based regional guidelines and clinician advice rather than self-prescribing high doses.

Conclusion

A food-first approach emphasizing leafy greens, legumes, fortified items, fermented foods, and appropriate animal products can meet most needs for vitamins K, B9, and B12. Targeted supplementation guided by testing and clinical advice helps address situations where diet alone is insufficient.
