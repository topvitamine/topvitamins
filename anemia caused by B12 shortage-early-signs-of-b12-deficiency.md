Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for nerve function, red blood cell formation, and DNA synthesis. Symptoms of deficiency often begin subtly and may progress over months to years. Early recognition and testing are important because some neurological changes can become irreversible if left untreated.

Quick overview: common early signs include persistent fatigue, pale or slightly jaundiced skin, memory difficulties or 'brain fog,' and sensory changes such as numbness or tingling in the hands and feet. These symptoms reflect B12’s roles in energy metabolism, hemoglobin formation, and maintenance of the myelin sheath around nerves.

Causes and risk groups

B12 is found primarily in animal-derived foods. Risk factors for deficiency include strict vegetarian or vegan diets, conditions that impair absorption (e.g., pernicious anemia, atrophic gastritis, Crohn’s disease, celiac disease), certain medications (proton pump inhibitors, metformin), and older age with reduced gastric acid production. Typical early clinical features

- Fatigue and low energy not fully relieved by rest
- Mild cognitive changes: forgetfulness, slowed thinking, difficulty concentrating
- Sensory symptoms: paresthesia (pins and needles), numbness, or subtle balance issues
- Pale or slightly yellow-tinged skin and mild breathlessness with exertion (early anemia)
- Mood changes such as increased irritability or low mood

Laboratory testing and interpretation

Screening usually starts with a serum B12 concentration. More sensitive markers include methylmalonic acid (MMA) and elevated homocysteine, which can indicate intracellular deficiency even when serum B12 appears borderline. A complete blood count (CBC) can reveal macrocytosis or megaloblastic anemia characteristic of advanced deficiency. Regular monitoring is recommended for at-risk populations; clinical context should guide interpretation.

Management principles

When deficiency is confirmed or strongly suspected, replacement therapy using oral, sublingual, or intramuscular routes can restore levels and reverse many hematologic and some neurologic symptoms if started early. Choice of route depends on severity and absorption capacity. Dosage and duration should be individualized and supervised by a clinician.

Prevention and lifestyle

Dietary diversification, fortified foods, and targeted supplementation can prevent deficiency in high-risk groups. Attention to gastrointestinal health and review of medications that impair absorption are practical measures.
