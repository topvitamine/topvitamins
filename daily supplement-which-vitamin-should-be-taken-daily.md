Introduction

In modern diets and lifestyles, consistent micronutrient intake can be difficult. While whole foods remain the primary source of vitamins and minerals, targeted daily supplementation can help fill gaps for many people. This article reviews key vitamins and one important mineral often considered for daily use and summarizes their roles, sources, deficiency signals, and safe intake considerations. Vitamin C

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble antioxidant involved in collagen synthesis, immune cell function, and iron absorption. Because it is not stored long-term, regular intake from fruits and vegetables (e.g., citrus, berries, peppers) is important. Recommended daily amounts are approximately 75 mg for women and 90 mg for men; higher short-term doses are sometimes used for immune support. Deficiency can present as fatigue, gum inflammation, and impaired wound healing.

Vitamin D

Vitamin D supports calcium and phosphate balance, bone health, muscle function, and immune modulation. It is synthesized in the skin via UVB exposure but dietary sources (fatty fish, fortified foods) are limited. Typical recommendations range 600–800 IU/day for most adults, with higher intakes for older adults or those with limited sun exposure. Screening and individualized dosing are advisable for suspected deficiency.

Vitamin B12

B12 (cobalamin) is essential for red blood cell production, DNA synthesis, and nervous system integrity. Absorption requires intrinsic factor, so older adults, people with certain gastrointestinal conditions, and those on strict plant-based diets may need supplements. Early deficiency signs include fatigue, cognitive changes, and peripheral neuropathy. Standard adult RDA is about 2.4 mcg, though therapeutic doses can be higher when deficiency is present.

Vitamin A

Vitamin A supports vision, epithelial health, and immune function. It occurs as preformed retinol in animal foods and as provitamin A carotenoids in plants (e.g., carrots, sweet potatoes). Adult needs are roughly 700–900 mcg RAE; because of toxicity risk with high retinol intake, supplementation should follow clinical guidance, especially in pregnancy.

Vitamin E

Vitamin E is a fat-soluble antioxidant that protects cell membranes from oxidative damage and contributes to skin and ocular health. Typical adult intake recommendations are around 15 mg/day (alpha-tocopherol). Dietary sources include nuts, seeds, and vegetable oils. High supplemental doses may affect blood clotting and should be used cautiously.

Zinc (trace mineral)

Zinc is not a vitamin but is a trace mineral critical to immune function, wound healing, and enzymatic reactions. Men generally need about 11 mg/day and women about 8 mg/day, with an upper intake around 40 mg/day. Vegetarians, pregnant or breastfeeding people, and those with malabsorption risk may need supplemental zinc.

Conclusion

No single nutrient is universally required for every person every day, but Vitamins C, D, B12, A, E, and zinc are commonly considered when assessing daily supplementation needs. Decisions should be based on diet, life stage, medical conditions, laboratory testing where appropriate, and clinical guidance.
