Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is essential for collagen synthesis, immune support, antioxidant defense, and non-heme iron absorption. While citrus fruits are often the first dietary sources that come to mind, many common fruits—including bananas—contribute some vitamin C to the diet. This article summarizes the evidence about vitamin C in bananas, compares it with other fruits, and highlights practical considerations for meeting daily needs.

Quick answer

- Bananas contain vitamin C, but in modest amounts: a medium banana (~118 g) provides about 8.7 mg (roughly 10% of the typical adult recommended daily intake of 75–90 mg).

Vitamin C in bananas: how much and why it matters

A medium banana supplies approximately 8.7 mg of vitamin C (about 8.7 mg per 100 g). This amount means bananas contribute to total daily vitamin C intake but are not a concentrated source. Factors such as ripeness, storage, and light or air exposure after peeling can slightly affect vitamin C levels, but variations are relatively small compared with differences between fruit types.

By contrast, several other fruits are substantially richer in vitamin C: kiwi (~92.7 mg/100 g), strawberries (~58.8 mg/100 g), oranges (~53.2 mg/100 g), papaya (~60.9 mg/100 g), and guava (over ~200 mg/100 g). Some tropical fruits and extracts—such as acerola—can be orders of magnitude higher in ascorbic acid.

Nutritional context: what bananas do well

Bananas are valuable for nutrients other than vitamin C. A medium banana is a noted source of potassium (~422 mg), provides dietary fiber (pectin and resistant starch), and supplies vitamin B6 and modest amounts of magnesium. These attributes support cardiovascular health, digestive function, and energy metabolism. Because of this broader nutrient profile, bananas remain a useful component of a balanced diet, especially when paired with vitamin-C–rich foods.

Comparing dietary strategies

If the goal is to prioritize vitamin C intake, choose fruits and vegetables high in ascorbic acid (citrus, berries, kiwi, papaya, bell peppers, and certain tropical fruits). For people relying on diet alone, including a variety of these items daily helps reach recommended levels. When access to fresh produce is limited—or when higher therapeutic intakes are needed—reliable alternatives such as standardized vitamin C formulations may be considered.

Takeaway

Bananas do provide vitamin C but at low concentrations relative to many other fruits. They are better valued for potassium, fiber, and vitamin B6. To meet vitamin C requirements conveniently, combine bananas with higher-ascorbic-acid fruits or vegetables and consider supplementation only when dietary intake or clinical circumstances warrant it.
