## Understanding what vitamin B12 is used for

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is an essential water-soluble vitamin required for several fundamental physiological processes. Its primary uses in the body include supporting red blood cell formation, enabling DNA synthesis, maintaining nervous system integrity through myelin production, and participating in energy metabolism via enzymatic reactions that help generate cellular ATP. Because humans cannot synthesize B12, dietary intake or supplementation is necessary to maintain adequate levels.

Clinical and population studies consistently show that B12 is particularly important for: hematologic health (preventing megaloblastic anemia), neurologic function (reducing risk of neuropathy and cognitive decline), and metabolic balance (lowering homocysteine, a cardiovascular risk marker). Individuals with limited intake of animal products, older adults with reduced gastric acid, and people with certain gastrointestinal disorders commonly benefit from targeted assessment and, where indicated, supplementation.

## Common uses and supplementation options

Supplemental B12 is used to correct deficiency, support pregnancy and fetal neural development, and manage symptoms attributable to low B12 such as fatigue, paresthesia, and memory complaints. Forms of supplementation include oral tablets (cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin), sublingual preparations, nasal sprays, and intramuscular injections when absorption is severely impaired. Each route has advantages: oral and sublingual products are convenient for maintenance, while injections rapidly restore stores in people with absorption disorders.

## Who should consider B12 and when injections are indicated

At-risk groups include vegans and vegetarians, older adults, and patients with conditions such as pernicious anemia, celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, or prior gastric surgery. Long-term use of medications like metformin or proton pump inhibitors can also impair B12 status. In these contexts, clinicians often recommend screening (serum B12, methylmalonic acid, homocysteine) and may initiate oral supplementation or intramuscular injections depending on severity and absorption capacity.

Injections are commonly used when there is documented severe deficiency, neurologic symptoms, or proven inability to absorb oral B12. They offer direct delivery into the bloodstream and typically produce more rapid symptom improvement compared with standard oral dosing.

## Absorption factors and practical considerations

Absorption of dietary B12 is a multistep process requiring gastric acid to release B12 from food proteins, binding to intrinsic factor, and uptake in the terminal ileum. Age-related atrophic gastritis, intestinal disorders, certain surgeries, and some medications can disrupt these steps. For people with impaired absorption, sublingual formulations, high-dose oral therapy, or injections can be effective alternatives.

Supplement choice may also consider active forms (methylcobalamin, adenosylcobalamin) versus cyanocobalamin and the presence of co-nutrients that support nerve and metabolic health.
