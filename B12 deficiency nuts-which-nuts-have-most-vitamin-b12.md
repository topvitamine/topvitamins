Quick Answer Summary

- Most commonly consumed nuts do not contain significant or bioavailable vitamin B12.
- Vitamin B12 is primarily in animal-derived foods; fortified plant products are the main reliable plant-based sources.
- Some commercially fortified nut milks and nut-based snacks may contain added B12 and can help vegans and vegetarians meet needs.

Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for DNA synthesis, red blood cell production, and nervous system function. Naturally occurring B12 is produced by certain bacteria and is concentrated in animal tissues, so strictly plant-based diets risk inadequate intake. Given their nutrient density and popularity, nuts are often evaluated as possible B12 sources. The short answer: standard nuts—almonds, walnuts, cashews, Brazil nuts, pistachios—do not provide meaningful amounts of vitamin B12.

Why nuts lack B12

Laboratory and nutrient databases report negligible or absent B12 in typical edible nuts. B12 synthesis requires specific bacteria commonly associated with animal microbiomes or specialized fermentation; soil or plant-surface contamination does not reliably supply human-bioavailable B12. Even when trace amounts are detected, they are usually nutritionally insignificant or potentially inactive analogs that do not support human requirements.

Fortified nut products and supplements

While natural nuts are poor B12 sources, industrial fortification can make nut-based foods useful carriers of B12. Many plant milks and some nut-based spreads or bars are fortified with synthetic forms such as cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin. Consumers should read labels to confirm the presence and form of added B12. Practical recommendations for plant-based eaters

- Rely on fortified foods (including some nut milks) and/or supplements to meet the recommended daily intake (about 2.4 µg for most adults).
- Prefer products that list the B12 form (cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin) and provide clear microgram amounts per serving.
- If using nut milks, verify that the specific brand and batch are fortified; homemade versions generally are not.

Nuts as complementary foods

Although nuts do not supply B12, they remain valuable in plant-based diets for healthy fats, protein, fiber, and micronutrients (e.g., vitamin E, selenium, magnesium). Their inclusion supports overall nutrition and can be paired with fortified B12 sources or supplements to maintain balanced intake.

Conclusion

Natural nuts are not reliable sources of vitamin B12. Fortified nut-based products can contribute to B12 intake when labeled with a bioavailable form, but supplementation or consistent consumption of fortified foods is the evidence-based approach for vegans and vegetarians.
